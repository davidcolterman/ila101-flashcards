A.1.1 — Ch. 1: Term Insurance  (A.1 Life Insurance Products)
----------------------------------------------------------------------

[1] Q: List coverage periods and face amount patterns for term.
A: Coverage: 10–30 years or to age (e.g., 65)
   Face Amount Patterns:
   • Level (very common) – constant DB
   • Decreasing – tied to declining balance (e.g., mortgage)
   • Increasing – CPI-linked for inflation

[2] Q: List the 4 premium patterns for level term.
A: 1. Level for entire period (very common)
   2. Modified – 1–2 increases over term
   3. Increasing every n years – keeps initial premium/commission lower
   4. Level then ART

[3] Q: Compare the 3 term premium schedules.
A: 1. Attained age:
     ◦ Everyone age x pays same premium
     ◦ Simplest to administer
     ◦ Con: May not be competitive at all ages
   2. Select scale:
     ◦ Based on issue age + duration
     ◦ Reflects UW effect
   3. Select & Ultimate (S&U):
     ◦ Grades from select →attained age
     ◦ Assumes UW effects wear off
     ◦ Pro: More competitive
     ◦ Con: Future mortality ↑risk

[4] Q: How to handle rising relative premiums in decreasing term?
A: • Limited-pay premiums (may require CSV)
   • Decreasing premium scales (less competitive)
   • Floor DB (imperfect solution)
   • OYT premiums (erratic, admin challenges)

[5] Q: Compare Par vs. Indeterminate term.
A: 1. Par term:
     ◦ Higher premium →funds dividends
     ◦ Pro: Higher persistency, cushion vs. adverse experience
     ◦ Con: Expensive, not popular
   2. Indeterminate (non-guaranteed):
     ◦ Insurer adjusts premiums prospectively
     ◦ Pro: Aggressive pricing
     ◦ Con: Anti-selection

[6] Q: List other special term types.
A: • Joint and second-to-die plans
   • Hybrid term (blend of term + WL)
   • Deposit term (pays endowment)

[7] Q: How does policy size affect premiums and lapses?
A: Premiums:
   • Per $1,000 ↓ as face ↑
   • Reflects decreasing average costs
   • Discounts larger than WL
   • Highest bands → reinsured
   Lapses:
   • ↑ with policy size
   • Different pattern than WL

[8] Q: Describe risk class and design-related premium differences.
A: Risk Class:
   • Smokers > non-smokers
   • Select risks → lower premiums
   Gender: Sex-distinct or unisex rates
   Design Practices:
   • Lapse-supported – higher lapses → higher profit
   • Cross-subsidization – price some cells aggressively

[9] Q: List key term and non-term riders.
A: Term Riders:
   • Base insured, spouse, or child coverage
   • One-Year Term (OYT) riders (purchase OYT with dividends)
   Non-Term Riders:
   1. WOP – Waives premium on qualified disability
   2. ROP – Refunds premiums at term end or death
   3. ADB – Accelerates DB on terminal illness
   4. Guaranteed Insurability – Purchase more coverage without UW
   5. Disability – Monthly income (% of original face)
   6. LTC – Benefits independent of DB

[10] Q: Formula for Final DB when ADB utilized?
A: Final DB = Original DB − Accelerated DBs

[11] Q: What triggers ADB riders?
A: Chronic illness: Unable to perform 2/6 ADLs:
   • Eating
   • Bathing
   • Dressing
   • Toileting
   • Transferring
   • Continence
   Critical illness: Heart attack, stroke, cancer, coronary bypass

[12] Q: How does term mortality compare to WL?
A: Most important pricing assumption
   Aggregate term mortality < WL because:
   • Higher average policy size
   • More select period exposure
   • Conversions shift higher risks → WL

[13] Q: Summarize SOA high face Delphi study on term vs. WL mortality.
A: • Term mortality > WL (for high face amounts)
   • Male improvement > females, smokers
   • Large policy mortality < “all amounts”
   • Best experience: $1M+ band

[14] Q: List key mortality considerations for post-level period and older ages.
A: Post-level period: Anti-selection (“conservation of deaths”)
   Older ages: Credibility, large sizes, widow effect, select period wear-off

[15] Q: How do lapse rates impact mortality and profits?
A: • Anti-selection effects: Lapses affect mortality
   • Impact by timing:
     ◦ High early lapses → harder to recover acquisition costs
     ◦ High late lapses → erode profits
   • Premium structure: Steep slopes ↑ lapses and anti-selection

[16] Q: Compare first-year vs. renewal term lapses to WL.
A: • First year: Term slightly < WL
   • Renewal: Term > WL

[17] Q: Describe new trends in term underwriting.
A: Traditional: Similar to WL
   New trends:
   • Select and super-select classes ↑
   • AUW minimizes time, cost, intrusion
   • Shift to predictive analytics vs. experience only

[18] Q: What are AUW characteristics and impact per SOA Delphi?
A: • Uses additional data sources instead of fluids
   • Sources: Rx, MIB, EHR
   • Mortality ↑5–10% (effect may diminish)

[19] Q: How do compensation and expenses affect term pricing vs. WL?
A: Compensation:
   • Term < WL (lower premiums)
   • Commissions sometimes “recycled” on renewal
   • Agents may encourage lapses↑compensation
   Expenses:
   • Large % of premium (especially early ART)
   • Term profits very sensitive to overhead allocation

[20] Q: Compare inflation risk: term vs. WL.
A: High inflation is greater risk for term than WL
   Reason: WL has more assets backing larger reserves →cushion

[21] Q: List key regulatory aspects impacting term.
A: • Statutory reserves – major profit impact
   • VM-20 (PBR) replacing overly conservative methods (XXX)
   • State regulation:
     ◦ Coverage length (e.g., NY no coverage > age 70)
     ◦ Advertising, disclosure, marketing
     ◦ Actuarial certifications
     ◦ Contestibility period (typically 2 years)

[22] Q: List common term profit objectives with formulas.
A: • Profit Margin (PM):
   PM = PV(Profits) / PV(Premiums)
   • Return on Equity (ROE):
   ROE = Net Income / Equity (discounted or undiscounted)
   • IRR: Discount rate where PV(Profits) = 0
   • Surplus Strain:
   Surplus Strain = First Year Income / First Year Premium
   • BEY: Year when accumulated assets ≥ reserves

[23] Q: What is term conversion and what are the benefits?
A: • Definition: Policy converted from one type to another
   • Most Common: Term → permanent (WL) with same UW rating
   • Timing: Policyholder discretion, limited time frame, or automatic
   • Benefits:
     ◦ Increases sales (more attractive product)
     ◦ Increases persistency (keeps healthy lives)
     ◦ Permanent insurance more profitable than term

[24] Q: List methods to manage conversion costs.
A: • Terminal reserve →funds part of premium
   • Pay commission only on discounted premium (varies)

[25] Q: List 3 approaches for charging conversion options.
A: 1. Charge only exercisers →highest premium for converters
   2. Charge only those wanting option →higher premium for option-holders
   3. Charge everyone

[26] Q: List pricing assumptions for conversion options.
A: • Election rate – % of insureds electing
   • Coverage % – Amount to be exercised
   • Lapse – Low post-conversion
   • Mortality – High when election rate low (anti-selection)

[27] Q: Formula for PV of extra mortality cost from conversions (age x, year r)?
A: A_{x,r} = _{r}p_{x} · e_{x,r} · K_{x,r} · v^{r}
   where:
   K_{x,r} = \sum_{t=1}^{∞} (_{t-1}p_{x+r})(v^{t})(q_{x+r+t-1}^{(C)} − q_{x+r+t-1}^{(S)})AR_{x+r+t}
   • A_{x,r}: PV at age x of extra mortality cost (policy converted at year r)
   • r: Duration since issue
   • t: Duration since conversion
   • e_{x,r}: Probability converts in duration r
   • q^{(C)}: Mortality for converted WL
   • q^{(S)}: Mortality for standard WL
   • AR: Amount at risk each year

[28] Q: Formula for the cost of conversions per unit issued?
A: Cost of conversions in year t per unit issued is the sum of:
   1. The cost of expected conversions in year t
   = Reserve Credits + (Option Handling Expense / Avg Units Issued Per Policy) × (Units Electing Option / Radix)
   2. The cumulative cost for years prior to t
   = Σ (Units Electing Options / Radix × % of Face Converted × Option Charge)
   Radix = total number of units issued

A.1.2 — Ch. 2: Whole Life  (A.1 Life Insurance Products)
----------------------------------------------------------------------

[29] Q: List WL characteristics.
A: Permanent life insurance with:
   • Coverage for life (if premiums paid)
   • Tabular CSV
   • Can be participating → dividends (cash, premiums, PUAs/OYT)
   • Premium patterns:
   1. Ordinary level for life (most common)
   2. Indeterminate
   3. Limited payment

[30] Q: List WL pricing considerations.
A: Mortality (significant risk)
   • No premium flexibility + very long coverage
   Persistency (Lapse)
   • Affects mortality and expense assumptions
   • 2012 SOA study findings:
     ◦ Lapse rates declining
     ◦ Lapses ↑ in poor economy
     ◦ Smaller policies → higher early lapses
     ◦ Male ≈ female lapses
     ◦ Stricter UW → lower early lapses
   Underwriting – trending toward AUW like term

A.1.3 — Ch. 3: Universal Life  (A.1 Life Insurance Products)
----------------------------------------------------------------------

[31] Q: List UL characteristics.
A: 1. Cash value: CV_{t} = (CV_{t-1} + Prem_{t} − Charges_{t})(1 + r)
   2. Credited rates: guaranteed min, portfolio vs. new money
     ◦ Key risk: spread compression if rates ↓
   3. Mortality charges: guaranteed max, attained age vs. S&U
   4. Surrender charges common
   5. Expenses: front-end loads, fixed charges
   6. DB options: face or face + CV
   7. Face amount changeable after issue
   8. Flexible premiums
   9. Partial withdrawals allowed
   10. Policy loans allowed (lower credited rate)
   11. Riders (similar to term)
   12. Persistency bonuses

[32] Q: Describe fixed premium UL (FPUL).
A: • A.k.a. “interest-sensitive WL ”
   • Fixed premium like permanent products
   • CV = max[Guaranteed CV, Net Accumulation Value]
   • Vanishing premium design most popular

[33] Q: Describe ULSG:
1. Main designs
2. Reserve implications
A: 1. Minimum no-lapse premium (MNLP) – most basic
   • SG based on cumulative minimum premiums paid
   • Generates little/no CSV
   2. Shadow account – in force if shadow account > 0
   • Has own charges/credits (SA ≠ CSV!)
   Reserve Implications: XXX (Reg 830) & AXXX (AG 38)
   • AXXX greatly ↑ ULSG reserves
   • Insurers needed relief → AG 48
   • VM-20 replacing XXX/AXXX → simpler designs

[34] Q: How is interest credited in indexed UL?
A: Index Crediting Rate = max[min(Index Change × Participation Rate, Cap), Floor]
   Index change methods:
   1. Point-to-point – index change between two points
   Index Change = (Index at End of Segment / Index at Beginning of Segment) − 1
   2. Averaging – average monthly index level
   Index Change = \frac{(1/12) × \sum_{m=1}^{12} Index_{m}}{BOY Index} − 1

[35] Q: List FPUL pros and cons.
A: • Pros:
   1. Similar to WL
   2. Higher commission
   3. Better persistency
   • Cons:
   1. Lacks flexibility
   2. Admin complexity
   3. Vanishing premiums don’t always vanish

[36] Q: List UL pricing considerations.
A: • Flexibility creates challenges (changing DBs, partial withdrawals, etc)
   • Scenario testing important
   • UL profit sources:
   1. Interest Earned – Interest Credited
   2. Cost of Insurance Charges – DBs Paid
   3. Expenses: Expense Charges + Surrender Charges – (Expenses + Commissions)
   • Asset/liability analysis (interest rate risk)

[38] Q: How does fixed premium variable life differ from WL?

[39] Q: Describe the Equitable design.

A.1.5 — Ch. 5: Survivorship Insurance  (A.1 Life Insurance Products)
----------------------------------------------------------------------

[40] Q: List survivorship insurance characteristics.
A: 1. Joint and survivor – pays on last death
   2. Wealth preservation
   3. High face amounts
   4. Par WL common – dividends buy PUA/term

[41] Q: List survivorship competitive measures.
A: 1. Rate of return on death at specified duration
   2. Minimum premium to fund benefits
   3. Minimum premium to vanish in specified years
   4. Minimum CSV to vanish premiums
   5. Policyholders value DBs > CSVs

[42] Q: List survivorship insurance riders.
A: • PUAs and term riders (very common)
   • Policy split rider
     ◦ Allows future policy split
     ◦ Few sold, very valuable to some
   • Estate preservation rider
     ◦ Increases DB for estate taxes
   • First-to-die term rider (estate planning)
     ◦ Pays benefit when first dies
     ◦ Uses: Pay-up policy, pay estate taxes, recover premiums

[43] Q: Compare single vs. dual status.
A: • Single Status
     ◦ Blend of “one-alive and two-alive” status
     ◦ Smoother CSVs and reserves
   • Dual Status
     ◦ 3 possibilities: Both alive, only X alive, only Y alive
     ◦ CSVs and reserves jump after first death

[44] Q: List factors affecting single vs. dual status decision.
A: • Perceived marketability
   • Administrative feasibility
   • Regulators’ attitudes
   • Perceived risk profile
   • Implications of increased dual-status term rider costs

[45] Q: List 3 dual-life status calculation methods.
A: 1. Exact Age
     ◦ First principles using exact age mortality
     ◦ Cumbersome: calculation, storage, validation
   2. Joint Equal Age
     ◦ Example: (55, 52) ≈ (53, 53)
   3. Equivalent Single Age
     ◦ Equates 2 ages to single age
     ◦ Overcharges early → undercharges later

[46] Q: List 3 substandard rating methods.
A: 1. Age Rateup
     ◦ Assigns higher age to substandard insured
   2. Extra Premium
     ◦ Increase premium for substandards
   3. COI Multiple
     ◦ Increase UL COI by a multiple

[47] Q: List requirements for uninsurable lives.
A: 1. Not terminally ill
   2. Standalone underwriting required
   3. Life expectancy ≥ 1-2 years
   4. No increase in contagion factors
   5. Not highly rated (Table D max)

[48] Q: List survivorship insurance pricing considerations.
A: • Mortality
     ◦ Single life mortality of joint policyholders ≠ single life market
     ◦ UW – concessions, medical
     ◦ Contagion risk
     ◦ Socio-economic class
     ◦ Impact of very low lapses
   • Persistency
     ◦ Very low lapse rates common
   • Expenses
     ◦ Usually higher than single life business
   • Reinsurance
     ◦ Very important in pricing
     ◦ Retention rates may be higher than other policies

A.1.6 — Ch. 6: Extra Premiums for Substandard Lives  (A.1 Life Insurance Products)
----------------------------------------------------------------------

[49] Q: List methods for determining extra mortality.
A: • Premium increase approaches:
   1. Flat extra: extra amount per 1000
   2. Table rating: multiple of standard mortality
   • Determining extra mortality:
   1. Numerical rating (sum of credits/debits) – arbitrary, may not properly evaluate risk
   2. Advance in age
   3. Lien method (increasing DB)
   4. Return of premiums
   5. Extra premiums + different nonforfeiture values
   • UL methods:
     ◦ Higher premium
     ◦ Reduced coverage

[50] Q: How to subdivide substandard business?
A: 1. Male/female (e.g. setback)
   2. Smoker/non-smoker (e.g. “forgive” classes for non-smoker)
   3. By plan
     ◦ If Substandard/Standard ratio high → higher lapse, NT rates
     ◦ Steeper slope → higher sales

[51] Q: List substandard expense considerations.
A: • Allocation approaches:
   1. Across all business
   2. Only to substandard
   3. Combination of 1 & 2 (most common)
   • Premium taxes and commissions: Allocate directly to substandard
   • Acquisition expenses: Higher than standard
   • Maintenance expenses: Similar to standard

[52] Q: List substandard pricing considerations:
• Not-taken and lapse rates
• Premium paying period
• Ratings expiration
• CSV level vs. standard
• Profitability assessment
A: • Not-taken and lapse rates higher than standard
   • Limit premium paying period
   • Ratings may be removed later
   • CSVs slightly higher than standard
   • Asset share tests fit premiums to profit objectives

[53] Q: Formula for extra substandard gross premium?
A: 1. Calculate standard GP:
   GP = NP(1 + ClaimCost%) + AcqExp/ä_{x:n} + OtherMaintExp + GP(% Prem Exp)
   2. Calculate substandard GPR using same formula
     ◦ Use substandard (rated) mortality and expense assumptions
   3. Extra substandard GP:
   GPR − GP

A.1.7 — Life Insurance Acceleration Riders  (A.1 Life Insurance Products)
----------------------------------------------------------------------

[54] Q: How do acceleration riders work and what triggers them?
A: Insurer pays portion of face before death (“living benefits”)
   • Policyholder must meet trigger criteria
   Trigger Events:
   Terminal Illness → Life expectancy < 12-24 months
   Chronic Illness → Unable to perform 2+ ADLs (e.g. bathing)
   Critical Illness → Meet critical illness criteria (e.g. heart attack)

[55] Q: List 3 chronic illness benefit designs.
A: 1. Actuarial discounting
     ◦ Acceleration Benefit = PV of accelerated portion of face
   2. Lien method
     ◦ Lien = Acceleration benefit paid
     ◦ Policyholder pays premium for full face
     ◦ DB = Face – Lien
   3. Chronic Illness Rider
     ◦ Explicit additional premium
   Tax treatment: Designed for favorable tax treatment (keep benefits tax-free)

[56] Q: List chronic illness acceleration risk controls.
A: 1. Supplemental UW (screen for ADL loss, over-insureds)
   2. Limit issue ages
   3. Use lien or actuarial discounting
   4. Limit acceleration amount (annual and max)
   5. Require ADL certification (licensed health care practitioner)
   6. Exclude temporary ADL losses
   7. Explicit exclusions in trigger criteria
   8. Limit to max table rating
   9. Contestability follows base policy
   10. Max benefit < 100% of base DB

[57] Q: Describe reinsurance participation for acceleration riders.
A: Terminal Illness: Proportional to base policy participation
   Chronic Illness:
   Permanent products
     ◦ High probability of ultimate claim
     ◦ If chronically ill, lapse rate ≈ 0%
     ◦ Reinsurer participation: Full, limited
   to one-time payment, or none
   Term products
     ◦ Much more uncertainty and risk
     ◦ Fewer ultimate death claims
     ◦ Life expectancy may be > term
     ◦ Term conversions: additional risk

A.2.1 — Ch. 1: Fixed Deferred Annuities  (A.2 Annuity Products)
----------------------------------------------------------------------

[58] Q: List deferred annuity product design features:
• Tax treatment
• Premium structure
• Charges
• Interest guarantees
A: • Tax treatment: Qualified or non-qualified
   • SPDAs: May have minimum required premiums (e.g. $5,000)
   • Charges: FELs, periodic fees, SCs
   • FPDAs: May have SCs based on premiums paid
   • Interest rates and guarantee periods:
     ◦ SPDAs: guarantee rate for 1–7 years
     ◦ FPDAs: lower minimum guaranteed rates

[59] Q: List 4 deferred annuity types.
A: 1. CD Annuities
     ◦ SCs work like CDs
     ◦ Penalty-free withdrawals 30–60 days after guarantee period (high lapses)
     ◦ Low commissions
   2. Market Value Adjusted (MVA) Annuities
     ◦ Protects company from interest rate risk
     ◦ AV ↓ as rates ↑
     ◦ AV ↑ as rates ↓
   3. Two-Tiered Annuities
     ◦ 2 account values
     ◦ Annuitization account rate > surrender account rate
   4. Non-Surrenderable Annuities (“Personal GICS”)

[60] Q: Formula for market value adjustments (MVA)?
A: MVA_{t} = \frac{1 + a}{1 + b + c}^{n-t}
   n = length of current guarantee period (years)
   t = years since guarantee period start
   a = current guaranteed rate
   b = current rate offered on similar product
   c = constant factor (0.000 to 0.005)
   If SC applies:
   Final CSV_{t} = AV_{t} × MVA_{t} × (1 − SC%_{t})

[61] Q: List deferred annuity features.
A: 1. Bailout provisions
   2. Penalty-free withdrawal provision
     ◦ Waives SC on portion of AV
     ◦ May be constant % or % of premiums paid
     ◦ May be tied to persistency
   3. Return of principal guarantee provisions
   4. Death benefits (usually DB = AV)
   5. Waiver of SC on annuitization
   6. Guaranteed settlement rates
   7. AV enhancement bonuses: Annuitization, persistency, large AV, higher credited
   rate first year

[62] Q: Describe bailout provisions.
A: • No SCs if Credited Rate < Bailout Rate
   • Medical bailouts (popular with age 50+ market)
   • Total cost = option cost + additional surplus
   • Option Cost = Avg Lost SC × Excess Lapse Rate × Probability of Trigger
   • Higher reserves and capital

[63] Q: List interest rate considerations.
A: • Interest Rate Risk (a.k.a. C-3 risk, disintermediation)
   • Interest Spread = Investment Earned Rate − Credited Rate
   • Target Spread Components:
   1. Expenses (e.g. maintenance, commissions)
   2. Product features (e.g. bailouts)
   3. Risk charges (e.g. disintermediation)
   4. Expected profit
   • Crediting Strategies:
   1. Ignore competition (fixed spread)
   2. Use competitor’s rate as cap or floor
   3. Weighted average of #1 and competitor’s rate

[64] Q: List deferred annuity pricing assumptions.
A: 1. Withdrawal (really important)
     ◦ Affected by SCs, credited rates, distribution system, guarantees, policyholder
   characteristics
   2. Partial Withdrawal Provisions
   3. Mortality (less important)
   4. Commissions and Marketing Expenses
     ◦ Percent of premium
     ◦ Affected by competitive pressures
     ◦ Examples: SPDA 3–10%, FPDA 7% FY then 3%
   5. Expenses (lower than life insurance)

[65] Q: List profit and pricing considerations for deferred annuities.
A: • First-year strain sources: commissions, reserves, required capital
   • Pricing should consider multiple interest rate scenarios
   • Profit objectives: profit margin, IRR, break-even year, GAAP ROE
     ◦ Make decisions considering all 3 together
     ◦ Low IRR + high profit margin = surplus strain
   • Pricing horizon usually 10–20 years

A.2.2 — Ch. 2: Variable Annuities  (A.2 Annuity Products)
----------------------------------------------------------------------

[66] Q: List variable annuity characteristics.
A: • Nearly all are SPDAs
   • Passes C-3 risk to policyholder
   • No guaranteed minimum CSV on separate account funds
   • Basic VA DB = full AV (waive SCs)
   • Product charges:
     ◦ Similar to fixed annuities: FELs, SCs, periodic fees
     ◦ % of asset charges: mortality, expense, profit, guarantee riders
   • GMDBs and GLBs add substantial risk

[67] Q: List VA guarantees.
A: 1. GMDBs (Guaranteed minimum death benefits) = max(AV, GMDB) on death
     ◦ Step-up: GMDB = highest AV on any past anniversary − Σ Withdrawals since
     ◦ Roll-up: GMDB_{t} = GMDB_{t-1} × (1 + r) + Premiums_{t} − Withdrawals_{t}
   2. GLBs (Guaranteed living benefits) – must be alive to exercise
     ◦ GMIB: Guaranteed minimum income benefit
     ◦ GMAB: Guaranteed minimum accumulation benefits
     ◦ GMWB: Guaranteed minimum withdrawal benefits
     ◦ GLWB: GMWB for life (most popular)

[68] Q: Formulas for VA unit fund values?
A: Fund Value = Units × Unit Value
   Units_{t} = Units_{t-1} + Units Purchased_{t} − Units Withdrawn_{t}
   Unit Value_{t} = Unit Value_{t-1} × NIF_{t}
   1. Open-Ended Separate Account:
   NIF_{t} = 1 + \frac{InvInc_{t} + UCG_{t} + RCG_{t-1} − Exp_{t}}{AV_{t-1} + Annuity Reserves_{t-1}} − Daily Asset Charges
   2. Unit Investment Trusts:
   NIF_{t} = 1 + \frac{Ending Value of Shares – Beginning Value of Shares}{AV_{t-1} + Annuity Reserves_{t-1}}

[69] Q: Formula for VA annuitization into monthly payment?
A: Pmt_{0} = \frac{Account Value}{12 ä_{x}^{(12)}}
   Pmt_{t} = \frac{Pmt_{t-1} × NIF_{t}}{(1 + AIR)^{1/12}}

[70] Q: How to reduce variable payout variability?
A: 1. Convert each annual payment to 1-year fixed annuity
   2. Floor payment at 75% of initial payment
   3. Floor payment at 100% and track actual payment separately
     ◦ If actual payment < floor, insurer “loans” shortfall
     ◦ “Loans” repaid when actual payments> floor
     ◦ Outstanding loan usually forgiven on death

[71] Q: List VA pricing considerations (no guarantees).
A: 1. Lapse rates (affect asset charge income)
     ◦ Dynamic lapse assumptions critical
   2. Premium persistency (difficult to estimate)
   3. Average size (higher premium → higher profit)
   4. Expenses (higher than fixed annuities)
   5. Commissions (lower than life insurance)
   6. Acquisition costs (recovery subject to equity risk)

[72] Q: List VA guarantee pricing considerations.
A: • GMDBs and GLBs require stochastic pricing
   • Company must hold reserves for GMDBs and GLBs
   • Pricing considerations to fund guarantee costs:
     ◦ Future fund allocations assumption
     ◦ Mean and variance of total returns
     ◦ Mortality for GMDBs and GLWBs
     ◦ GLB utilization (% electing and benefit amount elected)

A.2.3 — Ch. 3: Indexed Annuities  (A.2 Annuity Products)
----------------------------------------------------------------------

[73] Q: List FIA characteristics.
A: FIA = fixed deferred annuity with GMAV and index credit potential
   • GMAV ≥ minimum SNL value
   Indexed Account Value (IAV):
   • Credited interest based on index growth (e.g. S&P 500) floored at zero
   • Index credits hedged with call options (or equivalents)

[74] Q: How is interest credited under point-to-point design?
A: Point-to-Point (PTP) – based on index growth over period
   IndexGrowth_{t} = Index_{t} / Index_{t-1} − 1
   Adjustments (most common to least):
   IndexCredit_{t} =
     min(IndexGrowth_{t}, Cap)   with cap
     PartRate × IndexGrowth_{t}   with participation rate
     IndexGrowth_{t} − Fee%   with % of IAV fee
   Zero floor common: final index credit cannot be negative

[75] Q: How is interest credited under monthly sum cap design?
A: Average Monthly Sum – sum of monthly index growth during year
   IndexGrowth_{t} = \sum_{i=1}^{12} \frac{Index_{ti} − Index_{ti-1}}{Index_{ti-1}}
   Monthly Sum Cap (common):
   IndexGrowth_{t} = \sum_{i=1}^{12} min(MonthlyCap, \frac{Index_{ti} − Index_{ti-1}}{Index_{ti-1}})
   MSC typically not floored:
   • Down months drag down return (unlike PTP)

[76] Q: List innovative or exotic FIA return methods.
A: • Binary Returns – e.g. index credit = 5% if index gains; else 0%
   • High Water – based on highest index level
   IndexGrowth = Max Index Level / Initial Index Level
   • Index Choice – policyholder can choose their index (common)
   • Fixed interest account option
   • Fund transfers on anniversaries

[77] Q: How to price FIA from spread perspective? List risks.
A: Spread = Net Earned Rate − Pricing Spread − Hedging Budget
   • Pricing spread covers anticipated expenses and profit
   • Hedging budget = option cost as % of fund value
   • Adjust caps, participation, etc. to ensure option cost ≤ hedging budget
   Profitability risks:
   • Falling reinvestment rates
   • High index credits → ↑ IAVs → ↑ option costs
   • Higher future option costs caused by:
     ◦ ↑ equity volatility
     ◦ ↑ risk-free rates
     ◦ Other factors

[78] Q: Describe FIA static hedging.
A: Static Hedging – uses OTC call spread options
   • Call spread price = Call @ Current Index − Call @ Cap
   Payoff_{t} = max(0, Index_{t} − Lower Strike) − max(0, Index_{t} − Upper Strike)
   • Funding ratio = % of IAV hedged by options
     ◦ Often < 100% due to expected lapses
     ◦ If actual lapses > expected → insurer gains
     ◦ If actual lapses < expected → insurer loses profit

[79] Q: Describe FIA dynamic hedging.
A: Dynamic (Delta) Hedging – hold portfolio that replicates call spread option
   • Delta = expected change in option value per unit change in index
   • Main goal: hold Delta × notional amount of index
   • Must be rebalanced regularly since Delta changes
   Disadvantages:
   • Involves “buying high and selling low”
   • No downside protection like static hedging provides

[80] Q: How is GMAV funded for FIA? List key risks.
A: • Funded with fixed interest bonds
   • Key risks:
     ◦ Bonds could default or lose value if rates ↑
     ◦ Insurer may have to sell bonds early at loss to fund surrenders

[81] Q: Describe FIA reserving considerations.
A: FIA statutory reserving based on AG 33 and AG 35
   • AG 33 – prescribes CARVM for all fixed deferred annuities
     ◦ Reserve = largest projected future CSV on PV basis
   • AG 35 – prescribes 4 AG 33-consistent methods specifically for FIAs
     ◦ Type 1 method – HaR criteria must be met
     ◦ Type 2 methods – HaR criteria do not have to be met
     ◦ More complex and volatile than Type 1

[82] Q: List 5 income annuity types.
A: 1. Payment Certain (a_{n})
   2. Specialty Markets
     ◦ Structured settlements, lottery winnings, gift annuities, reverse mortgages
   3. Life Contingent
     ◦ Life only (a_{x})
     ◦ Life with n years certain (a_{n} + _{n|}a_{x} = a_{n} + _{n}p_{x} v^{n} a_{x+n})
     ◦ Unit refund (Refund = CSV – Total Payments Received)
   4. Joint and Survivor (a_{xy})

     ◦ Payment may change after first death
   5. Variable Payment – adjusts payment for investment performance

[83] Q: List income annuity pricing considerations.
A: 1. Interest Rates (discount at spot rates)
   2. Mortality (lower → higher PVFB)
     ◦ Assume mortality improvement – e.g. 1% per year: qx+t × (1 − 0.01)t
     ◦ Substandard methods: Rated age (common), constant multiple, constant extra
   deaths
   3. Expenses: premium taxes, maintenance expenses, commissions
   4. Regulatory costs: taxes, cost of capital
   5. Surplus strain impact on profitability
   6. Cash flow pattern varies greatly by product type

A.2.5 — Registered Index-Linked Annuities  (A.2 Annuity Products)
----------------------------------------------------------------------

[84] Q: Describe RILAs and compare with FIAs and VAs.
A: • RILAs have characteristics of FIAs and VAs
     ◦ Like FIA: Index credits hedged with options; subject to SNL
     ◦ Like VA: AV can go down (maybe a lot)
     ◦ RILA assets split between GA and SA
   • Higher AV growth potential than FIAs (higher caps)
   • Less downside protection than FIAs
     ◦ Floor design: FIA with floor below 0%
     ◦ Buffer design: insurer absorbs losses up to buffer
   • Crediting terms: 1 to 6 years
   • ROP riders common; GLWBs ↑ in popularity

[85] Q: Comparison of Deferred Annuities
A:
 | FA | FIA | RILA | VA
-+----+-----+------+---
General vs. Separate Account | GA | GA | Both | SA
Credited Interest | Declared | Indexed | Indexed | Market Return
Market losses possible | No | No | Yes | Yes
Contract values change daily | No | No | Yes | Yes
Subject to SNL | Yes | Yes | Yes | No
SC vs. MVA | Both | Both | Both | SC
[Low Risk → High Risk]

[86] Q: List the 2 most common RILA crediting structures.
A: 1. Cap with floor: Contractholder bears losses up to floor, then insurer bears all
   losses
   Credit = max[Floor, min[Cap, PartRate × IndexReturn]]
   2. Cap with buffer: Insurer incurs losses up to buffer, then contractholder bears all
   losses
   Credit =
     min[Cap, min[0, PartRate × IndexReturn + Buffer]]   if IndexReturn < 0
     min[Cap, PartRate × IndexReturn]   otherwise

[87] Q: Describe RILA rate setting and risk drivers.
A: • Rate setting process:
     ◦ Caps and buffers declared 1–2x/month
     ◦ Example: 15% cap, 10% buffer, 1-year term on Nasdaq
   • Key rate drivers: fixed income yields and option costs
     ◦ Fixed income yields ↑ → option budgets ↑
     ◦ Option costs vary with σ, risk-free rates, dividend yields, other factors
   • Rate sheet variable relationships:
     ◦ Caps ↑ as term ↑ (e.g. 1 or 6-year)
     ◦ Buffer design: caps ↓ as buffer ↑
     ◦ Buffer designs offer higher caps than floor designs
     ◦ Caps vary by index (e.g. S&P 500, Nasdaq)

[88] Q: How is FIA option budget determined?
A: FIA premiums invested in bonds of average credit rating (AA to BBB)
   Insurer purchases call spread to hedge index credits
   Option Budget = Earned Rate − Credit Default Charge − Expenses − Profit Margin
   • Credit default charge = default losses on invested assets
   • Expenses: commissions, acquisition, maintenance
   • Profit margin: based on insurer’s requirements and pricing guidelines
     ◦ Aka “pricing spread” or “target spread”

[89] Q: How to calculate RILA hedging costs for buffer and floor designs?
A: Typical approach: purchase derivatives that replicate contract payoff
   Buffer Design Option Cost = (ATM Call − OTM Call) − OTM Put
     [Call Spread = ATM Call − OTM Call]
   • Buy ATM call @ current index
   • Sell OTM call @ cap
   • Sell OTM put @ buffer
   Floor Design Option Cost = (ATM Call − OTM Call) + (OTM Put − ATM Put)
     [Call Spread = ATM Call − OTM Call; Put Spread = OTM Put − ATM Put]
   • Buy ATM call @ current index
   • Sell OTM call @ cap
   • Buy OTM put @ floor
   • Sell ATM put @ current index

[90] Q: How are RILA interim values determined?
A: RILA values must be calculated daily (interim values)
   • Available on surrender, death, etc.
   Two main methods:
   1. Market value approach – replicating option portfolio
     ◦ Inputs = index return, interest rates, equity σ, dividend yield
     ◦ Stronger match: minimizes balance sheet volatility and contractholder behavior risk
     ◦ Easier to explain to regulators than pro-rata
   2. Pro-rata approach – simple formula based on index return and time
     ◦ Easier to explain to advisors/contractholders than MV approach
     ◦ More balance sheet volatility and contractholder behavior risk

[91] Q: Describe RILA lock features.
A: Some RILAs allow contractholder to lock in interim values
   • Manual lock – contractholder can lock interim value on any non-anniversary day
     ◦ Locks in AV for remainder of index term
     ◦ Reduced only for withdrawals
   • Automatic lock – contractholder can provide target value to lock in
     ◦ Once interim value hits target, it is locked in permanently
     ◦ Target values can be modified until lock takes place

[92] Q: Describe common RILA riders.
A: ROP on death (“ROP DB”) is most common rider
   • DB = max[Interim Value, Premiums Paid − Withdrawals]
   GLWB riders more popular in recent years
   • Traditional benefit base: income payments = withdrawal % × benefit base
     ◦ Benefit base = nominal amount that grows at specified rate independent of AV
     ◦ Withdrawal % varies with age when withdrawals start
     ◦ If withdrawal > guaranteed amount → reduces future guaranteed withdrawals
   • Non-traditional (no benefit base): initial income payment = % of AV

[93] Q: Describe RILA hedging risk management.
A: • Hedging with options – major considerations:
   1. Transaction size: trading cost lowest when notional ≥ $1M
     ◦ Some insurers restrict index term start dates to 2–4x/mo to increase trade sizes
   2. Counterparty risk: option use introduces counterparty risk
   3. Liquidity: put options sold require collateral
     ◦ Collateral calls typically met with cash or treasuries
   4. Decrements: hedge positions should reflect deaths, surrenders, withdrawals
   • Hedging with VA GMxBs instead of options
     ◦ VA GMxBs typically have opposite equity market exposure as RILAs
     ◦ May be cheaper with less counterparty risk

[94] Q: Describe 4 RILA risks.
A: • Rate setting risk – profit ↓ as option prices ↑ after rate declaration
     ◦ Mitigate: more frequent rate adjustments
   • Investment risk – market and default risk in fixed income assets
     ◦ Mitigate: higher quality assets and appropriate pricing
   • Liquidity risk: contractholder behavior and collateral calls
     ◦ If surrenders spike → insurer may have to sell assets
     ◦ If index falls enough → insurer may have to sell assets to meet collateral calls
   • Operational risk – RILAs require significant expertise and complex operations
     ◦ Require expertise: hedging, investments, ALM, contract design
     ◦ Higher sophistication → higher risk of improper execution

[95] Q: Describe RILA asset management and regulatory considerations.
A: • General vs. separate account management
     ◦ GA assets often intermingled and not allocated to specific LOBs
     ◦ SA assets more often allocated to specific product/contract
     ◦ RILA assets held in both GA and SA
     ◦ RILA practice still emerging
   • Reserves and regulations
     ◦ NAIC developing nonforfeiture standard for RILAs
     ◦ RILAs follow VM-21 for US statutory reserves
     ◦ RILA RBC requirements follow those for VAs
     ◦ Under US GAAP:
     ◦ Base RILA contract follows ASC 815
     ◦ RILA riders classified as market risk benefits (MRBs)

A.2.6 — Structured Settlement Annuities  (A.2 Annuity Products)
----------------------------------------------------------------------

[96] Q: Why do governments view structured settlements positively?
A: • Helps control costs and reliance on government benefit programs
   • Less likely to be squandered than lump sum
   • Plaintiff doesn’t have to invest/manage large sum

[97] Q: List key steps to create a structured settlement.
A: • Defendant/casualty insurer agrees to pay settlement
   • Defendant purchases annuity contract from life insurer
     ◦ Marketed by specialized field force
     ◦ Consultants work between defendant and life insurer (earn commission)
   • Nearly all US structured settlements use qualified assignment
     ◦ Qualified assignment – defendant pays assignee to take over liability
     ◦ Assignee usually subsidiary of life insurer
     ◦ Releases defendant/casualty insurer from liability
   • Life insurer makes periodic payments to plaintiff

[98] Q: List benefit payment forms for structured settlements.
A: 1. Annuity certain – specified payments at periodic intervals over predetermined
   years
   2. Single life annuity – specified payments at periodic intervals for life
     ◦ Certain and life is common
   3. Temporary life annuity – single life annuity ending at specified date or death
   4. Lump sum – single payment at specified future date
     ◦ Can be certain or life-contingent
     ◦ Settlements often call for series of lump sums (e.g. every 5 years)
   Benefit escalator – payments increase at fixed rate (e.g. 2%) to protect against
   inflation

[99] Q: Describe mortality pricing considerations for structured settlements.
A: Mortality assumption – applicable to life-contingent payments
   • UW challenging
   • Settlement population ≠ normal insured populations
   • Settlement annuitant characteristics:
     ◦ Younger (before retirement)
     ◦ No anti-selection
     ◦ Low credibility (very limited data)
     ◦ Often have significant trauma (e.g. spinal injury) – very different from “normal”
   substandard cases (obesity, diabetes)
   • Age rate-ups common: set higher issue age
   • Mortality improvement: usually based on 1D scale (e.g. only differs by gender)

[100] Q: Describe interest rate pricing considerations for structured settlements.
A: Interest rates – required to discount benefit payments
   • Structured settlements are long-tailed liabilities (can extend well beyond 30
   years)
   • Max bond length usually 30 years (Treasuries, corporates)
   • Fixed nature of benefits allows investment in shorter, less liquid assets
     ◦ Private placements, mortgage-backed, etc.
   • Final rates = gross yields − margins for defaults, investment expense, profit

[101] Q: How are expense assumptions set for structured settlements?
A: • Percent of premium assumptions:
     ◦ Commissions and acquisition expenses that vary with premium
     ◦ Premium taxes
   • Fixed fee assumptions: good for large contracts; bad for small contracts
     ◦ Acquisition costs that don’t vary with premium
   • Percent of benefit assumptions:
     ◦ Maintenance and overhead

[102] Q: Describe pricing considerations:
• Post-tax return on invested surplus
• Administration of pricing/rate changes
A: • Post-Tax Return on Invested Surplus:
     ◦ Surplus strain from RC and conservative stat reserves
     ◦ Higher surplus strain → lower IRR
     ◦ Account for federal and state income taxes
   • Administration of Pricing/Rate Changes:
     ◦ Ratebook guarantee – insurers usually guarantee annuity rates for period
     ◦ Hold additional interest rate margin to hedge risk of falling rates

[103] Q: Describe mortality risk considerations for structured settlements.
A: • Credibility methods require expected basis: hard to find
   • Challenges using SOA studies:
     ◦ Tend to cover much longer periods
     ◦ Substandard SOA experience covers wide range of impairments
     ◦ UW standards may have changed over time
   • Anti-selection doesn’t occur in early durations as with life insurance
   • Very high competition amplifies bad UW risk
   • Mortality improvement could be substantial as medical treatment advances
     ◦ E.g. incurable spinal injury could be cured in future

[104] Q: Describe interest rate and A/L mismatch risk considerations for structured settlements.
A: • Typical approach:
     ◦ Match A/L cash flows for limited years
     ◦ Duration match in total
   • Substantial reinvestment risk: liability duration > asset duration
   • Strategies to mitigate reinvestment risk:
     ◦ Derivatives (futures, swaps)
     ◦ Long-maturity zero-coupon bonds
     ◦ Equities (but these increase RC)
   • Callable bonds and MBS introduce prepayment risk
   • To hedge ratebook guarantee risk, impose dollar limits on published rates

[105] Q: How do benefit escalators impact structured settlement risks?
A: Benefit escalators magnify mortality, interest rate, and reinvestment risk
   • Falling rates + mortality improvement = bad combo
   • Increasing payments offset discounting effect
   • Increases reinvestment risk beyond investment horizon

[106] Q: Describe reserving and valuation for structured settlements.
A: • Statutory Reserving – based on VM-22 for payout annuities
     ◦ Follows VM-22 for payouts:
     ◦ Reserve = PV of payments using prescribed mortality and interest
     ◦ May be modified to PBR approach in future (CTE 70)
     ◦ AG IX-A (9-A) prescribes “constant extra death method” for substandard cases
     ◦ Add deaths per 1000 until life expectancy matches UW
     ◦ Results in reserves that grade to standard basis
     ◦ Tends to produce mortality rates too high early, then too low later
     ◦ AG IX-B (9-B) provides guidance on durational interest rates
     ◦ Assigns new rate if benefit amounts exceed 110% of prior year
     ◦ Structured settlements subject to asset adequacy testing in US
   • GAAP Reserving – follows usual payout approach using rated age

[107] Q: Describe for structured settlements:
• Annuitization of attorneys’ fees
• Factoring
• Inflation protection variations
A: • Attorneys’ fees paid as annuity:
     ◦ Pricing similar to typical retirement annuities
     ◦ Not tax-free (not structured settlements)
   • Factoring (US only) – annuitant sells some/all payments to third party
     ◦ Exists because no other way to cash surrender contract
     ◦ Controversial: price may use high discount rate
   • Inflation protection variations:
     ◦ Most popular: benefit escalators
     ◦ Some indexed to CPI
     ◦ “Market indexed” to equity index (e.g. S&P 500) with guaranteed floor

[108] Q: Describe unique aspects of Canadian structured settlement market.
A: • Canadian structured settlements also tax-free
     ◦ Canadian tax authority = Canada Revenue Agency (CRA)
   • Notable differences with US market:
     ◦ 50% of structured settlements involve qualified assignments („100% in US)
     ◦ Market much smaller (2–3 insurers dominate)
     ◦ No factoring since structured settlement payments are non-transferrable

[116] Q: Outline pricing considerations for group annuities.

     ◦ Assumptions based on: zip code, job type, experience data
     ◦ Data consistency allows more aggressive pricing
     ◦ Mortality improvement usually based on industry tables
   3. Liquidity – relevant if deferred annuitants allowed to commute
     ◦ Requires assumption: % expected to choose commutation
   4. Market risk margins – for adverse market movements after quote
     ◦ Reduced by premium roll-forward or in-kind asset transfer

   8. Reinsurance or offshore structures used by insurer

[117] Q: Outline risk mitigation approaches for group annuities.

     ◦ Careful credit UW
     ◦ Invest in higher quality fixed income assets
     ◦ Limit non-fixed income investments

     ◦ Closely match A/L duration at key rates
   • Longevity risk: challenging (lifestyle, environment, medicine, pandemics)
     ◦ Limit # deferred annuitants
     ◦ Use conservative assumptions if data points misaligned
     ◦ Issue life business for natural hedge
     ◦ Reinsure longevity risk
   • Annuitant behavior risk:
     ◦ Use conservative assumptions for retirement date and pension form
   • Operational risk – incorrect payments, paying deceased annuitants
     ◦ Mitigate by regularly verifying annuitants alive

B.1.1–B.1.5 — Experience Assumptions: Introduction, Mortality, Lapse, Interest & Expense  (B.1 General Assumptions)
----------------------------------------------------------------------

[118] Q: List the 6 steps to establish experience assumptions.
A: “I DARDM” to establish experience assumptions!
   1. Identify assumptions needed
   2. Determine structure of each assumption
   3. Analyze experience and trends
   4. Review assumptions for reasonableness, consistency
   5. Document assumptions
   6. Monitor experience and update assumptions

[119] Q: List primary types of assumptions for experience studies.
A: 1. Obligation (liability) assumptions
     ◦ Mortality, lapse, expense, etc.
   2. Asset assumptions
     ◦ Investment income rate, capital gains rate, defaults, etc.
   3. Scenario assumptions
     ◦ Determinstic vs. stochastic interest rates
     ◦ Sensitivity testing

[120] Q: How to determine assumption structure?
A: Determine experience classes
   • Groups of policies with same assumption
   • Similar type, structure, marketing objectives
   Key principles when deciding complexity
   • Reflect differences in actual experience
   • Use objective definitions
   • Be practical and cost effective

[121] Q: List considerations when analyzing experience and trends.
A: 1. Evaluate credibility
     ◦ Quantity of data, homogeneity, reasonableness
   2. Evaluate data quality
     ◦ Alternative sources?
     ◦ Appropriate? Comprehensive enough?
   3. Actual vs. similar experience
     ◦ Use actual if available and credible
   4. Reflect trends
     ◦ Example: mortality improvement
   5. Reflect company and external factors
     ◦ UW, investment policy, other business practices
   6. Sensitivity test assumptions

[122] Q: List checks for reviewing assumptions for reasonableness and consistency.
A: Consistency Checks
   • Inflation consistent with investment earnings?
   • Mortality anti-selection and lapses
   Validation Checks
   • Static (starting reserves)
   • Dynamic (projected reserves)

[123] Q: How to document assumptions?
A: • Actual assumptions
     ◦ Value, applicable class
   • Data used
     ◦ Source, values, concerns, adjustments, reliance on others
   • Methods used to develop assumptions
     ◦ E.g. credibility method
   • How to use assumption
     ◦ Pricing vs. CFT
     ◦ Sensitivity testing
     ◦ Regulatory requirements

[124] Q: List key steps to determine mortality assumption.
A: 1. Determine Structure
     ◦ Example: S&U ALB table varying by M/F, SM/NS
   2. Analyze experience
     ◦ Do mortality study (e.g. 5-year CY study)
     ◦ Develop expected mortality rates from study
     ◦ Assess credibility of expected assumption
     ◦ Adjust mortality rates
     ◦ Trends (improvement, etc.)
     ◦ Anti-selection – conservation of deaths
     ◦ Blend with similar/industry experience if credibility low
     ◦ Adjust for differences in UW, distribution, market, etc.
     ◦ Blend M/F rates into aggregate rates (if needed)
   3. Monitor experience (A/E ratios)
   More detail on these steps in other flash cards

[125] Q: What is the structure of a mortality assumption?
A: • Select and ultimate common
   • Possible variations
     ◦ Risk class
     ◦ Selection process (type of UW)
     ◦ Size of policy (bigger face → lower mortality)
     ◦ Market method (direct, agent, etc.)
   • Mortality improvement
   • Age last birthday (ALB) vs. age nearest birthday (ANB)
   q^{ALB}(x) = 0.5 × [q^{ANB}(x) + q^{ANB}(x+1)]
   q^{ANB}(x) = 0.5 × [q^{ALB}(x) + q^{ALB}(x-1)]

[126] Q: How to analyze mortality experience using confidence intervals?
A: Mean and variance for expected claims E:
   E = nq
   Var(E) = npq
   Confidence interval for expected claims and the expected mortality rate q
   95% C.I. For E:
   E ± 1.96√Var(E)
   95% C.I. For q:
   95% C.I. For E / n

[127] Q: What are the 2 main types of mortality studies, and how are different risk classes
handled?
A: Typically done on standard issues, with separate studies for:
   1. Non-routine UW
   2. Conversions →usually higher mortality
   3. Sub-standards
   4. Non-forfeiture provisions (ETI, RPU)
   5. Multiple-life policies
   Two types of studies:
   1. Calendar year
     ◦ Look at activity for a single calendar year
     ◦ Must account for new policies, withdrawals, and deaths
   2. Anniversary-to-anniversary
     ◦ Simpler because study year coincides with policy year
     ◦ Done on an issue year / duration basis

[128] Q: How to determine calendar year mortality exposure?
A: A + N = W + D + B
   Situation → Exposure:
   • (A, W): s — Existing policy withdraws at age x + s
   • (A, D): 1 — Existing policy dies in calendar year
   • (A, B): 1 — Existing policy survives entire calendar year
   • (N, W): s − r — New policy enters at age x + r, then withdraws at age x + s
   • (N, D): 1 − r — New policy enters at age x + r, then dies in calendar year
   • (N, B): 1 − r — New policy enters at age x + r and survives entire calendar year

[129] Q: How to determine mortality rate from a mortality study?
A: Balducci assumption:
   q(x) = Deaths / Total Exposure = D / (A + (1−r)N − (1−s)W)
   Can be done at policy level or in aggregate
   • Policy level data
     ◦ Sum up individual exposures
   • Aggregate data (i.e. you just have the totals)
     ◦ Assume new issues and withdrawals occur mid-year (s = r = 0.5)
   q(x) = ΣD / (ΣA + 0.5ΣN − 0.5ΣW)

[130] Q: How to enhance credibility of mortality studies and develop confidence intervals for A/E
ratios?
A: Credibility Considerations
   1. Multi-year studies have higher credibility
     ◦ Problem: Too many years may hide trends (5 years is good)
   2. Credibility usually lower for amount-based studies
   3. A/E ratios useful for assessing credibility and tracking trends
   Actual-to-Expected (A/E) Ratios
   By Count: E = Σq(i), Var = Σq(i)[1−q(i)]
   By Amount: E = ΣA(i)q(i), Var = Σ[A(i)]²q(i)[1−q(i)]
   95% C.I. for Claims (CIC): E ± 1.96√Var(E)
   95% C.I. for A/E Ratio: CIC / E

[131] Q: What is the conservation of deaths principle?
A: q_{[x]+t} = w^{AS} q_{[x+r]+t-r} + (1 − w^{AS})q_{[x]+t}^{AS}
   Total mortality = weighted average of:
   1. Mortality of the "select" lives that lapse in duration r
   2. Mortality of the lives that stay with the original policy
   Key pricing concept: If you don’t account for selective lapsation, you will under-price
   the product

   begins

     ◦ Brokerage: Higher lapses

   3. Policy size
     ◦ Small policies: high early lapses
     ◦ Large policies: high later lapses
   4. Premium mode
     ◦ Lapses generally occur on premium due dates
     ◦ Flexible premium: assume uniform monthly
   5. Product type
     ◦ Deferred annuities: more sensitive to lapse
   rates than life insurance
   6. Conservation program effectiveness

[141] Q: Define:
• Study period
• Study population
• Cell
• Experience rates
• Rate types: Decrement rates, Utilization rates
A: • Study period – period of time covered
   • Study population – insureds, employees, etc.
   • Cell – combinations of data dimensioned by issue age, sex, smoker/nonsmoker,
   policy year, etc.
   • Experience rates – mortality rates, lapse rates, etc. for each cell
   • Rate type
     ◦ Decrement rates = probabilities from 0 to 1
     ◦ Mortality, morbidity, lapse, etc.
     ◦ Utilization rates ≠ probabilities (can exceed 1)
     ◦ Withdrawal rates, option election rates, etc.

[142] Q: Calculate EX reflecting withdrawal rates using Balducci Hypothesis:
• For individual calculations
• For grouped calculations
A: E_{x} = ℓ_{x} − \sum_{i=1}^{w_{x}}(1 − t_{i}) = (ℓ_{x} − w_{x}) + \sum_{i=1}^{w_{x}} t_{i}   for individual calculations
   E_{x} = ℓ_{x} − (1/2)w_{x}   for grouped calculations

[143] Q: Formula for exposure-weighted average mortality rate over an N-year period starting at
age x
A: q = \frac{\sum_{t=0}^{N} E_{x+t} q_{x+t}}{\sum_{t=0}^{N} E_{x+t}}
     = \frac{\sum_{t=0}^{N} d_{x+t}}{\sum_{t=0}^{N} E_{x+t}}

[144] Q: Amount-weighted studies: individual amount weights
A: Individual amount weights
   • Multiply each life year exposure by policy’s DB
   • Sum amount exposures and calculate qx’s in the usual way

[145] Q: Amount-weighted studies: grouped amount weights
A: Grouped amount weights
   • The ℓx terms → ℓx × Average DB In Force
   • The dx terms → dx × Average DB Paid on Death
   • The wx terms → wx × Average DB on Withdrawn Policies
   • Sum amount exposures and calculate qx’s in the usual way

[146] Q: Relationship between amounted-weighted q and life-weighted q?
A: Amounted-weighted q will be > life-weighted q if average DB paid on death > average
   DB in force

[147] Q: A/E ratio at individual level?
A: The A/E ratio at each age x is:
   (A/E)_{x} = \frac{d_{x}}{d_{x}^{e}} = \frac{q_{x}E_{x}}{q_{x}^{e}E_{x}} = \frac{q_{x}}{q_{x}^{e}}
   where e denotes expected values, and the others are actuals

[148] Q: A/E ratio at aggregate level?
A: The A/E ratio at an aggregate level is:
   q^{e} = \frac{ΣE_{x} q_{x}^{e}}{ΣE_{x}} = \frac{Σd_{x}^{e}}{ΣE_{x}}
   (A/E) = \frac{d}{d^{e}} = \frac{Σd_{x}}{Σd_{x}^{e}} = \frac{q}{q^{e}}

[149] Q: List uses of A/E analysis.
A: • Compare actual mortality or lapse experience to expected amounts
   • Develop best estimate assumptions as multiple of expected amounts
   Best Estimate Rate for Age x = (A/E) × q_{x}^{e}
   • Can be used for valuation, risk management, financial planning, etc.

[150] Q: Formulas for frequency and severity?
A: f_{x} = \frac{n_{x}}{E_{x}} = average claim frequency
   s_{x} = \frac{C_{x}}{n_{x}} = average claim amount
   • n_{x} = number of claims incurred at age x
   • C_{x} = total claim amount incurred at age x
   • E_{x} = central exposure (or initial)

[151] Q: Loss ratio formula?
A: Loss ratio = cost of claims as % of premiums paid (P_{x})
   LR_{x} = \frac{C_{x}}{P_{x}}

[152] Q: Assume: M_{x} = max w/d allowed — W_{x} = total actual w/d amount — n_{x} = # of contracts
who took w/d
Define formulas for:
• Count-based exposure (excludes deaths and lapses)
• Withdrawal frequency
• Average size (severity) of w/d taken
• Average w/d taken as % of max w/d
A: E_{x} = ℓ_{x} − d_{x} − w_{x} = count-based exposure (excludes deaths and lapses)
   f_{x} = \frac{n_{x}}{E_{x}} = withdrawal frequency
   s_{x} = \frac{W_{x}}{n_{x}} = average size (severity) of withdrawals taken
   u_{x} = \frac{s_{x}}{M_{x}} = average withdrawal taken as % of the max withdrawal
   Note: little w is still a lapse rate here; big W is what we’re

[153] Q: List distortions caused by amount-weighted calculations.
A: 1. One or few large amounts can distort numerator
     ◦ Not easy to fix after the fact (cap max amount allowed in study)
   2. Risks or behaviors can differ significantly by amount
     ◦ Small policies may have less commitment
     ◦ Large policies may be used to defraud
     ◦ Solution: add size band to cells

[154] Q: List advantages of multi-year studies.
A: • Higher credibility
   • Less distortion caused by reporting lags
   • Can populate many more cells
   • Allows study of trends if study-year or CY variable is included

[155] Q: List 4 examples of reporting lags.
A: • Death claims often not reported for months after death
   • LTCI claims not reported until after elimination period
   • “Shoe boxing” – lag between direct insurers and reinsurers
   • Life annuities sometimes continue paying after death

[156] Q: List 2 solutions for reporting lags.
A: • Wait a few months after study period before gathering data
   • Develop IBNR claims estimates from previous studies

[157] Q: List 3 examples of non-uniform events.
A: • Lapses often vary by month (e.g. PLT period)
   • Prorated mortality rates tend to underestimate deaths early in the year at old ages
   • LTCI claims immediately following claim

[158] Q: List 3 ways to compensate for non-uniform distributions.
A: 1. Use shorter interval of time
   2. Apply adjustment factors
   3. Use constant force mortality assumption for old ages

[159] Q: List considerations for inclusion of partial policy years in decrement studies.
A: • OK when event is evenly distributed over the year
     ◦ Common for biologically-driven rates (mortality, morbidity, etc.)
     ◦ Allocating PYs between CYs is also common
   • Otherwise, partial policy years will distort results
     ◦ Behavioral rates usually not evenly distributed
     ◦ Lapse, withdrawal, option election, etc.
     ◦ Do not allocate PYs between CYs

[160] Q: What is the annual exposure method?
A: Annual exposure method – consistent with Balducci Hypothesis
   • Exposes decrements to end of next anniversary (even if beyond study period)
   • Disadvantage: overstates rates in first partial year; understates rates in final
   partial
   • Why exposure is extended beyond study period:
     ◦ Rate is based on probability
     ◦ Captures exposure missed by initial partial year
     ◦ Also true of distributed exposure method

[161] Q: What is the distributed exposure method?
A: Distributed exposure method – assumes decrement is uniformly distributed
   • Allocates exposure and decrements by both PY and CY
   • More accurate when underlying rates are increasing; otherwise less accurate
   • Produces same total PY exposure as annual method, but CY allocation differs

[162] Q: Algorithm for calculating distributed exposure?
A: 1. Calculate annual exposure in usual way
   2. First-half exposure = PY t exposure in CY:
     ◦ Expose deaths and surviving policies from policy anniversary to end of CY
     ◦ Expose other decrements (lapse, etc.) from policy anniversary to decrement date
   3. Second-half exposure = Total Annual Exposure − First-Half Exposure
     ◦ This will be allocated to next CY
   In plain English: switch the partial year formulas in the annual exposure method
   • Move final partial year deaths to next study period’s first partial year

[163] Q: List product-related considerations that may affect experience study calculations.
A: • Deaths in premium grace period (30–60 days)
     ◦ Actual DB paid is net of overdue premiums, but use full DB in mortality study
     ◦ Expose lapses to beginning of grace period
   • Compromised and denied claims – don’t count as death claims in mortality
   study
   • Study variables changing over time (admin system changes)
     ◦ Admin systems may change plan codes or billing frequencies (distorts exposure)
   • Reinsured amounts – can study amounts net of reinsurance if material enough
   • Substandard and uninsurable lives – typically excluded

[164] Q: List at least 4 claim characteristics shared by LTCI and DI products.
A: 1. Involve multiple payments that can last for months or years
   2. Usually paid monthly
   3. Subject to elimination period before claim payments start (up to 1 year)
   4. Usually limited in some way (e.g. max age for DI, max total benefits, etc.)
   5. Paid when insured meets certain conditions
   6. Claims stop when insured recovers but can start again

[165] Q: List types of morbidity studies for LTCI and DI:
• Claim incidence studies
• Claim severity studies
• Claim termination studies
• Benefit utilization rate studies
A: 1. Claim incidence studies
     ◦ Claim incidence rates = annual probabilities
     ◦ Usually done on "all claims"
     ◦ Usually based on annual exposure method
   2. Claim severity studies
   Average length of claim = Total Months of Claims / Number of Claims
   Average cost per claim = Total Claims Paid / Number of Claims
   3. Claim termination studies – perform separate studies for recovery and death
     ◦ DI claims terminate when proof of disability not submitted
     ◦ LTC claims terminate when requests for reimbursement stops
     ◦ Termination rates are highest and most volatile for new claims
   4. Benefit utilization rate studies (LTCI only)
   Benefit Utilization Rate = Actual Benefits / Max Benefits

[166] Q: List 4 DI-specific considerations for experience studies.
A: • Elimination periods – claims usually reported after elimination period
   • Partial disability benefits – reduces average cost per claim
   • Recovery followed by relapse within 6 months
   • Claim settlements – lump sum paid in lieu of future monthly payments

[167] Q: List 5 LTCI-specific considerations for experience studies.
A: 1. Elimination period – typically 30, 60, 90, 180 and 365 days and can also vary by
   LTC claim type
   2. Some LTCI benefits indexed to inflation
   3. Mortality rates do not follow any established table
   4. Claim data must capture diagnosis (very important since it drives claim length)
   5. Claim data not always organized ideally and requires work

[168] Q: Experience study considerations for deferred annuities?
A: • Deferred annuity utilization rates
     ◦ GMxBs: compare benefit paid to amount payable in absence of GMxB
   • Contract year data challenges
     ◦ Data usually monthly
     ◦ Common approach: convert account balances to contract year basis using 13-month
   average

[169] Q: Experience study considerations for payout annuities?
A: • Immediate annuities
     ◦ Self-selection – people buy IAs when they think they’re healthier than average
     ◦ Choice of IA may indicate health
     ◦ Larger IAs tend to have lower mortality
     ◦ Amount exposure should be based on monthly payment, not reserves
     ◦ Mortality improvement is key consideration
   • Structured settlements – arise from injury lawsuits
     ◦ Usually involves one or more lump sums (not lifetime payouts)
     ◦ Mortality is higher than IAs
     ◦ Amount exposure should be based on reserves or premium paid

[178] Q: What is the purpose of graduation?
A: Graduation is a mathematical process that smooths an array of rates
   • Assumes that the “true” rates follow a continuous curve
   • Required when rates do not come from models
   • Can also be used on modeled rates
   • Goal: balance fit and smoothness

[179] Q: List the properties of a preferred graduation method.
A: 1. Produces same total events as ungraduated rates
   2. Parameters control amount of smoothness applied
     ◦ Input fit, table fit, smoothness

[180] Q: Credibility and data grouping considerations when graduating rates?
A: Non-credible (“incredible”) rates distort graduated rates
   • Smoothness calculation weights credible and non-credible rates equally
     ◦ Biggest problem: very young and very old ages
   • ↑credibility usually comes at cost of graduation
     ◦ Grouping rates by age (e.g. quinquennial)
     ◦ Grouping data by calendar or policy years

[181] Q: List the 4 steps of the graduation process.

   2. Review and adjust input as needed
   3. Run graduation process for 3 parameters: input fit, table fit, smoothness
     ◦ Set parameter value and run graduation algorithm
     ◦ Review/graph/evaluate results
     ◦ Repeat until satisfied with parameter
   4. Run final graduation using best fit and smoothness parameters

[182] Q: Formula for total variance for all lives (assuming individual data available)?
A: Total variance for all lives:
   Variance_{r} = q_{r}(1 − q_{r}) Σ Amount_{x}² × ExposedCount_{x}

[183] Q: Ways to approximate total variance for all lives?
A: Approximations for variance (from least refined to most):
   1. Use overall average size:
   Variancer ≈ AvgSizeOA² × qr(1 − qr) Σ ExposedCountx
   2. Use average size for lives contributing to qr:
   Variancer ≈ AvgSizer² × qr(1 − qr) Σ ExposedCountx
   3. Allocate average size between the 2 most common sizes in size groups
     ◦ Can also be used to develop factors to apply to method 1 or 2

[184] Q: List common interpolation methods for table development.

     ◦ Log-Linear or Log-Cubic – useful for mortality rates (↑at constant rate)
   • 2-dimensional methods
     ◦ Bilinear
     ◦ Bicubic (smoothest)

[185] Q: List 3 methods for extending rates in a table.

     ◦ Grade from study’s rates to existing table rates
   2. Other data sources

     ◦ Should reproduce rates for nearby credible ages

[186] Q: List 5 reasons why initial rates developed for a table may be deficient.

[187] Q: List the 5 components of enforcement reviews.

B.1.8 — Credibility Methods Applied to Life, Health, and Pensions  (B.1 General Assumptions)
----------------------------------------------------------------------

[192] Q: Define credibility and its relationship to data volume.
A: Credibility in actuarial context = statistical credibility
   • Arose more than a century ago in P&C industry
   • Low credibility does not mean “not believable”
     ◦ It means “should have less weight”
   • Credibility ↑as data volume↑(σ ↓)
   • Full credibility occurs when addition of more data would be immaterial
     ◦ Any remaining error is inconsequential

[193] Q: List the 3 major families of credibility methods.
A: 1. Limited Fluctuation Method (aka Classical Credibility)
     ◦ Intended to limit effect of random experience fluctuations on estimate
     ◦ Simple and widely used, but has theoretical shortcomings
   2. Greatest Accuracy Method (aka Bühlmann Credibility)
     ◦ Appeared in the 1960s
     ◦ Intended to make estimation errors as small as possible
     ◦ More complex and less popular, but more theoretically grounded
   3. Bayesian Method
     ◦ Combines prior distribution with likelihood function based on observed data
     ◦ Also the foundation of GAM
     ◦ Has not been used much, but usage may↑in the future

[194] Q: General formula for determining credibility-weighted rate?
A: Credibility-Weighted Rate =
     Z × Observed Rate + (1 − Z) × Prior Rate   (LFM)
     Z × Observed Rate + (1 − Z) × Portfolio Rate   (GAM)
   Observed Rate = best estimate or result from recent experience study
   Z = weight assigned to Observed Rate
   • Ranges from 100% (fully credible) to 0% (no credibility)
   1 − Z = remaining weight assigned to:
   • Prior Rate (LFM) – prior rate based on much larger experience study
     ◦ Assumed to be fully credible
     ◦ Often based on large industry table (e.g. 2001 VBT)
   • Portfolio Rate (GAM) – overall rate for a set of related risk groups

[195] Q: Prior rate used for Limited Fluctuation Credibility?
A: Prior rate = most appropriate estimate absent any experience data
   • Should be consistent with the business in scope
   • Potential problems:
     ◦ May be based on different target market, UW, and/or distribution system
     ◦ Requires adjustments or else will introduce error
     ◦ May not be fully credible

[196] Q: Credibility weight formula under Limited Fluctuation Credibility?
A: Z = min[1, √(NAC/NFC)]
   • NAC = number of actual observations (claims, policies, etc.)
   • NFC = number of observations required for full credibility
   • PFC = probability threshold for full credibility
   • RFC = error tolerance for full credibility
   Most common assumption: PFC = 90%, RFC = 5%
   • We want our rate to be within ±5% of true rate at least 90% of the time
   • NFC = 1082 for Poisson-distributed claims (P&C, health)
   • NFC = 1082p = 1082(1 − q) for binomially distributed claims (mortality, lapse)

[197] Q: Relationship between observed rate and full credibility?
A: • As q ↑, NFC ↓
   • Credibility is based on relative error: as q ↑, error threshold ↑
   • Small decrement studies require more exposed lives (n)

[198] Q: Asymptotic formula for credibility?
A: Developed by Albert W. Whitney (1918):
   Z = N / (N + K)
   where N = exposure (e.g. claim count) and K = "a constant"
   • Z = 0 for no experience data and Z → 1 as N → ∞
   • K can be determined by actuarial judgment or based on experience
   • Higher values of K result in:
     ◦ Lower credibility weights
     ◦ Slower convergence as N → ∞

[199] Q: How to calculate credibility weights based on confidence intervals?
A: Z = probability that observed rate is within "tight confidence interval"
   Z = Pr[μ_{T}(1 − r) ≤ μ ≤ μ_{T}(1 + r)]
   • μ_{T}, σ_{T} = true (unknown) values of mean and standard deviation
   • μ, σ = observed values
   • r = ratio that specifies the tight C.I. (analogous to RFC)
   Assuming standard normal distribution and μ/σ ≈ μ_{T}/σ_{T}:
   Z = Pr[−r(μ/σ) ≤ (μ − μ_{T})/σ_{T} ≤ r(μ/σ)]
   Credibility increases when μ/σ ↑ and/or r ↑ (wider C.I.)
   Normal distribution appropriate for large N; binomial is best for mortality/lapse

[200] Q: How to calculate number of deaths required for full credibility in mortality study?
A: 1. Calculate observed mortality rates by band and in aggregate
   q_{cnt} = Count of Deaths / Count Exposed
   q_{amt} = Amount of Deaths / Amount Exposed
   2. Calculate variance (σ^{2}) and standard deviation (σ)
   σ_{cnt}^{2} = (Count Exposed)(q_{cnt})(1 − q_{cnt})
   σ_{amt}^{2} = (Count Exposed)(Policy Size)²(q_{amt})(1 − q_{amt})
   3. Calculate standard deviation as % of deaths observed
   Std Dev as % of Deaths = σ / Count of Deaths
   Full Cred Std Dev as % of Deaths = 0.05/1.64485 = 3.04%
   4. Calculate deaths required for full credibility (count and amount)
   Study to Full Cred Ratio = Std Dev as % of Deaths / Full Cred Std Dev as % of Deaths
   Full Cred Deaths = (Study to Full Cred Ratio)² × Observed Deaths
   = 1082p for count-based

[201] Q: How does credibility differ on count vs. amount basis?
A: • Effect of policy count distribution by face amount size:
     ◦ Smaller policies usually outnumber larger policies
     ◦ Therefore count-based exposure is higher at lower face amounts
     ◦ Count-based σ2 ↓ as policy size ↑ (opposite for amount-based)
     ◦ Count-based variance is directly proportional to sample size: npq
     ◦ Amount-based variance = npq × Size^{2}
     ◦ Takeaway: count-based Z may overstate credibility on larger policies
   • Amount-based credibility weights tend to be lower than count-based
     ◦ Main reason: credibility ↓ as variance ↑
     ◦ Amount-based variance is usually higher than count-based

[202] Q: Goal and assumptions underlying Greatest Accuracy Credibility?
A: Goal: minimize expected value of square of differences between:
   1. B ·uhlmann-weighted estimate
   2. Bayesian predictive mean
   GAM makes 2 assumptions:
   1. Each risk group in portfolio follows its own unknown distribution
   2. The distributions of the groups follow an unknown distribution

[203] Q: Greatest Accuracy credibility weight formula?
A: Z = N/(N + K) = N/(N + v/a)
   N = claim count exposure
   Portfolio = collection of related risk groups
   • Groups of similar risks from different geographical areas or occupations
   • Collection of insurance companies
   v = expected value of process variance = E[Var[X]]
   • As v ↓, credibility ↑
   • v ↓ when risk group has large exposure or claim counts
   a = variance of hypothetical means = Var[E[X]]
   • As a ↑, credibility ↑
   • a ↑ when there is more variation in means
   • Extreme case: if a = 0, Z = 0 (nothing unique about each group)

[204] Q: List strengths and weaknesses of Limited Fluctuation Credibility.
A: Strengths of LFM:
   • Simple and easy: only requires number of claims
   • Useful for experience rating when prior rate is appropriate
   • Only requires data for risks being assessed and prior rate
   • Could be improved by using σ as % of claims to calculate Z under C.I. method
   Weaknesses of LFM:
   • Formulas and inputs are arbitrary
   • Prior rate gets same weight, 1 − Z, regardless of its accuracy
   • May significantly overstate count-based Z’s if rates are based on amount
   • Results distorted if there is no prior rate (e.g. new product)
   • Variance may be worse measure of “quality” than MSE used by GAM
   • Z reaches 1.0 prematurely compared with asymptotic formula

[205] Q: List strengths and weaknesses of Greatest Accuracy Credibility.
A: Strengths of GAM
   • Spells out all aspects and clearly states all assumptions
   • Produces exact answer by applying basic probability principles
   • Has clear objective function: minimize mean-square error
   • No arbitrary choices unrelated to observed random variables
   Weaknesses of GAM
   • Requires portfolio of data from comparable risk groups
   • Portfolio rate will be distorted if risk groups not sufficiently similar
     ◦ Analogous to prior rate not being appropriate under LFM
   • May be inaccurate when random variable has heavy tail
   • Empirical Bayesian estimates of σ2 can be negative, “which is nonsensical”

[206] Q: Compare Greatest Accuracy Credibility with Bayesian Credibility.
A: Bayesian credibility was starting point for Bühlmann credibility (GAM)
   • GAM credibility-weighted rate falls between observed rate and portfolio rate
   • Bayesian: not necessarily
   GAM has the following advantages over Bayesian:
   • Requires less mathematical skill
   • Computationally easier
   • Does not require prior distribution
     ◦ GAM uses experience data instead

[207] Q: List at least 4 characteristics of lapse-supported insurance.

   • ROP can convert limited-pay products to lapse-supported
   • Benefits of ROP:
     ◦ Less risky if lapses driven by independent events (e.g. employee turnover)
     ◦ Help blocks/companies with poor persistency
     ◦ ↑profitability in low income markets

B.2.1 — Selective Lapsation for Renewable Term  (B.2 Term-Specific Assumptions)
----------------------------------------------------------------------

[210] Q: List types of post-level premium structures in North America.
A: Post-level premium structures in the US:
   1. Jump to ART – premium jumps from level to much higher ART premiums
   2. Grade to ART – premium gradually ↑from level to ART
   3. Jump to new level period
   4. Jump to other – combination of 2 and 3
   In Canada, most products are “jump to new level”

[211] Q: Selective Lapsation Provisions from CIA Education Note “Expected Mortality”
A: • Selective lapses – have same mortality as newly selected lives (qrx+ts)
   • Lapse rates↑temporarily when premium rates ↑at renewal date
   • Healthy lives more likely to lapse at renewal than unhealthy lives
   • Factors that drive level of selective lapsation:
     ◦ Size of premium rate increase
     ◦ Period between premium increases
     ◦ Duration
     ◦ Policy size
     ◦ Distribution systems
   • Selective lapses may occur at times other than at renewal
   • PL mortality experience may not reflect selective lapses (adjust as needed)

[212] Q: SOA Survey on Post-Level Assumptions
A: • Most insurers assumed <100% lapse rate at end of level period
   • Assumed shock lapse↑as level period↑
     ◦ 100% more common for 20-30-yr term than 10- and 15-yr
     ◦ Median shock lapse: „80% for 10-yr to „95% for 30-yr
   • Median PL mortality = 230%–300% of level period
   • Mortality deterioration assumptions generally grade down over 3 years
   • Assumed lapses and mortality deterioration had strong correlation
   • Median premium jump = 3x–25x
     ◦ Varied by issue age, gender, term period, and risk class

[213] Q: SOA Report on Post-Level Experience: Lapse
A: Lapse dates set equal to beginning of grace period if they occurred during grace period
   • Grace period = time after missed insurance premium is due
   Key lapse findings:
   • Shock lapses ↑with premium jump ratio (PJR)
   • Lapse trends by PJR are similar across companies:
     ◦ ↑very quickly at lowest PJRs
     ◦ ↑at decreasing rate with PJR
     ◦ Level off at highest PJRs
   • Within each PJR band, lapses ↑with issue age and face amount
   • Lapses heavily skewed to end of final level year and beginning of first PL year

[214] Q: SOA Report on Post-Level Experience: Mortality
A: • Mortality rates ↑significantly with PJR
   • Higher lapses ↑effect of excess mortality in grace period
     ◦ Provides “free” life insurance to policies that have lapsed
   • Mortality ↑at increasing rate as lapse rates↑

[215] Q: Describe the subpopulations within a cohort of lives at beginning of the post-level
period.
A: T = U + A + S + P = 100% = total cohort
   • S = excess lapsers who have select mortality (healthy lives)
     ◦ Often unknown (requires estimation and sensitivity testing)
     ◦ S = Selective% × (Total Lapse Rate − Base Lapse Rate)
   • A = excess lapsers with average mortality
     ◦ Extra lapses that occur for reasons other than health
     ◦ A = (1 − Selective%) × (Total Lapse Rate − Base Lapse Rate)
   • U = lapsers with same mortality as underlying (base) mortality
     ◦ Normal, base lapses (not lapsing for health reasons)
   • P = persisting cohort (policyholders that don’t lapse)
     ◦ Generally unhealthy lives willing to pay high premiums to keep policy inforce
     ◦ We can solve for P = 1 − S − A − U
     ◦ Since only P remains inforce, total lapse rate = S + A + U

[216] Q: Describe 3 primary methods for estimating post-level mortality.
A: 1. Dukes-MacDonald
     ◦ Originally assumed all excess lapses are select
     ◦ Later variations (DM1, DM2) assume some excess lapses are non-select
   2. Becker-Kitsos
     ◦ Refines DM by adding effectiveness factor
     ◦ Assumes additional lapses are fully select plus extra amount of mortality
     ◦ Extra mortality grades off over select period
   3. VTP2: published by CIA in 1986 Valuation Technique Paper
     ◦ Similar to DM1, but with following differences:
     ◦ A and S lapse rates apply just prior to anniversary
     ◦ Underlying (U) lapse and mortality rate apply continuously
     ◦ DM1 assumes U lapses occur immediately prior to S lapses

[217] Q: Formula for persister mortality under Becker-Kitsos, Dukes-MacDonald (DM1 and
DM2), and VTP2?
A: q′_{[x]+t} =
     S × (q_{[x+t]} + q_{[x+t]}^{extra}) + U × q′_{[x]+t} + (1 − S − U) × q″_{[x]+t}   for BK
     S × q_{[x+t]} + A × q_{[x]+t} + U × q′_{[x]+t} + (1 − S − A − U) × q″_{[x]+t}   for DM1
     S × q_{[x+t]} + U × q′_{[x]+t} + (1 − S − U) × q″_{[x]+t}   for DM2
     S × q_{[x+t]} + A × q_{[x]+t} + (1 − S − A) × q″_{[x]+t}   for VTP2
   Under BK:
   • q_{[x+t]}^{extra} = R × G(t) × (q_{[x]+t} − q_{[x+t]})
   • Recommend assuming G = 1

[218] Q: Describe common problems/issues for all persister mortality methods.
A: • Skewness: all methods assume excess lapses occur EOY
     ◦ Reality: ~90% of lapses occur BOY right after renewal
     ◦ Underestimates mortality deterioration
   • Shape of underlying mortality table
     ◦ If industry table multiples too low, excess mortality < 0
     ◦ Excess mortality = q″ − q′
     ◦ Ideally, excess mortality should grade to zero over time
   • Accounting for grace period deaths
     ◦ More important when lapse rates are high
     ◦ DM1 mortality for persisters who die in grace period:
   [S × q_{[x+t]} + (A + U) × q′_{[x]+t}] × Grace Period / (365 × (1 − T))
     ◦ T = S + A + U = total lapse rate occurring at renewal date
     ◦ Add this extra mortality to PL persister mortality
     ◦ Under VTP2, formula is same with U = 0%

[219] Q: How appropriate are the main persister mortality methods for US and Canadian
experience?
A: Key conclusions were similar based on US and Canadian experience:
   • VTP2 doesn’t replicate actual post-renewal mortality unless you use very high S
   • DM1 comes closer to matching actual mortality experience
   • Caveats:
     ◦ Credibility is low, so use caution with results
     ◦ No Canadian data reflects both mortality and lapse

[220] Q: How can VTP2 be revised to be more accurate?
A: Original VTP2 calculated selective and average lapse rates incorrectly
   S = (w_{total} − U) × Selective%
   A = (w_{total} − U) × (1 − Selective%)
   The correct calculation reflects that excess lapse rates occur after underlying lapses:
   S = [(w_{total} − U) × Selective%] / (1 − U)
   A = [(w_{total} − U) × (1 − Selective%)] / (1 − U)
   "VTP2-revised" results in higher select and average lapse rates
   • And therefore higher persister mortality since q″ ↑ as P ↓
   • q″ was as much as 22% higher after correcting lapse rates

B.2.2 — Term Conversions: Pricing and Reserving  (B.2 Term-Specific Assumptions)

[221] Q: List 3 philosophies for covering cost of conversions.
A: 1. Reflect conversion cost in term pricing (most common)
     ◦ This is what the author advocates
     ◦ Allows reserves to build for conversions
     ◦ Aligns revenue with source of risk (term “swaption”)
   2. Develop separate permanent premiums for conversions
     ◦ Avoids cross-subsidization but is administratively challenging
     ◦ Alternatively, could reinsure conversions using YRT PISM rates
   3. Reflect extra conversion mortality in permanent policy pricing
     ◦ Might be appealing if insurer has mature experience
     ◦ Can make permanent mortality artificially high

[222] Q: Two stage model for pricing term conversions?
A: 1. At issue, determine expected extra mortality cost for term conversions
     ◦ For each year when conversion is possible calculate:
   Extra NSP for Conversions = K_{x,r} = PVFB_{Converted} − PVFB_{Standard WL}
   = reserve for conversions at duration r
   2. Calculate charge for term policyholders
   A_{x,r} = _{r}p_{x} × e_{x,r} × K_{x,r} × v^{r}
   Total PV of Expected Excess Mortality = Σ_{r} A_{x,r}
   Level Conversion Charge = Total PVFB of Excess Mortality / ä_{x:n|}

[223] Q: Factors that cause cost of conversions to vary?
A: Conversion cost ↑when base mortality ↑
   • Higher issue ages
   • Males (compared to females)
   • Smokers (compared to non-smokers)
   • Non-preferred (compared to preferred classes)

[224] Q: Relationship between conversion period length and conversion cost?
A: Restricting conversion period reduces cost of conversion
   • Disallowing conversions after year 10, reduces cost roughly 30%
   • Caveat: policyholders will accelerate conversions in response

[225] Q: Compare newer statistical approaches with traditional experience studies.
A: Advantages of statistical approaches over traditional experience studies:
   • Consider all variables simultaneously (eliminates bias in correlated variables)
   • Handles data more efficiently
   • Generates smoother curves without manual adjustments
   • Provides distribution of outcomes rather than only mean
   • Cross-term variables can capture interrelationships and improve model fit
   Key disadvantage of statistical models: can become complex
   • Simpler models easier to interpret and implement in practice
   • Actuary must balance complexity/accuracy with simplicity

[226] Q: List key factors that cause conversion rates to vary.
A: • Late converters have highest conversion rates
     ◦ Year 10 conversion rate≈ 7% compared to <1% in years 1–9
   • Year 10 conversion rates higher for worse risk classes
     ◦ Smokers > Standard NS > Preferred NS
     ◦ Interpretation: smokers more likely to be declined if applying for new policy
   • Impact of issue age varies by duration:
     ◦ Early years: youngest issue ages have slightly higher conversion rates
     ◦ Middle years: no difference by issue age
     ◦ Years 9–10: older issue age→ higher conversion rates
   • Large policies have highest year 10 conversion rates
     ◦ A 1M policy’s conversion rate is nearly 2x a <100K policy

[227] Q: List key factors that cause mortality to vary for converted policies.
A: • PISM ratios highest 1 year after conversion, then fall
     ◦ High mortality immediately after conversion indicates anti-selection
     ◦ Even 10 years later, PC mortality > 100% of level period
   • Larger policies have higher PC mortality (more anti-selection)
   • Late converters have highest PISM ratios after conversion
     ◦ Late converter PISM ratios decline after year 1
     ◦ Middle converter PISM ratios slightly higher than early converters
     ◦ Middle and early converter PISM ratios are flat after conversion
   • Younger issue ages have higher PISM ratios after conversion
     ◦ <40 group’s initial PISM ratio>500%
     ◦ Next highest group: 60+ (300%)
     ◦ All PISM ratios decline by year after conversion

[228] Q: List key factors that cause lapse rates to vary for converted policies.
A: • Overall lapse rates highest 1–2 years after conversion
     ◦ PC lapse rates roughly 5% and 5.5% 1 and 2 years after conversion
     ◦ Lapse rates fall to about 2.5% by year 4, then level off
   • Lapse rates by gender fairly similar all years after conversion
     ◦ Female lapses slightly higher initially; reverses later
     ◦ Gender may not be useful model variable
   • Late converters have lower lapse rates after conversion
   • Larger policies have lower PC lapse rates

[229] Q: List most significant drivers of anti-selection on term conversions.
A: Anti-selection highest for late converters
   • Highest conversion rates
   • Highest initial post-conversion mortality ratios
   • Lowest post-conversion lapse rates
   Other factors that ↑anti-selection risk:
   • Younger issue ages
   • Larger policies
   • Smokers

[230] Q: Why is product classification important for statutory accounting?
A: Product classification determines:
   1. Revenue and costs reporting in income statement
   2. Methodologies and assumptions for policy reserves
   3. Annual statement exhibits, schedules, and supporting info

[231] Q: List four classifications under SSAP No. 50.
A: 1. Life contracts – financial assistance to beneficiary when insured dies
     ◦ Long coverage period; mortality risk ↑with age
     ◦ Examples: WL, endowments, term, UL, variable life, annuities
   2. Deposit-type contracts – no insurance risk
     ◦ Financial/investment instruments (like banks)
     ◦ Examples: annuities certain, lottery payouts, dividend accumulations
   3. A&H contracts – protection vs. economic loss from medical condition
   4. P&C contracts – protection vs. physical/financial damage to property or injury

[232] Q: What does SSAP No. 51 cover?
A: Income recognition and reserving for life contracts
   • Recognize premiums as revenue on gross basis
     ◦ Gross premium = amount charged to policyholder
     ◦ Net premium = amount used in stat reserves
     ◦ Loading = Gross – Net Premium
   • Stat reserves required for all unmatured contracts
   • Methods/assumptions: Appendices A-820 (SVL), A-822 (AOMR), C (AGs)
   • Reserves must comply with ASOPs from ASB

[233] Q: Define RBC ratio and list 5 major risk categories.
A: RBC ratio = Total Adjusted Capital / Life Insurer’s RBC
   5 major risk categories:
   1. C-0: Asset Risk – Affiliates – default risk in affiliated investments
   2. C-1: Asset Risk – Other – default risk (debt) — market losses (equity)
   3. C-2: Insurance Risk – adverse mortality/morbidity
   4. C-3: Interest Rate, Health & Market Risk
     ◦ Reinvestment rates < guaranteed rates
     ◦ Capital losses (sale price < cost)
     ◦ Losses on variable product guarantees from market return changes
   5. C-4: General Business Risk – fraud, mismanagement, other risks

C.1.3–C.1.4 — Ch. 11: Valuation Methodologies  (C.1 US Statutory Valuation)
----------------------------------------------------------------------

[234] Q: What is the net level premium reserve method?
A: Net premiums are constant % of gross premiums
   _{t}V_{x}^{NLP} = PVFB_{t} − NP_{0} · ä_{x+t}
   NP_{0} = PB_{0} = (PVFB_{0} / ä_{x}) = NP for first policy year
   r_{t}^{GP} = gross premium ratio = GP_{t} / GP_{0}
   ä_{x} = 1 + v · _{1}p_{x} · r_{1}^{GP} + v² · _{2}p_{x} · r_{2}^{GP} + ···
   ä_{x+t} = r_{t}^{GP} + v · _{1}p_{x+t} · r_{t+1}^{GP} + v² · _{2}p_{x+t} · r_{t+2}^{GP} + ···
   NP_{t} = PB_{t} = PB_{0} · r_{t}^{GP}

[235] Q: What is the full preliminary term (FPT) reserve method?
A: FPT = Modified NLP with formulaic expense allowance
   _{t}V_{x}^{FPT} = _{t}V_{x}^{NLP} − _{t}VE_{x} = PVFB_{t} − (PVPB_{t} + PVPE_{t})
   _{t}VE_{x} = PVPE_{t} = PE_{0} × ä_{x+t}
   PE_{0} = EA_{x} / ä_{x}
   EA_{x} = NP_{1} − c_{x} = (PVFB_{1} / ä_{x+1}) − c_{x}
   c_{x} = v · q_{x} · DB = first-year COI
   NP_{t} = c_{x} for t = 0 (α)
   NP_{t} = PB_{t} + PE_{t} = (PVFB_{1} / ä_{x+1}) · r_{t}^{GP} for t ≥ 1 (β)

[236] Q: What is the commissioner’s reserve valuation method (CRVM)?
A: • Modified reserve where EA is smaller of:
   1. EA under FPT for contract
   2. EA under FPT assuming 20-pay contract
   • Smallest reserves allowed by SVL
   • _{t}V_{x}^{CRVM} = _{t}V_{x}^{FPT} unless m < 20 years

[237] Q: Formulas for mean and mid-terminal reserves?
A: Mean Reserve – DPA = Mid-Terminal Reserve + UPL
   InterpMeanV = (1 − h)(_{t-1}V_{x} + NP_{t}) + h(_{t}V_{x})
   MeanV = [(_{t-1}V_{x} + NP_{t}) + (_{t}V_{x})] / 2  if h = 0.5 (common)
   DPA = Σ Modal NPs due between val date and next anniversary
   InterpMidV = (1 − h)(_{t-1}V_{x}) + h(_{t}V_{x})
   MidV = (_{t-1}V_{x} + _{t}V_{x}) / 2  if h = 0.5 (common)
   UPL = (# months until next premium / # months between premium payment) × Modal NP
   DPA and UPL held separately in stat financial statements

[238] Q: List types of continuous reserves.
A: 1. Semicontinuous
     ◦ DB paid at moment of death
     ◦ Premiums paid BOY
   2. Fully Continuous
     ◦ DB paid at moment of death
     ◦ NP paid continuously throughout year
     ◦ UPL for NPs paid beyond val date
   3. Discounted Continuous
     ◦ Semicontinuous
     ◦ Assumes refund of unearned premium at death
     ◦ Requires DPA

[239] Q: What is the immediate payment of claims reserve (IPCR)?
A: Required for reserve methods assuming curtate death benefits
   • Reality: DBs paid closer to time of death
   IPCR_{t} =
     (i/3)PVFB_{x+t}   if DB paid without interest
     (i/2)PVFB_{x+t}   if DB paid with interest
   Based on "death portion of reserve"

C.1.5 — Fundamentals of the PBA to Statutory Reserves  (C.1 US Statutory Valuation)
----------------------------------------------------------------------

[240] Q: List problems with CRVM addressed by principles-based approach.
A: • Rules-based and highly conservative assumptions
     ◦ Prescribed mortality and expense allowance
     ◦ Interest rate based on formula
     ◦ No explicit lapse assumption
   • Reserves don’t vary with risk
     ◦ Same reserve for super preferred vs. standard class
   • CRVM unchanged for decades despite product innovation
   • Reg XXX and AXXX →excessive reserves for term and ULSG

[241] Q: What are the overarching principles of PBRs?
A: Overarching Principles
   1. Use conservatism reflecting:
     ◦ Unfavorable events if reasonable probability
     ◦ Tail risk (if significant)
   2. Consistency with company’s risk management
   3. Assumptions: prescribed and non-prescribed
     ◦ Non-prescribed = blend of company and industry experience
   4. Margins for uncertainty
     ◦ Greater uncertainty →larger margin →larger reserve
   Also:
   1. Ensure solvency
   2. NOT be excessive
   3. Reflect contracts’ risk

[242] Q: List Valuation Manual objectives.
A: 1. Consolidate min reserve requirements into one document
   2. Promote uniformity among state valuation requirements
   3. Provide efficient, consistent & timely process to update requirements
   4. Mandate/facilitate specific reporting of experience data
   5. Enhance compliance with SVL revisions

[243] Q: Outline the steps for determining VM-20 minimum reserve.
A: 1. Calculate net premium reserve (NPR) floor (ALWAYS for all policies)
     ◦ Formulaic, prescribed assumptions (similar to CRVM)
   2. Calculate PBRs: deterministic and/or stochastic (depends on exclusion tests)
     ◦ Cash flow projection model
     ◦ Assumptions = blend of company and industry experience
     ◦ Determine in aggregate or by policy group, then allocate
     ◦ Starting asset collar = 98% to max(NPR, 102%) of final reserve
     ◦ Time period: until reserve no longer changes materially
   3. Excess Reserve = max[DR, SR] − NPR
     ◦ For DR and SR, add ± allocated pre-tax IMR
     ◦ Floored at 0
   4. Min Reserve = NPR + max[0, max(DR, SR) − (NPR − DPA)]
   5. Aggregate min reserve = sum of min reserve for each product group
     ◦ Term, ULSG, other life

[244] Q: Steps for determining eligibility for exclusion tests?
A: 1. Classify policies: term, ULSG, other life
   2. Does group use clearly defined hedging strategy?
     ◦ If YES: Always do stochastic and deterministic (done)
     ◦ Else →#3
   3. Decide if want stochastic exclusion test
     ◦ Any group: pass →no stochastic reserve required
   4. Other life only: decide if want deterministic exclusion test
     ◦ Only eligible if did stochastic exclusion test first
     ◦ Pass →no deterministic reserve required
     ◦ Term and ULSG must ALWAYS calculate deterministic reserves

[245] Q: What is the net premium reserve methodology?
A: NPR = PV(Benefits) − PV(Net Premiums)
   Floored by GREATER of:
   1. COI through next paid-to date
   2. Cash value floor
   Differences vs. CRVM
   • Different EA
   • Prescribed lapses for term and ULSG
   • Prescribed mortality can be adjusted
   Similarities with CRVM
   • Prescribed CSO mortality
   • Same interest rates
   • Same gross premiums

[246] Q: What are the NPR’s objectives?
A: • Floor gives uniform baseline for industry
   • Easier to audit than DR or SR
   • Only reserve required if pass both exclusion tests
   • Can be used as allocation basis for DR/SR results

[247] Q: What is the deterministic PBR methodology?
A: Determined in aggregate using single economic scenario
   1. PV of Cash Flow Approach
   APV(Ben, Exp & Related) − APV(Prems & Related)
     ◦ APV(Ben, Exp & Related) includes existing PLs & SA AV
     ◦ APV(Prems & Related) includes PL, reins, & derivative CFs
   Discount rates vary by model segment (driven by assets in segment)
   2. Direct Iteration Approach
     ◦ DR = starting asset amount that matures all future benefits & expenses
     ◦ Project all CFs iteratively until balance found

[248] Q: What is the stochastic PBR methodology?
A: Determined in aggregate using same CFs as deterministic
   1. Project model segment CFs using stochastically generated scenarios
   2. PV negative of projected statement value of assets
     ◦ Discount rates = 1.05 × 1-year treasury scenario rates
   3. Sum amounts in (2) across all model segments at end/beginning of each
   projection year
   4. Scenario reserve = sum of starting assets across all segments + max of values
   above
   5. Stochastic reserve = CTE 70 of scenario reserve distribution

[249] Q: General assumption considerations for deterministic and stochastic PBRs?
A: • Most assumptions = blend of company and industry experience
     ◦ Higher company data credibility →closer to company experience
     ◦ ”Prudent estimate”
   • Prescribed assumptions limited to:
     ◦ Interest rate movements (DR only)
     ◦ Equity performance (DR only)
     ◦ Spreads over treasuries on reinvestment
     ◦ Definition of industry mortality table and grading method
     ◦ ULSG: definition of industry lapse table
   • Margins for adverse deviation and estimate error (non-stochastic
   assumptions)
     ◦ Greater uncertainty →larger margin →larger reserve
     ◦ Higher margin if credibility, data quality, assumption reliability are low

[250] Q: VM-20 mortality assumptions?
A: • VM-20 prescribes company/industry blending table
     ◦ Varies with credibility and ”sufficient data period”
   • VM-20 prescribed margins vary by attained age
     ◦ Company margins also vary with credibility
   • Mortality improvement assumptions:
     ◦ Between experience date and valuation date:
     ◦ Improve company exp using supported company-specific factors
     ◦ Improve industry exp using SOA-published rates
     ◦ After valuation date (future mortality):
     ◦ Use VM-20 prescribed improvement rates

[251] Q: VM-20 policyholder behavior assumptions?
A: PH behavior risk factors:
   • Premium payment patterns and premium persistency
   • Partial and full withdrawals (surrenders)
   • Allocation between investment options
   • Other PH benefit options
   VM-20 requirements:
   • Use experience from same block being modeled; else similar block
   • Include margin: lower credibility →more conservative assumption
   • Dynamic assumptions consistent with economic scenario

[252] Q: VM-20 expense assumptions?
A: Same assumption for DR and SR (except inflation effects)
   Considerations:
   • Spread capital expenditures (IT, etc.)
   • Assume going concern
   • Expense basis
     ◦ Align with actual expense
     ◦ Consistent between new and in-force
   • Reflect inflation
   • NO future expense improvements
   • NO taxes
   • Consistency with related assumptions
   Expense types to include:
   1. Direct + indirect + overhead
   2. Acquisition expenses
   3. Expected future non-recurring
   4. Efficiencies from
   combinations/mergers
   Use FULLY allocated expenses

[253] Q: Compare reinsurance: rules-based vs. principles-based.
A: Rules-Based
   • Mirror-image reserves (usually)
   • Ceding company reserve credit = reinsurer’s assumed reserve
   Principles-Based
   • Mirror-image reserves not required (or likely)
   • Ceding company and reinsurer use own assumptions
   • Reins Reserve Credit = Pre-Reins Min Reserve − Post-Reins Min Reserve
     ◦ Pre-reins run: follow VM-20 assuming reins didn’t exist
     ◦ 98% to 102% collar for starting assets does not apply
   • Reins features like stop-loss may require additional stochastic analysis
   • If YRT rates non-guaranteed: reserve credit based on pre-PBR approach

[254] Q: Overview of asset modeling framework under VM-20?
A: Existing Assets = SA Assets + PLs + Derivatives + GA Assets
   • ”Solve” for GA Asset balance so total = 98% to 102% of reported reserve
   • Requires iterations
   Reinvestment Assets
   • VM-20 prescribes yield spread
   • Reflect investment policies of company
     ◦ Should not produce lower reserve than ”alternative investment strategy”
     ◦ VM-20 prescribes 50/50 mix of A and AA public non-callable corporate bonds
   Reflect default costs on all modeled assets

[255] Q: List the criteria for a Clearly Defined Hedging Strategy (CDHS).
A: 1. Risks being hedged: cash flow, fee income, interest credits, etc.
   2. Hedging objectives
   3. Material risks not hedged: mortality, withdrawal, etc.
   4. Financial instruments used
   5. Trading rules and tolerances
   6. Metrics and frequency for effectiveness measurement
   7. Conditions and duration for non-hedging
   8. Responsible group or area
   9. Identified basis, gap, or assumption risks
   10. Ineffectiveness circumstances

[256] Q: Overview of economic assumptions under VM-20?
A: • Based on NAIC’s Generator of Economic Scenarios (GOES)
   • GOES generates Treasury rates and S&P 500 total returns
     ◦ Based on prescribed parameters (same for all companies)
   • GOES can generate up to 10,000 scenarios
     ◦ Companies may use subset for SR
     ◦ Subset use = simplification technique (must not materially understate reserves)
   • DR scenario is No. 12 from stochastic exclusion test set

[257] Q: Outline the key roles and responsibilities for corporate governance under VM-20?
A: 1. Board of directors
     ◦ Oversee management’s process to correct material control weaknesses
     ◦ Oversee PBR infrastructure / consistency with company’s risk management
     ◦ Interacts with senior management
     ◦ Document reviews and actions
   2. Senior management
     ◦ Establish PBR infrastructure and modeling resources
     ◦ Review PBR assumptions, methods, and models
     ◦ Review significant/unusual issues with PBR results
     ◦ Adopt internal VM compliance controls
   3. Qualified actuary(ies)
     ◦ Must meet Academy’s qualification standards
     ◦ Oversees PBR calculation
     ◦ Develops internal standards for actuarial practices, controls, documentation
     ◦ Summary report to board and senior management

     ◦ Issue VM certifications but not necessarily opinion on reserve adequacy

[258] Q: Value of future experience submissions?
A: • Helps regulators and reviewers
     ◦ Easier to monitor, perform reasonableness checks, develop thresholds
   • Facilitates credibility-based blends
   • Provides database of inter-company experience

[259] Q: Two PBR support components implemented by NAIC?

[260] Q: GAAP definition, primary audience, and information sources?
A: GAAP = conventions, rules, and procedures defining accepted accounting practices
   • Provides transparency to capital providers and related audiences
   • Capital providers = stockholders and policyholders
     ◦ Stock companies owned by stockholders
     ◦ Mutual companies owned by policyholders
   • Financial statements = primary GAAP information source
     ◦ Income statement, balance sheet, etc.
   • Other sources:
     ◦ Conference calls with analysts
     ◦ Responses to rule-makers’ queries
     ◦ Supplementary investor information
     ◦ Press releases

[261] Q: Purpose of GAAP financial statements?
A: • Summarizes enterprise’s assets, liabilities, and transaction effects on earnings
   • Expressed in single, uniform currency unadjusted for inflation
     ◦ Non-monetary info converted to monetary
   • Measures single entity, not entire industry
     ◦ Subsidiaries consolidated into one set of statements
   • Reportable segments based on operating segments
     ◦ Operating segment = relatively homogenous segment per company’s
   decision-makers
     ◦ May differ company to company
   • Measured over clearly defined period (quarterly or annually)

[262] Q: List users of financial statements.
A: • Capital providers (debt and equity)
     ◦ Financial institutions
     ◦ Investment banks
     ◦ Private lenders
     ◦ Commercial banks
     ◦ Individual investors
   • Brokers and dealers in financial instruments issued by enterprise
   • Expert advisors to users
     ◦ Attorneys, actuaries, accountants
   • Policyholders or other stakeholders

[263] Q: List parties with GAAP accounting oversight roles.
A: • Management responsibilities:
     ◦ Fair presentation per GAAP
     ◦ Complete financial reporting process
     ◦ Explain matters not evident in numerical info
   • Auditors: opine on fairness of presentation and internal control effectiveness
     ◦ Independent external auditors – not employees (hired by/report to Board)
     ◦ Opinions based on testing and COSO criteria
     ◦ Internal auditors – can be employees or contracted third parties
   • Other regulatory bodies: SEC, IRS, state insurance departments
   • Users acting on behalf of others: stock analysts, rating agencies

[264] Q: Standard-setters for GAAP accounting?
A: • Financial Accounting Standards Board (FASB)
     ◦ Establishes uniform standards for financial statement contents
     ◦ Maintains Accounting Standards Codification (ASC)
     ◦ Issues Statements of Financial Accounting Concepts (SFACs)
     ◦ Standardization reduces need for individual specific info requests
   • AICPA and PCAOB: issue standards for accountants and auditors
     ◦ AICPA: American Institute of Certified Public Accountants
     ◦ PCAOB: Public Company Accounting Oversight Board
   • SEC: ultimate regulator of US public companies
     ◦ Can de-list or halt stock trading
     ◦ Issues pronouncements: manuals, staff bulletins, enforcement releases

[265] Q: Meaning and importance of relevance and reliability for GAAP?
A: Financial statement info must be relevant and reliable
   • Relevant info must be timely
     ◦ Perfect info not valuable if too late
   • Relevant = consistent and comparable
     ◦ Consistent = based on similar/identical measures from prior periods
     ◦ Comparable = equivalent to measures used in other enterprises
     ◦ Should be comparable across time periods
     ◦ Prior results restated when errors surface
     ◦ Some standards entity-specific (e.g. ASC 944 for insurers only)
   • Relevant = provides appropriate detail
     ◦ Adequate detail informs without confusing
   • Reliable = materially accurate and verifiable
   • Reliability also implies ”free of bias”

[266] Q: Key relationships between GAAP balance sheet and comprehensive income?
A: Balance Sheet: Assets = Liabilities + Equity
   • Equity = Assets − Liabilities
   • Equity = Paid-In Stock + Paid-In Additional Capital + Retained Earnings
   • EOY Equity = BOY Equity + Comprehensive Income + Owner Transactions
     ◦ Owner Transactions = Capital Contributions − Dividends
   Comprehensive Income = Net Income + Other Comprehensive Income (OCI)
   • Income Statement: Net Income = Revenues − Expenses
     ◦ Revenue = Premiums + Fees + InvInc + Realized Gains
     ◦ Expenses = Benefits + Expenses − Realized Losses
   • OCI = equity changes from gains/losses not central to operations
     ◦ Changes in unrealized gains/losses on AFS assets
     ◦ Shadow adjustments
     ◦ MRB FV changes due to own credit risk

[267] Q: Define asset, liability, and equity per SFAC No. 8 (CON8 Ch4).
A: SFAC No. 8 (”CON8 Ch4”) defines balance sheet elements:
   • Asset = present right of entity to economic benefit
     ◦ Restricts others’ access to asset
     ◦ Provisions for losses required if probable future benefit Ó
     ◦ Includes tangible (e.g. financial instruments) and intangible (e.g. goodwill)
   • Liability = present obligation of entity to transfer economic benefit
     ◦ ASC 450 requires liabilities for contingent events that are probable and reasonably
   estimable
   • Equity = residual interest in entity’s assets after deducting liabilities
     ◦ Aka ”net assets”

[268] Q: SFAC No. 5 criteria for recognition and measurement?
A: 1. Definition: asset, liability, revenue, or expense
   2. Measurability: must have quantifiable attribute with sufficient reliability
     ◦ Historical cost: easy via company records; adjust for depreciation
     ◦ Fair value: from active secondary markets or expert appraisals
     ◦ Present value: discount CFs at rate that can vary
     ◦ Least preferable, but necessary for most ILA products
   Always use uniform monetary units unadjusted for inflation
   3. Relevance: consistent, comparable, meaningful in users’ decision-making
   4. Reliability: accurate, verifiable, free of bias

[269] Q: Compare cash basis vs. accrual accounting.
A: • Cash basis is simplest
     ◦ Record entries only when cash changes hands
     ◦ Not appropriate for incomplete or long-term transactions (like life insurance)
   • Accrual alters timing of when cash transactions are recognized
     ◦ Goal: earnings emerge proportionally to ”completeness” of earnings process
     ◦ Examples: reserves, DAC, unearned premium liabilities

[270] Q: What is the double-entry accounting system?
A: GAAP predicated on double-entry accounting
   Financial Statement
   Account Type
   Debit
   Credit
   Revenue
   –
   +
   Income Statement
   Expense
   +
   –
   Balance Sheet
   Asset
   +
   –
   Liability or Equity
   –
   +
   This system ensures A = L + E always holds
   • Debits = ↑assets and expenses
   • Credits = ↑liabilities, equity, and revenue
   • Credits and debits always balance in any period

[271] Q: Considerations for materiality in GAAP financial statements?
A: Key tradeoff: approximately correct, timely info > more accurate info too late
   Estimates should be precise enough for informed decisions
   • Some users require more precision than others
   • Material errors should be corrected
   • Immaterial imprecision doesn’t need correction
   • ”Immaterial” = impact won’t influence decision-making
     ◦ Requires judgment

[272] Q: List key disclosures required under GAAP.
A: • Footnotes and narrative disclosures supplement primary financial statements
     ◦ E.g. describe future liability not currently quantifiable
   • SEC Regulation S-K – market risk exposure impact on financial statements
     ◦ Quantitative disclosures: market risk, earnings sensitivity
     ◦ Qualitative info on exposures
   • Disclosures for accounting principle changes
     ◦ ASC 250: accounting changes and error corrections
     ◦ ASC 855: significant events between period end and statement release
   • Additional GAAP disclosures:
     ◦ Company and product description
     ◦ Summary of significant accounting policies
     ◦ Details of invested assets
     ◦ Details of reserves and accounting assets

C.2.2–C.2.3 — Ch. 5: The Liability for Future Policy Benefits  (C.2 US GAAP and IFRS 17 Valuation)

[273] Q: List acquisition costs capitalized in DAC (traditional & LP).
A: Deferrable = incremental direct costs of successful acquisition:
   • Employee comp tied to selling, UW, issue, processing issuedcontracts
   • Other costs not incurred if the contract were not acquired
   • Commissions > ultimate (renewal) level
   • LP: all premium-based commissions (ultimate level = 0)
   • Certain direct marketing costs
   Expense all else as incurred (overhead, general advertising, unsuccessful efforts)
   Ignore premium taxes & FIT in the LFPB and DAC (exception: premium taxes on LP
   contracts may be deferrable)

[274] Q: Formulas for grouped DAC amortization and rollforward?
A: AmortRatio_{t} = (BOY DAC_{t} + Cap_{t}) / Σ(s≥t) expected BOYIF_{s}
   Amort_{t} = AmortRatio_{t} × BOYIF_{t}
   EOY DAC_{t} = BOY DAC_{t} + Cap_{t} − Amort_{t} − True-up_{t}
   • Yr 1: Amort_{1} = Deferrable × BOYIF_{1} / ΣBOYIF
   • Constant-level basis: amount/units inforce (initial DB if non-level)
   • No interest accretion on DAC

[275] Q: Describe the DAC experience true-up (unlocking).
A: True-up_{t} = DAC_{t} × (expected BOYIF_{t} − actual BOYIF_{t}) / expected BOYIF_{t}
   • Actual IF < expected → write DAC down (extra amortization)
   • One-sided: no adjustment if more persist than expected
   • Revise future amort prospectively over the new inforce projection
   • True-up is just extra amort; total amort + true-ups = deferrable

[276] Q: Considerations for cohort grouping and unit of account?
A: Contract grouping necessary to calculate NPR
   • NPR based on group experience/assumptions
   • Reserves can still be calculated at contract level
   Groups must consist of same cohort (issued within 1 year)
   Contracts in group should be similar by:
   • Type (e.g. don’t mix term and WL)
   • Contract length
   • Premium paying period (don’t mix LP and non-LP)
   • Rating class
   • Issue age (possibly banded)
   • Face amount band
   More (smaller) groups →↑assumption precision, BUT:
   • Credibility ↓
   • NPR more likely to be capped

[277] Q: LFPB for traditional nonpar insurance?
A: NPR_{t} = \frac{PV_{0}(Actual Benefits Through t) + PV_{0}(Expected Benefits After t)}{PV_{0}(Actual GPs Through t) + PV_{0}(Expected GPs After t)}
   LFPB_{t} = PVFB_{t} − NPR_{t} × PVGP_{t}
   = (LFPB_{t-1} + NPR_{t} × GP_{t})(1 + i) − Ben_{t}
   = LFPB_{t-1} + NP_{t} + Accretion_{t} − Ben_{t}
   • NPR calculated at group level and capped at 100%
   • "Benefits" = DBs, maturities, CSV, and claim/termination-related expenses (LAE)
   • CF assumptions = best estimates without PADs revised at least annually
   • Discount rate = upper-medium grade yields (A-rated)
     ◦ Maximize relevant observable inputs; minimize unobservable
     ◦ Must reflect duration characteristics of liabilities
     ◦ Can be curve or blended single rate
     ◦ Measure LFPB at original (accretion rate) and current DR
   • LFPB can’t be negative at group level: floor at 0

[278] Q: Rules for truing up NPR for traditional long-duration insurance?
A: • CF assumptions not locked in at issue
   • Recalculate (”true-up”) NPR from issue (at least annually)
   NPR_{t} = \frac{PV_{0}(Actual Benefits Through t) + PV_{0}(Expected Benefits After t)}{PV_{0}(Actual GPs Through t) + PV_{0}(Expected GPs After t)}
     ◦ Replace estimated CFs with actuals
     ◦ Must revise/re-confirm assumptions when CFs updated
     ◦ Use original discount rate (”accretion interest rate”)
   • NPR can be updated more frequently than annually (e.g. quarterly)
     ◦ Review at same time each year
     ◦ Interim reviews may be needed:
     ◦ Special situations: pandemics, medical advances, competitive changes
     ◦ Other significant developments
     ◦ Must update all CF components
     ◦ Must address all assumptions (exception: LAE can be locked-in)

[279] Q: How to quantify and present remeasurement gains/losses?
A: ASC 944 requires separate remeasurement loss/gain line on income statement
   Total effect on income when benefits deviate from expected:
   Actual Benefits Incurred
   + Remeasurement Loss ( = Revised BOP LFPB − Original BOP LFPB)
   + Change in Reserve ( = EOP LFPB − Revised BOP LFPB)
   = Total Benefit Expense
   Calculate all items above using at-inception DR (accretion interest rate)

[280] Q: How to present GAAP net income on income statement and in SOE format for
traditional nonpar?
A: GAAP net income =
   GAAP Revenue = Gross Premiums + Net Investment Income
   − GAAP Expenses = DBs + SBs + IncrLFPB + Non-Def Exp + DAC Amort
   SOE Format:
   (+) Premium Load: GP − NP
   (+) Interest Spread: NII − Accretion
   (−) Acquisition Costs: DAC Amort + Non-Def AcqCost
   (−) All Other Expenses: MaintExp + RenewalComm + PremTax + Overhead
   (−) Benefit Deviation: Actual Benefits − Expected Benefits
   (+) Other Reserve Unlock: IncrLFPB Before NPR Unlock − IncrLFPB After NPR Unlock
        + Int Spread Before NPR Unlock − Int Spread After NPR Unlock
        + Prem Load Before NPR Unlock − Prem Load After NPR Unlock
   = GAAP Net Income
   DAC is non-interest-bearing: large early DAC ↓ invested assets → ↓ early interest spread → contributes
   to yr-1 loss (amort largest in yr 1)

[281] Q: ASOP 10 guidance for setting best estimate assumptions?
A: • Use best estimates without conservatism or PADs
   • Best estimate = ”most likely, average, or central” outcome
     ◦ Corresponds to mode, mean, or median of probability distribution
     ◦ Other interpretations possible
   • Considerations when selecting best estimates:
     ◦ Characteristics/magnitude of company’s business
     ◦ Company maturity and growth rate
     ◦ Company’s prior experience and trends
     ◦ Medical, economic, social, technological developments
     ◦ Actual recent company experience (if relevant and credible)
     ◦ Relevant industry data to supplement company experience
     ◦ ASOP 23 for data quality issues

[282] Q: Considerations for mortality and lapse assumptions for traditional nonpar?
A: Mortality
   • Typically based on company experience studies
   • New products may require industry experience, adjusted as needed
   • Reflect marketing and UW practices
   • Reflect historical mortality improvement if actuary believes it will continue
   • Can be dependent on lapses (anti-selection)
   Lapse rates
   • Account for face, issue year/age, duration, premium paying period/frequency
   • Composite rates should account for current and future business mix

[283] Q: Assumption considerations for non-fixed premiums?
A: • Most nonpar trad contracts have fixed premiums
     ◦ Fixed premiums = contract parameter, not assumption
   • Examples with adjustable premiums:
     ◦ Many term policies: premiums adjustable up to contract maximum
     ◦ Indeterminate premium contracts: premiums reset prospectively if assumptions
   change
   • Adjustable premiums require best estimate assumption considering:
     ◦ Current premium levels
     ◦ Historical premium patterns
     ◦ Expectations of future increases/decreases
     ◦ Contractually guaranteed maximum premiums
   • Include actual premiums when unlocking NPR
   • Indeterminate premium policies are UL-type if satisfying 1 of:
     ◦ Stated account balance with interest credits/charges not fixed/guaranteed
     ◦ Interest rates and market conditions mainly drive changes in contract elements

[284] Q: Treatment of coupons, dividends, and conversion privileges for traditional nonpar?
A: • LFPB should reflect coupons, endowments, and conversion privileges
   • Dividend-paying policies not following contribution principle:
     ◦ Dividend revisions may follow different schedule than other assumption revisions
     ◦ Dividend scale slope impacts LFPB
     ◦ May create anomalies when dividend elements don’t align with current experience
     ◦ E.g. dividend investment return assumption may not match current returns
     ◦ PH dividend option utilization usually not reflected in LFPB
     ◦ Assume dividends always paid in cash
   • If shareholder dividends restricted, establish UPPEA
     ◦ Do not include PH dividends in LFPB when using UPPEA

[285] Q: Accounting for riders on traditional nonpar?
A: If rider can’t exist standalone (e.g. premium waiver, LTCI/CI combo):
   • Treat base + rider as single unit
   • Include rider charges and benefits in LFPB
   • Include deferrable acquisition costs in DAC
   • Riders often profitable →will lower NPR, all else equal
   Separate riders insuring risk other than base contract
   • Examples: disability income riders, spouse term riders
   • Assumptions should be internally consistent with base
   • In some situations, insurers set rider GAAP reserve = stat reserve
     ◦ Appropriate if not materially different

[286] Q: GAAP valuation approaches for disability waiver riders?
A: If disability not occurred:
   1. Gross approach – value rider separately from base
     ◦ Reserve = PV expected waived premiums − NPR × PV expected rider charges
   2. Net approach – integrate rider with base
     ◦ Reduce base contract premiums for expected waived amounts
   Mortality assumption approaches:
   • Could use higher mortality for contracts with active disability
   • Could use averaged mortality assumption for all contracts (disabled or not)
   • Consider credibility/availability of disabled life mortality data

[287] Q: Rules for updating discount rates on traditional nonpar?
A: Update every reporting period (even if not addressing CF assumptions)
   Change in LFPB from new discount rates→ OCI
   OCI_{t} = AOCI_{t} − AOCI_{t-1}
   AOCI_{t} = accumulated OCI at time t
   = LFPB_{t} based on original DR − LFPB_{t} based on current DR
   Do NOT recalculate NPR using current DR
   • Always use NPR from most recent CF experience/assumption unlocking
   OCI accounting reduces GAAP equity volatility
   • LFPB on balance sheet based on current discount rates
   • When interest rates↑: AFS asset FV and LFPB ”FV”↓
   • Reductions in AFS asset FV → negative OCI
   • Reductions in LFPB ”FV”→ positive OCI

[288] Q: Compare 3 approaches for locked-in interest accretion when using discount rate curve.
A: 3 approaches for locking in curve of accretion rates:
   1. Spot rate – use original spot rates for each CF through time
   2. Forward rate – use implied period-specific forward rates
   3. Effective yield – use IRR based on spot curve
   Differences:
   • Effective yield may exceed long-term spot rates if premiums payable over multiple
   years and yield curve upward sloping
     ◦ From ”leveraging effect”: premiums > benefits in early years, then reverses
     ◦ Can cause spot rate accretion > effective yield method
   • Spot rate interest accretion may better match investment income
   • Forward rate usually produces least accretion in early years
     ◦ Accretion in later years very high (may exceed investment income)
     ◦ Also → lower early reserves
     ◦ Forward curves tend to dip/spike→ affects interest accretion

[289] Q: Practical challenges and solutions for multiple issue dates within cohort?
A: Locked-in DRs must reflect rate environment when contracts sold
   • Challenge: interest rates vary throughout issue year
   • May prevent using single yield curve at BOY/MOY/EOY
   Possible solutions
   • Use average curve
     ◦ Could be weighted (e.g. by premium) or simple
     ◦ Could average only key dates (e.g. month-ends)
   • Use provisional yield curve(s)
     ◦ Lock in final yield curve(s) at EOY
   • Set DRs by sub-cohorts (e.g. monthly, quarterly)
     ◦ Avoids provisional yield curves
     ◦ NPR and cap still set at cohort level

[290] Q: How to apply ASC 820 guidance for unobservable discount rates?
A: Follow ASC 820 level 3 measurement:
   • Reflect market participants’ assumptions to price assets/liabilities, including risk
   • Reflect risk in both valuation technique and inputs
   • Reduce DRs by margin to reflect significant uncertainty from lack of market activity
   • Insurer’s own data should be adjusted to reflect market participant’s perspective
     ◦ Doesn’t need to be exhaustive effort
   Approaches when no A-rated rates for foreign currency:
   • Use highest available rating instead
   • Subtract assumed spread from highest rated rates

[291] Q: Techniques for estimating long-dated interest rates?
A: 1. Interpolate between last observable rate and assumed LT rate
   2. Determine forward rate from last observable rate and extrapolate
   3. Add A-rated spread for shorter rates to longer risk-free rates
     ◦ Often risk-free rate curve extends beyond A-rated curve
     ◦ Assume A-rated spreads over risk-free rates constant
     ◦ Can augment either other method

[292] Q: Nature of limited-pay contracts and how they’re accounted for?
A: LP contracts have premium payment periods < coverage period
   • Single-pay, 10-pay, 20-pay, payments to age 65, etc.
   • Also includes life payout annuities (usually single premium)
   • Problem: high profit during premium period, then losses
   Solution: defer GP in excess of NP
   • Life insurance: DPL amortized vs. discounted inforce
     ◦ If experience = assumptions, profits emerge as level % of inforce
   • Payout annuities: DPL amortized vs. PV future benefits
   • Use same CF assumptions and accretion rates as LFPB
     ◦ Update experience/assumptions when LFPB updated
     ◦ Do not update discount rate

[293] Q: How to determine DPL for limited-pay traditional nonpar?
A: 1. Determine annual profit to defer
   Profit_{t} = (1 − NPR) × GP_{t}
   2. Calculate DPL as constant % of inforce
   DPL% = PV_{0}(Profit) / PV_{0}(BOYIF*)
   DPL_{t} = DPL% × PV_{t}(BOYIF*) − PV_{t}(Profit)
   = (DPL_{t-1} + Profit_{t}) × (1 + i) − DPL% × BOYIF*_{t-1}
   Discount BOYIF* as of EOY
   3. Include ↑ in DPL on income statement as expense
     ◦ As profit capitalized, DPL ↑ to offset and defer it
     ◦ DPL ↓ each period after premiums end releasing profit into income

[294] Q: Define restricted dividends.
A: Shareholder dividends may be restricted by law/charter/policy form
   • Protects PHs’ share of profits (future PH dividends)
   • PH surplus share excluded from stockholder equity
   When restricted dividends exist, insurer must establish UPPEA
   • UPPEA = deferred surplus (profits) paid as future PH dividends
   • Smooths net income →more even stream of shareholder dividends
   • Do not include dividends in LFPB

[295] Q: How to calculate UPPEA?
A: Assuming shareholder dividends restricted to 10% of surplus:
   1. Project GAAP income statement
   2. Calculate GAAP net income ignoring PH dividends
   3. Calculate running total of GAAP surplus before PH dividends
   CumSurplus_{t} = CumSurplus_{t-1} + NetIncomeBefore_{t}
   4. Calculate running total of PH dividends paid
   5. UPPEA_{t} = 0.90 × CumSurplus_{t} − CumPHDivs_{t}
   6. Calculate final GAAP net income:
   NetIncome_{t} = NetIncomeBefore_{t} − PHDivs_{t} − ∆UPPEA_{t}
   Shadow UPPEA may be needed to offset OCI from AFS assets
   • Changes in shadow UPPEA→ OCI

[296] Q: Examples and characteristics of claims liabilities?
A: Claims liabilities established for:
   • Pending death claims
   • Resisted claims
   • Incurred but not reported (IBNR)
   Characteristics:
   • Usually not discounted (paid out soon)
   • Less significant for life insurance (claims paid quickly)
   • IBNR can be shown separately on balance sheet or included with LFPB
   • Allocate change in total IBNR to cohorts using ”rational allocation” method
     ◦ Liabilities for pending/resisted claims tied to original cohort more easily

[297] Q: Accounting for premium accruals?
A: Reported separately on GAAP balance sheet:
   • Advance premium liability – offsets premiums paid in advance
   • Receivable asset for due/unpaid premiums – ↑income in place of premiums
   Netted against or added GAAP reserve (don’t report separately):
   • DPA for non-annual modes (relevant to mean reserves)
   • UPL (relevant to midterminal reserves)

[298] Q: Challenges in explaining change in LFPB to others?
A: • New policies more sensitive (more affected by PV effects)
     ◦ Opposite for older policies (↓future CFs remaining)
   • Reserve sensitivity ↑with NPR (max sensitivity at 100% cap)
   • Many experience/assumption changes impact both benefits and premiums
   • Deviations in current experience also impact future experience
   • If experience only updated annually, impact affected by deviations in prior periods
   • 1 − NPR = insurer’s best estimate profit margin (excluding expenses)
     ◦ Changes every time NPR changes

[299] Q: Considerations for interim reporting?
A: Interim reporting – many insurers report GAAP earnings quarterly
   • Will ↑volatility for insurers not updating NPR each quarter
   • Prior quarterly results can’t be restated at EOY (must stand on their own)
   • Will likely cause results to differ from annual-only reporting

[300] Q: Disaggregated rollforward disclosure requirement for traditional nonpar?
A: Key disclosure: YTD disaggregated tabular rollforward of GAAP reserve from
   beginning to ending balance
   • Must be gross of reinsurance recoverables and include any DPL
   • Show future NPs and expected benefits separately
   • Aggregation level should align with insurer’s business
     ◦ Separate balances by geography, LOB, distribution channel, etc.
   Examples of potentially relevant items:
   • Newly issued contracts
   • Accrued interest and NPs collected
   • Benefit and expense payments
   • Derecognition of contracts due to death or lapse
   • Details around true-ups, unlocking, discount rate updates, etc.

C.2.4 — Insurance Contracts First Impressions (IFRS 17)  (C.2 US GAAP and IFRS 17 Valuation)
----------------------------------------------------------------------

[301] Q: IFRS 17 scope, general measurement model, and effective date?
A: Scope: contracts meeting IFRS 17 definition of insurance contract
   General measurement model
   • Liability = Fulfillment Cash Flows (FCF) + Contractual Service Margin (CSM)
   • FCF = PV expected CFs + risk adjustment for non-financial risk
   • CSM = unearned profit released as insurer provides services
   • Assumptions each reporting date = current estimates
   • Simplifications/modifications to GMM:
     ◦ PAA for short-duration coverages
     ◦ VFA for investment contracts with discretionary participation features
     ◦ Modified GMM for reinsurance contracts held
   Effective date: January 1, 2023
   • Early adoption concurrent with IFRS 9 (financial instruments) allowed

[302] Q: IFRS 17 presentation requirements?
A: • Separate P&L into:
   1. Insurance service result (earnings from insurance components)
   2. Insurance finance income/expense (earnings from investment components)
     ◦ Can be further disaggregated between P&L and OCI
   • Insurance revenue reflects changes in liability for future services
     ◦ Excludes investment components and premium

[303] Q: How will IFRS 17 impact how insurers’ earnings are compared and measured?
A: • ↑global comparability/transparency of insurers’ financial health
   • Greater volatility in financial results and equity
     ◦ Key driver: use of current market discount rates
     ◦ ↑visibility of economic mismatches between assets/liabilities
     ◦ Product design and/or investment allocation may change in response
   • Key financial metrics will change
     ◦ Premium volumes no longer key earnings driver
     ◦ Profit emergence may change significantly
   • Clearer earnings picture: separation of financial vs. insurance drivers
   • Life vs. non-life sector impacts
     ◦ Bigger impact for life insurers:
     ◦ Significant change: No more locked-in assumptions
     ◦ More visibility on minimum interest guarantees
     ◦ Non-life insurers will want to qualify for PAA to maintain familiarity

[304] Q: List ways IFRS 17 will impact insurers’ processes.
A: • New routines
     ◦ Identifying and accounting for onerous contracts
     ◦ Presenting explicit margin for non-financial risk
     ◦ Accounting for ceded reinsurance separately from direct contracts
   • Communication challenges
     ◦ New presentation and disclosure requirements
     ◦ New KPIs
     ◦ Education for internal and external users
   • New data, systems, process, and control demands
     ◦ Key challenges: long time-horizon typical for insurance — legacy systems
     ◦ Will require new controls and upgrades
   • Scarce resources under pressure
     ◦ IFRS 17 requires significant human talent/resources
   • Opportunities for streamlining and greater efficiency
   • 2nd order impacts: IFRS 17 will trigger new tax/regulatory requirements

[305] Q: Definition of insurance contract under IFRS 17?
A: Insurance contract – when one party (issuer) accepts significant insurance risk from
   another (PH)
   • Entitles PH to compensation from issuer when insured event occurs
   • Must be enforceable by law
   • Doesn’t have to be written (can be oral, etc.)
   • Doesn’t include financial instruments or service agreements
   • Includes contracts issued by mutual entities even though PHs bear pooled risk

[306] Q: Define ”insurance risk” under IFRS 17 and provide examples of insurance vs.
non-insurance risks.
A: Insurance contracts expose issuer to insurance risk or insurance and financial
   risk
   Insurance risk – non-financial risk transferred from PH to issuer
   Examples of insurance risks:
   • Death or survival
   • Injury, illness, or disability
   • Property loss due to damage/theft
   • Failure to make debt payment when due
   • Any other non-financial variable specific to contract party
   Examples of financial risks:
   • Interest rates
   • Prices of financial instruments or commodities
   • Currency exchange rates
   • Price/rate indices
   • Credit ratings/indices
   • Any other financial variable specific to contract party

[307] Q: Treatment of catastrophe-type non-financial variables under IFRS 17?
A: Non-financial variables must be specific to a party to be insurance risks
   • Insurance risks:
     ◦ Contract covering weather event damaging PH’s assets
     ◦ Insurance swaps triggered by climate variable specific to contract party
   • Not insurance risks (not specific to contract party):
     ◦ Contract based on regional weather index
     ◦ Cat bonds with principal/interest impacted by climate variables
   Loan or bond = insurance contract if principal/interest can be adversely impacted by
   uncertain non-financial events affecting debtor
   • Example: Loan balance forgiven on death of debtor
   • Insurance contract issuer = bondholder (aka investor or lender)

[308] Q: Treatment of residual value guarantee-type non-financial variables under IFRS 17?
A: Insurance contracts if cover risk of changes in FV of specific non-financial asset held
   by contract party
   • Not insurance contracts if only covers risk of changes in market prices
   Residual value guarantee on vehicle:
   • Insurance contract if:
     ◦ Guarantees residual value of vehicle
     ◦ Amount payable varies with vehicle condition when sold
   • Not insurance contract if vehicle must be restored to specified condition

[309] Q: How to assess significant insurance risk under IFRS 17?
A: Insurance risk is significant if a scenario with commercial substance exists
   where on PV basis, issuer could:
   1. Suffer loss caused by insured event
   2. Pay significantly more than if event didn’t occur
   Additional considerations:
   • Assess at contract level
   • Insurance risk can be significant even if:
     ◦ Insured event extremely unlikely
     ◦ Expected PV contingent CFs / Expected PV all CFs is small
     ◦ Always consider TVM
     ◦ Significant insurance risk may not be present if issuer can delay timely
   reimbursement

[310] Q: Additional considerations when assessing insurance risk (review back).
A: • Insurance contract must involve uncertainty with one of:
     ◦ Probability of insured event
     ◦ Timing of insured event
     ◦ Amount payable if insured event occurs
   • PH must suffer adverse effect to receive compensation
     ◦ Lapse/persistency and expense risks not adverse to PH
     ◦ Insurer could transfer these risks to another entity to create insurance contract
     ◦ Second entity would apply IFRS 17, but not first
   • Investment contract may become insurance contract in future
     ◦ Example: deferred annuity allowing annuitization into life annuity
   • Reinsurance contract = insurance contract if either:
     ◦ Exposes reinsurer to significant loss
     ◦ Transfers substantially all insurance risk on reinsured business

[311] Q: Define investment contracts with discretionary participation features.
A: Investment contract with DPF pays amounts that are:
   1. Expected to be significant portion of total contractual benefits
   2. Paid at issuer’s discretion (timing or amount)
   3. Contractually based on:
     ◦ Returns on specified pool of contracts
     ◦ Realized/unrealized returns on specified pool of assets held by issuer
     ◦ Profit or loss of issuer
   NOT insurance contracts, but follow IFRS 17 if issued by entity that
   also issues insurance
   • If issued by any other entity, follow IAS 32, IFRS 7, and/or IFRS 9

[312] Q: Treatment of fixed-fee service contracts under IFRS 17?
A: Fixed-fee service contract – contract where service level depends on uncertain event
   • These are insurance contracts
   • Example: issuer charges fixed fee; agrees to repair car if breaks down
   Can apply IFRS 15 if meets these conditions:
   • Contract price doesn’t reflect individual customer risk
   • Compensation = service, not cash
   • Insurance risk arises primarily from uncertainty in service usage frequency
   IFRS 15 choice made for individual contracts and can’t be changed

[313] Q: Treatment of financial guarantee contracts under IFRS 17?
A: Reimburse PH for losses when specified debtor fails to make payment
   • Usually meets insurance contract definition
   • Not insurance if PH doesn’t incur loss
   IFRS 17 can be optionally applied if issuer regards FGCs as insurance contracts and
   accounts for them as such
   • Election made contract-by-contract and can’t be changed

[314] Q: Initial recognition of insurance contracts under IFRS 17 GMM?
A: Initial Liability = Initial FCF + Initial CSM
   • FCF = risk-adjusted PV of insurer’s rights and obligations to PHs
     ◦ Reflects expected CFs, TVM, and risk adjustment
     ◦ FCF = PV expected CFs + risk adjustment for non-financial risk
     ◦ Expected CFs based on current estimates
     ◦ Risk adj = compensation required to bear uncertainty in amount/timing of
   non-financial CFs
   • CSM = unearned profit recognized as insurer provides services
     ◦ Includes effects of CFs occurring on recognition date
     ◦ Includes de-recognition of any A/L previously recognized before IFRS 17
   For profitable groups, Initial FCF < 0
   • Set CSM = −FCF → no gain at inception (all profit deferred)
   For onerous (non-profitable) groups, Initial FCF > 0
   • Set CSM = 0 (no profit to defer; recognize loss immediately)
   • Amount of FCF called Loss Component

[315] Q: Subsequent recognition of insurance contracts under IFRS 17 GMM?
A: Liability after inception = sum of:
   • Liability for remaining coverage = FCF for future services + remaining CSM
     ◦ Always measured at current estimates
   • Liability for incurred claims
   Changes in FCF broken into:
   • TVM effects: recognize in statement of financial performance
   • Changes related to past/current service: recognize in P&L
   • Changes related to discount rates: recognize in OCI
   • Changes related to future services (e.g. assumption revisions)
     ◦ CSM adjusted to offset these
   Interest accretes on CSM each period using original DR

[316] Q: Basic IFRS 17 aggregation requirements?
A: All contracts must be aggregated into groups on initial recognition:
   1. Onerous contracts
   2. Contracts with no significant possibility of becoming onerous
   3. All remaining contracts
   Contracts within each group issued within 1 year of each other

[317] Q: Broad steps in IFRS 17 aggregation process?
A: 1. Identify portfolios of insurance contracts (term, WL, SPDAs, etc.)
     ◦ Portfolio = contracts with similar risks managed together
   2. Group onerous contracts within each portfolio
     ◦ Can assess in sets only if supportable; otherwise assess individual contracts
     ◦ Contracts priced to be very competitive more likely onerous
     ◦ Identification more challenging if more variation in:
     ◦ Pricing
     ◦ UW practices
     ◦ Contract features/benefits
     ◦ Distribution channel and marketing method
   3. Group profitable contracts based on possibility of becoming onerous
     ◦ As with onerous, set-level assessment allowed if supportable
     ◦ Possibility of becoming onerous based on:
     ◦ Estimates from entity’s internal reporting
     ◦ Likelihood that assumption changes could make contract onerous
     ◦ Analysis requires judgment; focus on most sensitive assumptions
     ◦ Gathering info beyond internal reporting not required
     ◦ Smaller initial CSMs more likely to become onerous

[318] Q: What is the premium allocation approach under IFRS 17?
A: PAA similar to US GAAP short-duration accounting
   • Liability for remaining coverage = unearned premium reduced for acquisition costs
   • Assumed to produce results similar to GMM
   Automatically eligible: contracts with coverage periods ≤ 1 year
   Other contracts: eligible if PAA measurement doesn’t differ materially from GMM
   • Key requirement: FCF can’thave significant variability before incurred claims
   Many GI/P&C contracts qualify (short coverage periods, no EDs)
   Most life insurance contracts do not qualify for PAA
   • Insurers may choose to apply GMM to all contracts for consistency anyway

[319] Q: How to determine FCF variability level for PAA eligibility?
A: Factors that ↑variability in FCFs:
   • Existence of embedded derivatives
   • Length of coverage period
   Considerations when determining FCF variability:
   • Key market/risk factors (e.g. interest rates, adjustable premiums)
   • Pricing information
   • Sensitivity analysis: could establish ”margin of difference”
   • Perform analysis more often in unstable interest rate environments

[320] Q: Definition of direct participating contract under IFRS 17?
A: Direct participating contracts = specific type of participation contract under IFRS 17
   • Obligation to PH = FV of Underlying Items − Variable Fee
   Insurance contract is direct participating contract when:
   • Contractual terms specify PH participates in share of clearly identified pool of
   underlying items
   • Entity expects to pay PH amount = substantial share of FV returns on underlying
   items
   • Entity expects substantial proportion of change in amounts payable to vary with
   change in FV of underlying items
   Assess criteria at inception and never again unless contract modified
   If all criteria met, contract can follow variable fee approach (VFA)

[321] Q: Key differences between IFRS 17 GMM and VFA?
A: • Changes in FCF arising from TVM and financial risks
     ◦ GMM: Recognize immediately in insurance finance income/expense
     ◦ VFA: Recognize in CSM (provided CSM > 0)
     ◦ Insurer’s share regarded as part of variable fee
   • Interest rate accreted to CSM
     ◦ GMM: Interest rate at initial recognition
     ◦ VFA: None since CSM remeasured for changes in financial risks

[322] Q: Meaning of ”contractual terms” in definition of insurance contract with direct
participation features?
A: Link to underlying items must be enforceable by law
   • Sometimes by regulator on behalf of PH
   • Agreement doesn’t need to be written: can be oral, electronic, etc.
   • Practice can vary across legal jurisdiction and industry

[323] Q: Meaning of ”clearly identified pool of underlying items” in definition of insurance
contract with direct participation features?
A: • ”Pool” can be anything as long as clearly identified in contract
     ◦ Examples: portfolio of assets or net assets of entity
   • Entity not required to hold pool
   • Underlying items don’t have to exist for contract duration
   • Clearly identified pool does NOT exist when:
     ◦ Entity can retrospectively change underlying items
     ◦ No underlying items identified
   • Suppose insurer issues 2 types of contracts with discretionary credited rates:
     ◦ Contract X: Credited rate = 3% currently
     ◦ Does not create link with any clearly identified underlying items
     ◦ Contract Y: Credited rate = return on defined portfolio minus 0.5% margin
     ◦ Does create link

[324] Q: Meaning of ”substantial” in definition of insurance contract with direct participation
features?
A: Meaning of ”substantial” in ”substantial share” and ”substantial proportion”
   • ”Substantial” based on how much discretion entity expects to exercise
   • Use probability-weighted average across scenarios
     ◦ In some scenarios, FV returns will exceed guaranteed minimums

[325] Q: Treatment of reinsurance under IFRS 17?
A: IFRS 17 defines two broad types of reinsurance contracts
   1. Reinsurance contracts issued (reinsurance assumed)
     ◦ Follow GMM
   2. Reinsurance contracts held (ceded reinsurance)
     ◦ Apply GMM with modifications
     ◦ Account for reins held separately from underlying contracts
     ◦ Obligations for underlying contracts remain after reins

[326] Q: What is the modified GMM for reinsurance contracts held?
A: Modified GMM for reinsurance contracts held reflects that:
   • Ceded reins usually asset, not liability
   • Reins contracts separate from underlying contracts
   • Ceding company pays reinsurer premium; reinsurer reimburses ceding company
   • Ceding companies usually don’t profit from reins contracts held
   • CSM for reins contracts held can be positive or negative
     ◦ Reflects net gain or cost at inception
     ◦ Negative CSM reflects ceded deferred profit

[327] Q: Other considerations for reinsurance contracts held (review back).
A: • Reins contracts can never be direct participating contracts
     ◦ I.e. VFA never allowed for any reins contract (held or issued)
   • Reins can follow PAA if eligible
     ◦ Assessment separate from underlying contracts
     ◦ Reins coverage may be > 1 year even if underlying contracts < 1 year
   • Reins contracts follow same aggregation requirements as underlying contracts
     ◦ May result in group with only one reins contract

[328] Q: Differences in earliest recognition date for proportionate vs. non-proportionate
reinsurance under IFRS 17?
A: Proportionate reinsurance: begin recognizing at same date as underlying contracts
   • I.e. at least one underlying contract must be recognized first
   Non-proportionate reinsurance: begin recognizing at earlier of:
   • Beginning of reins group’s coverage period
   • Date entity recognizes onerous underlying group

C.3.1 — Ch. 1: Modeling of Equity-Linked Insurance  (C.3 Modeling Equity-Linked Products)
----------------------------------------------------------------------

[329] Q: TVM accumulation function and properties?
A: Value of money changes over time
   a(t) = accumulation function
   a(0) = 1
   a(t + s) = a(t)a(s)   t, s ≥ 0
   Above equality must be satisfied at all times
   • If a(t + s) < a(t)a(s), investor better off withdrawing at t and immediately
   reinvesting
   • If a(t + s) > a(t)a(s), investor better off waiting until t + s maturity

[330] Q: How does Law of Large Numbers apply to traditional insurance risks?
A: LLN: average of many i.i.d. random variables approaches expected value
   X̄_{n} := (1/n)(X_{1} + · · · + X_{n}) → µ, n → ∞.
   X_{1}, X_{2}, etc. can be insurance claims (life, auto, fire, etc.)
   Mortality and longevity are diversifiable risks
   • At policy level, outcomes uncertain
   • Uncertainty ↓as number of homogeneous policies inforce↑
   Key point: variability of average claim size↓as insurer sells more policies

[331] Q: What is the Central Limit Theorem?
A: Lindeberg-Lévy CLT: average of large number of i.i.d. random variables is
   approximately normally distributed
   Z_{n} = \frac{X̄_{n} − E(X̄_{n})}{√V(X̄_{n})} → Z,   n → ∞
   Z = standard normal random variable

[332] Q: What is the Equivalence Premium Principle?
A: Under equivalence premium principle, P = PV expected benefits only
   P = PVFB =
     Be^{-rT} _{T}p_{x}   pure endowment
     B · Ā_{x} = B · E(e^{-r T_{x}})   whole life
     Ce^{-rn} _{n}p_{x} ā_{x+n}   deferred immediate annuity
   B and C = benefits and annuity payments, respectively
   This net single premium → expected loss of 0 for each policy:
   E(L_{i}) = PVFB − P = 0

[333] Q: What is the Portfolio Percentile Premium Principle?
A: More commonly used than EPP: essentially EPP + margin
   P = E(X) + [√V(X) Φ^{-1}(α)] / √n
   = Net Single Premium + Margin
   Φ^{-1}(α) = inverse standard normal CDF at αth percentile
   • Margin = SD of losses × number of SDs desired
   • P will be sufficient to cover claims α% of time
   • By CLT, margin → 0 as policy count (n) → ∞
   Variance of pure endowment (_{T}q_{x} = 1 − _{T}p_{x}):
   V(X) = Be^{-rT} _{T}p_{x} _{T}q_{x} = E(X) · _{T}q_{x}

[334] Q: Fundamental principles of Equity-Linked Insurance?
A: • Traditional Insurance: LLN diversifies mortality/longevity risk
   • ELI: Investment risk is non-diversifiable (systematic)
     ◦ Market dips impact all contracts simultaneously: ↑ max(G − F, 0)
     ◦ Investment risk remains regardless of qx certainty
     ◦ Selling more contracts ↑ exposure to investment risk
   • ELI Violates LLN Assumptions:
     ◦ Not Independent: Contracts correlated via market (F vs G)
     ◦ Not Identical Mean: Liabilities vary (e.g., by contract size)
     ◦ Wealthy PHs buy larger policies

[335] Q: Sources of investment, revenue, and expenses for deferred VAs?
A: • Sources of investment – qualified vs. non-qualified
     ◦ Qualified – tax advantaged funds meeting tax code requirements for retirement plans
     ◦ Non-qualified – after-tax money (e.g. savings account, real estate sale, etc.)
   • Sources of revenue – fees and charges assessed to PHs
     ◦ GMxB rider charges: % of guarantee base
     ◦ GMDB insurance charges: % of FV
     ◦ Admin expense charges: fixed rate or % of FV, often in M&E fee
     ◦ Investment management fee: % of FV
     ◦ Surrender charge
   • Sources of expenses
     ◦ Acquisition costs: agent commissions, marketing costs, UW costs
     ◦ Hedging costs: acquiring and managing GMxB hedging portfolios

[336] Q: VA accumulation phase formula using unit calculations?
A: Subaccount value = number of units × unit value
   Initial Units = Initial Purchase Payment / Accumulation Unit Value
   Additional purchases add units; outflows reduce units
   Initial UV = arbitrary value, then updated for income, capital gains, M&E fees
   At any time, FV = Units × Unit Value

[337] Q: VA accumulation phase formula ignoring unit calculations?
A: Subaccount value evolves from time 0 as:
   F_{k/n} = F_{0} \frac{S_{k/n}}{S_{0}} (1 − m/n)^{k},   k = 1, 2, . . . , nT   (discrete time)
   F_{t} = F_{0} \frac{S_{t}}{S_{0}} e^{-mt}   (continuous time)
   PV of insurer’s fee income ("margin offset"):
   M_{k/n} = \sum_{j=1}^{k} e^{-r(j-1)/n} (m_{e}/n) F_{(j-1)/n}
   where m_{e} = FV-based GMxB charge rate (margin offset)

[338] Q: Possible definitions of guarantee base (G) for VAs?
A: • Reset option – Ensures G ≥ F immediately after each renewal date (T_{k})
   G_{Tk} = max[G_{Tk-1}, F_{Tk}]
   • Roll-up option – G accrues interest at constant rate ρ
   G_{(k+1)/n} = G_{k/n}(1 + ρ/n)   or   G_{k/n} = G_{0}(1 + ρ/n)^{k}
   • Step-up option (aka ratchet) – reset option that can happen any period
     ◦ Lifetime high step-up: G = largest FV to date
   G_{(k+1)/n} = max[G_{k/n}, F_{(k+1)/n}]
     ◦ Annual high step-up: if FV ↑, G ↑ by same rate
   G_{(k+1)/n} = (G_{k/n}/F_{k/n}) max{F_{k/n}, F_{(k+1)/n}}   or   G_{k/n} = G_{0} Π(j=0 to k-1) max{1, F_{(j+1)/n}/F_{j/n}}
   • Other variations: combinations (max), continuous time, etc.

[339] Q: Common guaranteed minimum benefits sold with VAs?
A: • GMMB – Guarantees MB = max[G_{T}, F_{T}] at maturity T
   • GMAB – Guarantees F = G immediately after each reset date T_{k}
     ◦ If F > G, G reset to F; else F reset to G
     ◦ "Payoff" = max[G_{Tk} − F_{Tk}, 0] at each reset date T_{k}
     ◦ Another variation only adjusts G if F > G
   • GMDB – Guarantees DB = max[G_{t}, F_{t}]
   • GMWB – Guarantees withdrawal benefit, w, as % of G
     ◦ Withdrawals paid until guarantee fully withdrawn
     ◦ "Ruin time" = point at which FV = 0
     ◦ Takes T = F_{0}/w periods to deplete guarantee
   • GLWB – Lifetime version of GMWB
     ◦ Guaranteed withdrawals continue for life even if FV = 0
     ◦ Guarantee charge assessed on G, not F like others

[340] Q: Net liability for common VA guaranteed minimum benefits?
A: L = PV Expected Payments In Excess of FV − PV Expected Margin Offset Income
   Expected Benefits in Excess of FV
   GMMB: _{T}p_{x} max[G_{T} − F_{T}, 0]
   GMAB: _{Tk}p_{x} max[G_{Tk} − F_{Tk}, 0] for each reset date T_{k}
   GMDB: _{t-1}p_{x}q_{x+t-1} max[G_{t} − F_{t}, 0] for t = 1, 2, . . . , T
   GMWB: w_{t} for t > ruin time
   GLWB: _{t}p_{x} w_{t} for t > expected lifetime
   Interpretation:
   • If L > 0, L = assets insurer must have to cover future benefits in excess of fees
   • If L < 0, |L| = PV expected profits
   • Sign can change through time and by scenario

[341] Q: Simplified/practical annual FV formulas (review back).
A: For all GMxBs other than GLWB:
   F_{t} = (S_{t}/S_{t-1})F_{t-1}(1 − m) − w_{t}
   m = FV-based charge including GMxB rider charge
   w_{t} = withdrawal or surrender amount
   For GLWB only:
   F_{t} = (S_{t}/S_{t-1})[F_{t-1}(1 − m) − m_{w} × G_{t-1}] − w_{t}
   m_{w} = guarantee-based fee rate applied to G
   m = FV-based charge not including m_{w}

[342] Q: How to modify VA formulas for flexible premiums?
A: Some VAs allow additional contributions, c
   F_{(k+1)/n} = (S_{(k+1)/n}/S_{k/n}) F_{k/n}(1 − m/n) + c/n
   G also ↑ with each contribution—e.g. for roll-up:
   G_{k/n} = G_{k/n}(1 + ρ/n) + c/n

[343] Q: Mechanics of immediate variable annuity?
A: Initial calculations:
   P_{0} = F_{0}/ä_{n|} and Units = P_{0}/V_{0}
   Subsequent calculations:
   P_{k} = Units × V_{k} or P_{k-1} × V_{k} if ignoring units
   V_{k} = V_{k-1} × I_{k}/(1 + i)
   I_{k} = (Previous NAV + InvInc & CapGains − CapLosses & Charges (m)) / Previous NAV
   NAV = PH’s net asset value (FV); I_{k} = net investment factor (NIF)

[344] Q: Formulas used to model immediate variable annuities?
A: NIF expressed as:
   I_{k} = (S_{k}/S_{k-1})(1 − m)
   Allows rewriting formulas for P_{k} and F_{k}:
   P_{k} = P_{0}(S_{k}/S_{0})\frac{1 - m}{1 + i}^{k}
   F_{k} = (S_{k}/S_{0})(1 − m)^{k}(F_{0} − P_{0}ä_{k|})

[345] Q: Key differences with life-contingent variable immediate annuities?
A: Life-contingent version based on life annuity factor with AIR
   P_{0} = \frac{F_{0}}{ä_{x}}
   F_{k} = \frac{S_{k}}{S_{0}}(1 − m)^{k}(F_{0} − P_{0}ä_{k|})   (Same as traditional)
   Unlike traditional immediate annuity, LC payments stop immediately on death
   • If PH dies before expected lifetime K_{x}, FV > 0 at death
     ◦ Insurer Profit = FV released on death (F_{k})
   • If PH outlives K_{x}, FV = 0, but insurer pays P_{k} until death
   Liability = \sum_{k=1}^{K_{x}} P_{k} I(ä_{k|} > ä_{x})
   • Interaction between investments and mortality = more uncertainty for insurer

[346] Q: Compare Fixed-Indexed Annuities (aka EIAs) with VAs.
A: Key difference with traditional fixed DAs: more upside through index participation
   Differences with VAs:
   • Cash Flows:
     ◦ FIAs: Assets held in GA; insurer bears equity risk
     ◦ VAs: Assets held in SA; PHs bear equity risk (except GMxBs)
   • Risk and Reward:
     ◦ FIAs: partial participation in equity returns with caps
     ◦ VAs: full participation without caps
   • Fees:
     ◦ FIAs: no fees; control costs via participation rates, caps, floors
     ◦ VAs: charge fees for management and guarantees (like mutual funds)
   • Regulation:
     ◦ FIAs: fixed annuities regulated as insurance products
     ◦ VAs: securities regulated by SEC

[347] Q: Common index crediting methods for fixed indexed annuities?
A: 1. Point-to-Point – compares index at beginning and end of term
     ◦ Guaranteed min maturity value = G = Pe^{gT}
   2. Cliquet – compares index value at beginning and end of each year
     ◦ Annual index return may be capped at c
     ◦ Guaranteed min annual return = g where g < c < 1
   3. High-Water Mark – based on highest index value on policy anniversary
     ◦ Guaranteed min maturity value = G = Pe^{gT}
   Each design includes index participation rate, α
   • Can be applied in ”simple” or ”compound” way
   • Results similar for small index returns

[348] Q: Maturity value formulas (review back).
A: PTP maturity value:
   max(P(1 + α(S_{T}−S_{0})/S_{0}), Pe^{gT})   or   max(P(S_{T}/S_{0})^{α}, Pe^{gT})
   Cliquet maturity value:
   P Π(k=1 to T) max(min(1 + α(S_{k}−S_{k-1})/S_{k-1}, e^{c}), e^{g})   or   P Π(k=1 to T) max(min((S_{k}/S_{k-1})^{α}, e^{c}), e^{g})
   HWM maturity value:
   max(P(1 + α(max[S_{k}]/S_{0} − 1)), Pe^{gT})   or   max(P(max[S_{k}]/S_{0})^{α}, Pe^{gT})

C.3.2 — Ch. 4: Pricing and Valuation  (C.3 Modeling Equity-Linked Products)
----------------------------------------------------------------------

[349] Q: How do forwards and futures work?
A: Agreement to buy/sell asset at set price (forward price) on future date
   • No cost to enter (simply an agreement)
   • Forwards typically private and customized
   • Futures like forwards but standardized and traded on exchanges
   Payoff: Contract value at maturity (can be positive or negative)
   • Long position payoff = S_{T} − F
   • Short position payoff = F − S_{T}

[350] Q: How do call and put options work?
A: • Call option: Gives buyer option to buy S at strike (K) by set date
     ◦ Long call payoff = (S_{T} − K)+
   • Put option: Gives buyer option to sell S at strike (K) by set date
     ◦ Long put payoff = (K − S_{T})+
   • Short side: Negative payoff if long side exercises
     ◦ Else, short’s gain = premium received
   • European options: Exercisable only at maturity
     ◦ Path-independent: Payoff depends only on S at maturity
     ◦ Includes PTP FIAs
   • American options: Exercisable anytime up to maturity
     ◦ Path-dependent: Payoff depends on S before maturity
     ◦ Includes cliquet and HWM FIAs

[351] Q: Define arbitrage and no-arbitrage pricing.
A: Arbitrage = trading strategy that:
   1. Begins with no money
   2. Zero probability of losing money
   3. Positive probability of making money
   No-arbitrage principle or law of one price – two transactions with equivalent CFs
   should have same price
   • In efficient market, arbitrage shouldn’t exist
   No-arbitrage forward price: F = S_{t} e^{r(T-t)}
   • If F > S_{t} e^{r(T-t)}, borrow to buy asset; enter short side of forward
   • If F < S_{t} e^{r(T-t)}, shortsell asset; enter long side of forward

[352] Q: Binomial tree discrete time pricing model?
A: Binomial model assumptions
   • Only two assets: risky (S_{t}) and risk-free (B_{t}) earning r
   • S_{t} can go up by factor u or down by factor d with real-world probabilities:
   Pr[S_{t+1} = uS_{t}] = p = 1 − q
   Pr[S_{t+1} = dS_{t}] = q = 1 − p
   • d < e^{r} < u (else arbitrage exists)
   • Self-financing: no external CFs needed
     ◦ No transaction costs
     ◦ Unlimited borrowing/selling/shortselling/buying
   Two equivalent methods for binomial option pricing:
   1. Replication portfolio method
   2. Risk-neutral probability method

[353] Q: Single period replication portfolio method?
A: Goal: Construct portfolio (X_{0}) replicating option’s payoffs in one period
   Stock Price: S_{0} → uS_{0} (up) or dS_{0} (down)
   Option: V_{0} → V_{1}(u) or V_{1}(d)
   Replication Portfolio: X_{0} = B_{0}e^{-r} + Δ_{0}S_{0} → B_{0} + Δ_{0}uS_{0} or B_{0} + Δ_{0}dS_{0}
   where V_{1} = (S_{1} − K)+ for call or (K − S_{1})+ for put
   Key steps to calculate option price:
   1. Set V_{1}(u) = B_{0} + Δ_{0}uS_{0} and V_{1}(d) = B_{0} + Δ_{0}dS_{0}
   2. Solve for Δ_{0} = (V_{1}(u) − V_{1}(d)) / (S_{0}(u − d)) = portion of S to hold ("delta")
   3. Solve for B_{0} = V_{1}(u) − Δ_{0}uS_{0} or B_{0} = V_{1}(d) − Δ_{0}dS_{0}
   4. No-arbitrage option price = V_{0} = X_{0}

[354] Q: Single period risk-neutral probability method?
A: Suppose we can express S_{0} as risk-free PV of special expected value:
   S_{0} = e^{-r} Ẽ[S_{1}]
   = e^{-r}[p̃uS_{0} + q̃dS_{0}]
   = e^{-r}[p̃uS_{0} + (1 − p̃)dS_{0}]
   p̃ and q̃ = risk-neutral (RN) probabilities (≠ RW probabilities p and q)
   p̃ = (e^{r} − d)/(u − d)   and   q̃ = 1 − p̃ = (u − e^{r})/(u − d)
   This allows expressing no-arbitrage price as:
   V_{0} = e^{-r} Ẽ[V_{1}] = e^{-r}[p̃V_{1}(u) + q̃V_{1}(d)]
   Under RN framework, all assets expected to yield risk-free rate
   B_{1} = B_{0}e^{r}     Ẽ[S_{1}] = S_{0}e^{r}     Ẽ[V_{1}] = V_{0}e^{r}

[355] Q: Multi-period risk-neutral probability method?
A: Work backwards using single period equations (2-period binomial tree: S_{0} → uS_{0}/dS_{0} → u²S_{0}/udS_{0}/d²S_{0}, with option values V_{0} → V_{1}(u)/V_{1}(d) → V_{2}(u,u)/V_{2}(u,d)/V_{2}(d,d)):
   1. At time 1: Solve for 2 possible values of V_{1}
   V_{1}(u) = e^{-r}[p̃V_{2}(u,u) + q̃V_{2}(u,d)]
   V_{1}(d) = e^{-r}[p̃V_{2}(d,u) + q̃V_{2}(d,d)]
   2. At time 0: Solve for option price:
   V_{0} = e^{-r}[p̃V_{1}(u) + q̃V_{1}(d)]

[356] Q: Black-Scholes framework assumptions?
A: Black-Scholes assumptions similar to binomial model:
   • Two assets: risky S_{t} and risk-free B_{t} earning r
   • S_{t} is continuous variable: follows geometric Brownian motion
   • Self-financing: no external CFs needed
     ◦ No transaction costs
     ◦ Unlimited borrowing/selling/shortselling/buying
   RN formulas for options/derivatives based on "Feymann-Kac formula"
   f(t, x) = E[e^{-r(T-t)}h(X_{T})|X_{t} = x]
   where:
   • f(t, x) = value at time t when state variable X has value x
   • h(X_{T}) = payoff function at maturity

[357] Q: Black-Scholes RN price for forwards?
A: No-arbitrage cost to enter forward at inception date t:
   f(t, S_{t}) = S_{t} − Fe^{-r(T-t)} = 0
   Cost of forward at any point after inception:
   f(t, S_{t}) = S_{t} − Fe^{-r(T-t)}

[358] Q: Black-Scholes RN price for calls and puts?
A: No-arbitrage prices of European call and put:
   c_{t} = Ẽ[e^{-r(T-t)}(S_{T} − K)+|S_{t} = s]        p_{t} = Ẽ[e^{-r(T-t)}(K − S_{T})+|S_{t} = s]
   = sΦ(d_{1}) − Ke^{-r(T-t)}Φ(d_{2})              = Ke^{-r(T-t)}Φ(−d_{2}) − sΦ(−d_{1})
   where:
   Φ(z) = standard normal CDF and Φ(−z) = 1 − Φ(z)
   d_{1} = [ln(s/K) + (r + σ²/2)(T − t)] / (σ√(T − t))
   d_{2} = d_{1} − σ√(T − t)
   Put-call parity relates call and put prices:
   c_{t} − p_{t} = s − Ke^{-r(T-t)}

[359] Q: How to calculate distributable earnings on traditional income statement?
A: 1. Calculate Pre-Tax Book Profit = Revenues − Expenses − Benefits
     ◦ Revenues = Premium + InvInc + Policy Fee Income
     ◦ Expenses = Admin Expenses + Commissions + GMDB Cost
     ◦ Benefits = Death Claims + Annuitizations + Surrender Benefits +↑Resv + GMDB
   Benefit
   2. Calculate After-Tax Book Profit = Pre-Tax Book Profit − Tax
     ◦ Tax = Tax Rate× Pre-Tax Book Profit
   3. Calculate ↑Target Surplus = EOY Targ Surplus− BOY Targ Surplus
     ◦ EOY Targ Surplus = Targ Surplus Rate× EOY Resv
     ◦ BOY Targ Surplus = Previous Year EOY Targ Surplus
   4. Calculate After-Tax Interest on Targ Surplus = BOY Targ Surplus
   ×Surplus Int Rate × (1 − Tax Rate)
   5. Calculate Distributable Earnings = After-Tax Book Profit − ↑Targ Surplus +
   After-Tax Interest on Targ Surplus

[360] Q: Distributable earnings – simpler income statement vs. traditional method.
A:
 | Traditional | Simpler
-+-------------+--------
Pre-Tax Book Profit | Revenues − Expenses − Benefits | Revenues − Expenses − GMDB Benefit
Revenues | Premium + InvInc + Policy Fee Income | Surrender Charges + Policy Fee Income + Risk Charges
Expenses | Admin Expenses + Commissions + GMDB Cost | Admin Expenses + Commissions + GMDB Cost
Benefits | Death Claims + Annuitizations + Surrender Benefits + ↑Resv + GMDB Benefit | — (folded into GMDB Benefit above)
Steps 2–5 (Tax, ↑Targ Surplus, Interest, Distributable Earnings) | Same | Same

[361] Q: Three key profit measures in actuarial pricing?
A: 1. Net present value (NPV) of distributable earnings
     ◦ Discount rate = hurdle rate
     ◦ NPV > 0 suggests net profit for insurer; else net loss
   2. IRR = discount rate making NPV = 0
     ◦ If IRR > hurdle rate, project expected to be profitable
   3. Profit margin (PM)
   PM = NPV / PV of premiums   or   PM = NPV / PV of Revenues
   First-year strain: Large deficit in first year
   Limitation for ELI: Typically considers only one economic scenario
   • Monte Carlo simulations ideal

[362] Q: Compare actuarial vs. no-arbitrage pricing.
A: • Actuarial pricing: based on quantile of profit under RW measure
     ◦ Includes all relevant RW CFs
     ◦ Aims for positive NPV in certain % of RW scenarios: Pr[NPV > 0] > α
     ◦ Mirrors portfolio percentile premium principle
   • No-arbitrage pricing: based on expected profit under RN measure
     ◦ Ignores expenses, surrender charges, taxes
     ◦ Expected profit = Ẽ[L] = 0
     ◦ Mirrors equivalence premium principle
     ◦ Rarely used in practice

C.3.3 — Simulation of a GMAB  (C.3 Modeling Equity-Linked Products)
----------------------------------------------------------------------

[363] Q: GMAB cash flow profile and impact of fund return?
A: • Policyholder Cash Flows:
     ◦ Before maturity: Withdrawals/DB per decrement rate
     ◦ At maturity: gets max(FV, GMAB)
   • Insurer Cash Flows:
     ◦ Year 1: Negative from initial expenses
     ◦ Year 2+ before maturity: Positive from M&E and GMAB rider fees
     ◦ At maturity: pays max(GMAB − FV, 0)
   • Positive PV of insurer CFs → insurer profitable
   • Impact of Fund Return:
     ◦ Lower assumed fund return → ↑likelihood of GMAB payout
     ◦ Very poor (negative) returns→ large insurer losses
     ◦ GMAB payout can far exceed rider revenue in bad scenarios
     ◦ Risk not diversifiable like mortality risk

[364] Q: How do puts hedge a GMAB?
A: Key takeaway: Hedging significantly stabilizes insurer CFs but at a cost
   • Since GMAB = put option, insurer can hedge with put option
   • In scenarios triggering GMAB payout, put option also pays off
   • Put proceeds help offset GMAB payout
   • Put premium = additional insurer expense
   • Some volatility remains since fee income sensitive to fund performance

[365] Q: GMAB hedge put option parameters (review back)
A: n = same term as GMAB
   K = CurrentIndex × [GMAB / (Premium × (1 − m)^{n})]
   Notional = [Premium × (1 − w)^{n}] / CurrentIndex
   Total Put Cost = Notional × Black-Scholes Put Price
   where:
   • m = total fee = M&E fee + GMAB rider fee
   • K = strike price of single put option on index
     ◦ m → higher strike price (↑ probability of payoff)
   • Notional = number of put options needed
     ◦ Reduced since some PHs lapse (w) before GMAB exercised
   • Black-Scholes Put Price = price of put on just one unit of index

[366] Q: Considerations for stochastic simulation of GMAB?
A: Problem: Closed-form solutions usually don’t exist (PH behavior, etc.)
   Monte Carlo simulations usually more practical
   • Involves simulating 1000s of RN or RW scenarios
   • Strength of RN: more accurate for pricing (cost of hedging)
     ◦ Average value more accurate than RW approach
     ◦ Monte Carlo RN average price→ Black-Scholes price if enough simulations
   • Strength of RW: provides meaningful distribution of results to analyze
     ◦ Shows asymmetry: most scenarios profitable, but unprofitable scenarios can be very
   bad
     ◦ Not as accurate as RN since assumed fund return > risk-free rate

C.3.4 — VA Guaranteed Living Benefits Utilization  (C.3 Modeling Equity-Linked Products)

[367] Q: Popularity of guaranteed living withdrawal benefits vs. other GLBs?
A: • GLWB and GMIB most popular by far
     ◦ 90% of VA GLBs = GLWB or GMIB
     ◦ GLWB has highest sales growth (most premium year 1)
     ◦ GLWB significantly more popular than non-lifetime version (GMWB)
   • GLWB and GMIB have highest persistency (very low surrender rates)
   • GMAB popular with younger buyers

[368] Q: 4 inter-connected relationships for GLWB and GMIB utilization?
A: 1. When withdrawals start (age and source of funding most significant)
     ◦ After age 70, withdrawals↑with age and duration
     ◦ 2/3 of contracts funded with qualified money (tax-favored retirement funds)
   2. Method of withdrawals (SWPs most significant)
     ◦ SWPs popular for lifetime benefits and older owners (65+)
     ◦ Withdrawals usually continue once started (low surrenders)
   3. Amount of withdrawal taken
     ◦ Most owners take max allowed by contract (e.g. 4% of benefit base/year)
     ◦ Younger owners more likely to exceed max
     ◦ ITM has little to no effect
   4. Surrender rates – factors→ low surrenders:
     ◦ Older owners (65+) in general—even lower if taking withdrawals
     ◦ Younger owners nottaking withdrawals yet
     ◦ SWPs have lower surrenders than non-systematic
     ◦ Withdrawal amount ≈ 100% of max (not too far below/above)
     ◦ Higher ITM → lower surrenders
     ◦ Still in surrender charge period (higher lapse when exiting)

[369] Q: Action steps and issues for pricing VA GLBs?
A: 1. Expect high utilization of lifetime benefits (hence low lapses)
     ◦ Many used to fund retirement
     ◦ GLB riders popular
   2. Qualified money has highest withdrawal risk
   3. Withdrawal activity can predict surrender activity
   4. Modeling GLB utilization important, especially for lifetime options

[370] Q: Product design comparison of popular VA GLBs?
A: 1. GLWB
     ◦ Lifetime benefits available ages 50–99 (usually 50s–80s)
     ◦ Median max withdrawal allowed = 4%
     ◦ Benefit base usually reduced proportionally for excess withdrawals
   2. GMIB
     ◦ Most common design: roll-up or max(roll-up, ratchet)
     ◦ Most are B-share (have SCs)
     ◦ Annuitization rates extremely low (< 1%), but↑with age, size, and ITM
   3. GMAB
     ◦ Most common benefit base design: 100% of premiums paid in
     ◦ Typical waiting period: 10+ years
   4. GMWB
     ◦ Differences with GLWB
     ◦ No automatic↑to benefit base if delay withdrawals
     ◦ No cap on benefit base

D.1.1 — Ch. 1: Basic Terms and Concepts  (D.1 Reinsurance Topics)
----------------------------------------------------------------------

[371] Q: Basic Reinsurance Terminology (review back of card)
A: • Reinsurance: Insurance on some/all of an insurer’s risks
   • Ceding Company (CC): Insurer that cedes risks to reinsurer
     ◦ Aka direct insurer or cedant
   • Reinsurer: Insurer that accepts (assumes) risk from CC
   • Treaty: Legal agreement between CC and reinsurer
   • Retention: Amount of risk CC retains (does not cede)
   • Retrocessionaire: Reinsurer that accepts risk from another reinsurer
   • Cession: Portion of risk transferred to reinsurer
   • Retrocession: Portion of risk transferred from reinsurer to retrocessionaire

[372] Q: A Brief History of Reinsurance (review back of card)
A: • Ancient origins (BCE)
     ◦ Risk spreading on Chinese merchant ships, Babylonian maritime loans
     ◦ Greeks/Romans included risk fees on maritime loans
   • Medieval times
     ◦ First insurance contract (1298), reinsurance contract (1370, marine)
     ◦ Term “reinsurance” first used in Florence (1457)
   • Modern developments
     ◦ 18th-century England legislation strengthened insurable interest
     ◦ 19th-century fires spurred fire insurance, founding of reinsurers (e.g., Swiss Re)
     ◦ US life reinsurance began with American Central Life (1904)
   • Post-WWII to early 21st century
     ◦ Reinsurance expanded post-WWII; N. American reinsurers ↓ since 1990s
     ◦ Remains vital in life/health, growing use in annuities

[373] Q: Compare recurring vs. portfolio reinsurance
A: • Recurring: Agreements on new policies
   • Portfolio: Agreements to cede significant portions of inforce policies (aka block
   reinsurance)

[374] Q: Compare indemnity vs. assumption reinsurance
A: Indemnity (most common):
   • Reinsurer reimburses CC for claims share
   • No contractual relationship with p/h
   • CC remains liable even if reinsurer defaults
   • May include recapture provisions
   Assumption:
   • Reinsurer becomes directly liable to p/h
   • CC no longer liable
   • Requires novation (contract replacement)
   • P/h must agree, receive assumption certificates

[375] Q: Compare proportional vs. non-proportional reinsurance
A: Proportional: Responsibility based on benefit known in advance
   • Most common for life/health
   • Coinsurance: Proportional sharing of risks/terms
   • Mod-Co: Like coinsurance, CC retains reserves
     ◦ Reinsurer pays reserve ↑ net of inv income
   • YRT: Transfers only mortality/morbidity risk in NAR
   Non-proportional: Liability depends on claims incurred
   • More common for unpredictable claims (P&C)
   • Stop Loss: Covers losses > attachment point
   • Catastrophe: High-severity, low-frequency events

[376] Q: Compare automatic vs. facultative reinsurance
A: • Automatic: Risks exceeding retention that meet UW criteria are automatically
   ceded
     ◦ CC must cede, reinsurer must accept
   • Facultative: CC may choose to cede or not
     ◦ Reinsurer must approve each risk
   • Variations:
     ◦ Conditional automatic: Reinsurer provides UW service
     ◦ Facultative obligatory: Reinsurer can only decline if it hits full retention on that
   individual

[377] Q: Compare excess treaty vs. quota share reinsurance
A: • Excess treaty: Covers risks based on scheduled retention limits
   • Quota share: Fixed % of each risk is ceded
     ◦ Typically applies to first dollar of coverage
     ◦ Can be combined with excess risk management

[378] Q: Compare experience rated vs. non-experience rated reinsurance
A: • Experience Rated: CC shares in reinsurer profits via experience refund
     ◦ Higher reinsurance premiums
   • Non-Experience Rated: More common today

[379] Q: Compare traditional vs. financial reinsurance
A: • Traditional: Primary purpose is risk transfer
   • Financial: Primary purpose is specific business objective (e.g., surplus ↑, tax
   ↓, acquiring blocks), risk transfer secondary
     ◦ Distinctions may require close examination of risks transferred

[380] Q: List uses of reinsurance
A: Risk management:
   • Mortality/morbidity transfer
   • Lapse/surrender transfer
   • Investment risk transfer
   Business/financial strategy:
   • New business financing, UW assistance
   • Entering new markets, M&A, divesting product lines
   • Optimizing admin functions
   • Reinsurer returns/inforce ↑
   • Surplus management/optimization
   • Redundant reserve management

[381] Q: How can reinsurance transfer risk?
A: Mortality/morbidity (oldest, most common):
   • Life: Excess/QS treaties; retention limit key to limiting insolvency
   • Deferred annuities: Usually % of fund reinsured
   • Payout annuities: Rare (flat retention or QS)
   Lapse/surrender (relevant when):
   • Significant first-year strain
   • Premiums ↑ sharply
   • Level premiums, claims ↑
   • Anti-selection/disintermediation from high lapses
   Investment:
   • Leverage reinsurer capabilities, limit concentrations
   • Common with coinsurance/mod-co

[382] Q: How does reinsurance aid new business/market entry?
A: • New business financing: Relieves surplus strain
   • UW assistance: Manuals, training, audit, difficult case review
   • Market entry: Expertise on products, regulation, business lines
   • Fronting: Direct insurer issues policies, reinsures substantially all risk
     ◦ Reinsurer handles admin
     ◦ Common in P&C/A&H; rare for ILA/health

[383] Q: Why might CC cede large portions of new business?
A: • Low marginal cost (reinsurer’s lower expenses or different assumptions)
     ◦ Small profit > no profit
   • Allows writing larger policies
   • UW gains experience from reinsurer
   • Recapture provisions → future profit ↑

[384] Q: How does reinsurance relate to divestiture, M&A, and admin optimization?
A: • Divesting product line: Exit lines/areas, reallocate capital, simplify admin,
   overhead Ó
   • M&A: Transfer blocks or sell/purchase companies
     ◦ Assumption typically used for permanent sales; indemnity sometimes preferred
   • Admin optimization:
     ◦ Cede business →admin burden Ó
     ◦ Assume business →use excess admin capacity

[385] Q: How does reinsurance benefit reinsurers?
A: • Returns ↑: Assume business for profit and ROI
   • Inforce ↑: Leverage under-utilized admin capacity, spread overhead, augment
   direct sales

[386] Q: What is the purpose of producer-owned reinsurance companies (PORCs)?
A: • Substantial reinsurance volume ceded to PORCs
   • Allows high-producing agent groups to participate in earnings
   • Agent loyalty ↑, rewards high production

[387] Q: How does reinsurance optimize surplus and fund redundant reserves?
A: Surplus optimization:
   • Fund acquisition costs from high sales
   • Meet RBC/statutory/rating requirements
   • Replenish surplus post-poor experience
   • Meet dividend/debt service objectives
   SPVs (form of captive):
   • Raise capital, mitigate risks via securitization
   • Securitization = selling cash flow streams (premiums, claims)
   • Major use: Fund redundant XXX/AXXX reserves

D.1.2, D.1.4, D.1.6 — Ch. 4: Basic Methods (YRT, Coinsurance, Mod-co)  (D.1 Reinsurance Topics)
----------------------------------------------------------------------

[388] Q: Describe YRT reinsurance and which products it suits.
A: Reinsures mortality/morbidity risk only
   CC pays:
   • Reins premium to reinsurer
   • All p/h benefits and expenses
   Reinsurer pays:
   • Reinsured claims (reimburses CC)
   • Expense allowance
   CC takes small ceded reserve credit (= unearned YRT premium)
   Good for:
   • Term, WL, UL (mortality)
   • Disability, LTCI, critical illness (morbidity)
   Not good for:
   • Lapse/investment risk transfer
   • Annuities
   Provides little surplus relief vs. coinsurance

[389] Q: How is ceded NAR calculated under YRT?
A: NAR = Face − Terminal Reserve (or per treaty definition)
   • Calculated at policy level
   • Ignore deficiency reserves
   • YRT Premiums = YRT Rates × Ceded NAR
   • Ceded DB = Reinsurer’s NAR share
   • NAR must be well documented in treaty
     ◦ Companies agree on method
     ◦ DB in calculation = DB paid on death
     ◦ NAR used ≈ actual NAR
   • Total Face = NAR + _{t}V_{x} (visualized as a stacked bar: NAR on top of the reserve)

[390] Q: List 4 YRT retention methods.
A: 1. Pro Rata (preferred)
     ◦ Constant reinsured % = Orig Face Ceded / Total Orig Face
     ◦ → Very small reins amounts in later durations
   2. Level/Constant Retention
     ◦ CC retains fixed NAR amount
     ◦ Reinsured NAR ↓ over time
   3. Constant Risk Reinsured (rare)
     ◦ Reinsured amount = constant
     ◦ Retained NAR ↓ over time
   4. Formula Retention
     ◦ NAR and retention per agreed formula

[391] Q: How are YRT reinsurance premiums calculated?
A: ReinsPrem = (YRT Rate × Ceded NAR)/1000 + Cession Fee
   • YRT premium rate = multiple of mortality table
     ◦ Vary by age, duration, sex, UW class
     ◦ Select periods common (e.g., 15 years)
   • May have zero first-year premium (ZFT)
   • Calendar year scales avoid year-end reins reserves
   • Substandard ratings:
     ◦ Table rating: ↑ by multiple (e.g., 150%)
     ◦ Flat extra: May have allowance on extra premium
   • Riders: Accidental death may be reinsured separately, term riders use base
   • Monthly renewable term (MRT) for UL: YRT done monthly

[392] Q: Describe coinsurance and compare to YRT.
A: Reinsurer has proportionate share of:
   • Direct p/h premium
   • Direct reserve
   • All benefits (mortality, morbidity, lapse, surrender, investment)
   vs. YRT:
   • Cedes more premium, reserves, benefits
     ◦ Reinsurer must track all these
     ◦ EAs more complicated
   • Can be used for any insurance/annuities
   • More surplus relief than YRT
   • Passes deficiency reserves to reinsurer

[393] Q: How are coinsurance premiums calculated? List approaches for banded premiums.
A: ReinsPrem = (Amount Ceded Per 1000) × (GP Per 1000)
   Approaches for banded premiums:
   1. Simplest
     ◦ Use actual GP per policy
     ◦ Common allowances for all bands
   2. More equitable (more complicated)
     ◦ Use actual GP per policy
     ◦ Vary allowances by band
   3. Single band
     ◦ Use GP for specific band regardless of actual
     ◦ One set of allowances for all policies

[394] Q: Describe variations in coinsurance expense allowances.
A: • Allowance = % of ceded premium
   • Uniquely developed per product
   • Varies by duration, sex, risk class, band, etc.
     ◦ Based on reinsurer profit objectives
   • Premium tax methods:
     ◦ Most common: Reinsurer includes provision in basic allowance
     ◦ Reimburse exact amount (challenging)
     ◦ Pay fixed amount as proxy

[395] Q: How are the following treated under coinsurance:
• Policy fee
• Policyholder dividends
• Policy loans
A: • Policy fee: May be fully retained by CC
     ◦ Reinsurer may coinsure so it’s included in deficiency reserve
   • P/h dividends: Reinsurer may or may not participate
     ◦ If no participation → asset mgmt challenges for CC
   • Policy loans: No reinsurer participation ever
   • Substandards and riders treated similarly to YRT

[396] Q: Compare mod-co to regular coinsurance.
A: Mod-co = coinsurance except:
   1. CC retains assets backing ceded reserves
   2. No ceded reserve credit on CC balance sheet
   3. No reinsured reserve on reinsurer balance sheet
   4. Reinsurer pays mod-co adjustment to CC
     ◦ Main complexity vs. coinsurance
   Advantages vs. coinsurance:
   • CC has more investment control
   • Good for interest-sensitive liabilities, p/h dividends
   • More assets for policy loans
   • Good when reinsurer offshore/unauthorized
     ◦ No reserve credit questions

[397] Q: How is the mod-co adjustment calculated?
A: Positive mod-co adjustment = payment by reinsurer
   Mod-co adjustment = Ending ceded stat reserves − Beginning ceded stat reserves − "Interest" on beginning ceded stat reserves
   Mod-co interest rate must be clearly defined in treaty
   • Should ensure reinsurer participates in total asset performance
     ◦ Asset defaults, capital gains/losses, timing risks, inv income
   • Not simple interest rate in practice
   • Governed by NAIC Life & Health Reins Agreements Model Reg

[398] Q: List goals and advantages of advanced reinsurance.
A: Goals:
   1. Surplus relief to CC
   2. Reduce asset transfers between companies
   Advantages for CC:

   2. Improve security of ceded reserve credit
   3. Coordinate asset mgmt for asset-sensitive liabilities
   4. Minimize capital gains/losses at inception
   5. Easier recapture (minimizes capital gains/losses)

   • i × OSA_{t-1} = inv income on beginning OSA
   • RiskCharge = % of beginning OSA
   OSA calculation same for all methods

[401] Q: Compare reinsurance report vs. FW balance calculation.
A: Net Cash Due to Reinsurer = Ceded premium − Allowances − Ceded benefits − Mod-co adjustment (if any) − Experience refund (if any)
   EOY FW Balance = BOY FW Balance + Ceded premium − Allowances − Ceded benefits − Mod-co adjustment (if any) − Experience refund (if any) − Risk charge

   paid in cash)

[402] Q: Compare FW coinsurance vs. regular coinsurance.
A: Differences:
   1. No cash exchanged at inception/renewal
   2. Net cash owed → accounts payable/receivable
     ◦ Net cash owed > 0 → accounts payable liability
     ◦ Otherwise → accounts receivable asset
     ◦ One company’s receivable = other’s payable
   3. Assets stay with CC (like mod-co)
     ◦ But CC shows ceded reserve credit (like regular coinsurance)
     ◦ FW balance adjusted for interest on retained assets
     ◦ Authors include ceded interest in ceded premium
   4. Alternative OSA calculation for FW:
   OSA_{t} = OSA_{t-1} + CededPrem_{t}
   − CededBen_{t} − CededResIncr_{t} − ExperienceRefund_{t} − RiskCharge_{t}

[403] Q: Describe FW mod-co.
A: Tip: Think of it as “allowance retained mod-co”
   FW-Mod-Co = mod-co except:
   • Reinsurer retains initial allowance
   • Reinsurer’s initial FW liability = initial allowance
   • CC’s initial FW asset = initial allowance
   FW-Mod-Co may violate state laws
   • State laws usually require quarterly settlement
   • May be possible to get approval

[404] Q: Describe part-co.
A: Part-co = blend of regular coinsurance and mod-co
   • Main advantages:
     ◦ Eliminates/minimizes cash transfer
     ◦ No FW balance to track
   • Cash owed by each party cancels at inception:
     ◦ Initial coinsurance reserve = initial allowance
     ◦ Remaining reserves are mod-co reinsured
   • Co/mod-co split adjusted in renewals to minimize cash flow:
     ◦ Generally, stat profits ↓ coinsurance portion
     ◦ Could be designed so coinsurance portion = reinsurer’s outstanding investment
   • Disadvantages:
     ◦ Very complicated to administer/understand
     ◦ Regulators may be concerned about ↑ coinsurance portion
     ◦ Must calculate stat gain twice: preliminary then final

[405] Q: What are SPV objectives?
A: • SPV owner = “sponsor” (reinsurer, direct insurer, or parent)
   • Sponsor objectives: Fund redundant reserves with low-cost capital
     ◦ Allows other capital for higher risk (higher returns)
     ◦ Redundant Reserves = Total Stat Reserves − Economic Reserves
     ◦ Total Stat Reserves = Basic Stat Reserve + XXX Reserves
     ◦ Economic Reserves = GAAP BenRes − DAC
     ◦ Should emerge from cash flows without additional funding
   • Investor objectives: Low-risk investment
     ◦ Low loss possibility
     ◦ Willing to accept low return (low risk)
     ◦ Investors: Bondholders or bank issuing LOC

[406] Q: Describe SPV capital structure.
A: • =Required Capital” = capital contributed by sponsor
     ◦ Greater of min regulatory capital and capital required by investors
     ◦ May not tie to regulatory/rating agency capital
   • Economic Reserves + Required Capital
     ◦ Should fund claims/expenses even under adverse developments
     ◦ Higher RC = less risk for investors
     ◦ If RC too high → SPV may not be viable

[407] Q: List the 3 trust types in typical securitization SPV model.
A: Captive (SPV)
   Economic Reserve Trust
   (100% Coinsurance Cash Flows)
   Capital Account Trust
   (Parent’s RC Paid-In)
   Redundant Reserve Trust
   (Investor Cash or LOC)
   Investors
   (Or a Bank)
   Parent (Sponsor)
   (Re)Insurer
   Losses

[408] Q: List steps for structuring an SPV.
A: 1. Sponsor forms captive, contributes RC
   2. If issuing bonds, document:
     ◦ Restrictions on use of funds
     ◦ Delineation between reserve/capital requirements
     ◦ Investor funds used only after reserves/RC
     ◦ How/when dividends, cash, etc. may be paid to sponsor
     ◦ Any experience refunds
     ◦ Interest payment on bonds
     ◦ Repayment of initial principal
   3. Place funds in 3 trusts:
     ◦ Redundant reserve trust
     ◦ Capital account trust
     ◦ Economic reserve trust

[409] Q: List SPV investor considerations.
A: • SPVs expensive to establish: Requires sufficient volume
   • Bonds no longer issued post-crisis
   • Structures usually include NB for limited time (e.g., 1 year)
   • SPVs usually cover limited product groups (e.g., level term)
   • Max funding required for RC/redundant reserves at inception
     ◦ But could have agreement for more in future
   • Usually based on some form of coinsurance
   • CC must be able to take ceded reserve credit (hence trusts)
   • Assets in SPV for long time (30+ years)
   • Investor retains inv income from assets

[410] Q: List advantages of using LOCs for SPVs.
A: • Only annual redundancy amount needs LOC funding
   • Bank ↑ LOC amount each year for guaranteed fixed charge
     ◦ Avoids refinancing the LOC
     ◦ Better than bonds (would need reissue/refinance)
   • Long-term LOC costs ↓ as banks gain comfort

E.1.1 — Regulatory Capital Adequacy for Life Insurance Companies  (E.1 Regulation Topics)
----------------------------------------------------------------------

[411] Q: Define 3 types of capital.
• Regulatory required capital
• Available capital
• Target capital
A: • Regulatory required capital – Required for p/h protection
     ◦ Regulators represent p/h interests (solvency focused)
     ◦ Held in safe investments for adverse events
     ◦ Min thresholds trigger regulator intervention
   • Available capital – Used for growth, operational efficiency, or competitive
   positioning
   • Target capital – Reflects stakeholder perspectives:
     ◦ Stockholders: Seek gains; prefer less conservatism
     ◦ Debtholders: Prefer conservative capital
     ◦ Regulators/p/h: Prefer conservative capital
     ◦ Management: Balance; avoid excessive capital

[412] Q: Compare factor-based vs. model-based regulatory capital methods.
A: 1. Factor-based (US)
     ◦ RC requirements = factors × financial statement amounts
     ◦ Factors sometimes set using industry-wide models
     ◦ Aggregate RC = individual RCs combined via correlation matrix
   2. Model-based (Canada, EU, Bermuda)
     ◦ More actuary judgment
     ◦ Models subject to regulatory review/approval

[413] Q: Describe NAIC RBC and primary risk categories.
A: NAIC: Mostly factor-based with some model-based components
   • Many assumptions prescribed
   • Shift to principles-based → more model-based
   • Assets/liabilities measured under statutory accounting (conservative, mostly
   BV-based)
   4 primary risk categories:
   1. C0/C1: Asset Risk
   2. C2: Insurance Risk
   3. C3: Interest Rate/Market Risk
   4. C4: Business Risk

[414] Q: Describe NAIC RBC asset risk component (C0, C1).
A: • C0: Default risk for affiliated companies, OBS items (derivatives)
   • C1: Divided into C1cs (unaffiliated common stock) and C1o (all other)
   • C1o: Default risk for bonds, mortgages, RE, preferred stock
     ◦ RC = factor × BV of asset
     ◦ Factors ↑ as credit rating ↓ (0% for Treasuries, 30% for junk bonds)
   • Additional adjustments:
     ◦ Asset concentration factor × 10 largest holdings
     ◦ Diversification factor × total bond RC (factors ↓ as issuer count ↑)

[415] Q: Describe NAIC RBC insurance risk component (C2).
A: • C2mort: Risk DBs > expected
     ◦ RC = factor × NAR
     ◦ Factors vary by:
     ◦ Individual vs. group life
     ◦ Insurer’s ability to change NGEs
     ◦ Portfolio size (factor ↓ as inforce ↑)
   • C2long: Risk payout annuity benefits > expected
     ◦ RC = factor × annuity reserves
     ◦ Only life-contingent annuities in active payout
     ◦ Factors vary with reserve size
   • Final C2 reflects negative correlation between mortality/longevity:
   C2 = √(C2mort² + C2long² + 2 × C2mort × C2long × CorrFactor)

[416] Q: Describe NAIC RBC interest rate/market risk component (C3).
A: • C3a: Interest rate change risk (factor × reserves)
   • C3b: Health care capitation risk
   • C3c: Market risk
     ◦ C-3 Phase I: Certain fixed annuities, single premium life
     ◦ Model-based, stochastic projection with prescribed scenarios
     ◦ RC based on subset of worst scenarios
     ◦ C-3 Phase II: VAs
     ◦ Model-based, stochastic projection with prescribed scenarios
     ◦ Requires reserves at CTE 70
     ◦ RC = CTE 98 Reserves − CTE 70 Reserves
     ◦ Non-single premium life RC = factor × reserves

[417] Q: Describe NAIC RBC business risk component (C4).
A: • Covers OR and any other risk not in C0–C3
   • RC = factor × annual premiums (premiums gross of reinsurance)

[418] Q: Formula for NAIC authorized control level RBC and ratio?
A: Authorized control level (ACL) RBC = covariance-adjusted sum
   ACL RBC = C0 + C4a + √[(C1o + C3a)² + (C1cs + C3c)² + C2² + C3b² + C4b²]
   RBC ACL Ratio = Total Adjusted Capital / ACL RBC
   • C0 = Asset risk (affiliated amounts)
   • C1cs = Asset risk (unaffiliated common stock)
   • C1o = All other asset risk (excl. common stock)
   • C2 = Insurance risk
   • C3a = Interest rate risk
   • C3b = Health credit risk
   • C3c = Market risk
   • C4a = Business risk (non-health)
   • C4b = Health admin expense business risk

[419] Q: List US regulatory action levels.
A: Level RBC ACL Ratio Action
   Trend Test Corridor [200%, 300%) Company performs trend test
   Company Action [150%, 200%) Company prepares/submits RBC plan to regulator
   Regulatory Action [100%, 150%) Company submits RBC plan; regulator issues corrective
   action order
   Authorized Control [70%, 100%) Regulator authorized to take any actions necessary
   Mandatory Control <70% Company deemed insolvent, placed under regulatory control

[420] Q: Describe Canadian solvency regulation and available capital tiers.
A: Canadian insurers regulated at federal level by OSFI
   • Canadian assets/liabilities based on IFRS
   LICAT – significant capital assessment measure used by OSFI
   Available Capital = Tier 1 Capital + Tier 2 Capital
   • Tier 1 Capital = mostly shareholder equity, retained earnings (no limit)
   • Tier 2 Capital = assets not meeting Tier 1 criteria
     ◦ Hybrid instruments, subordinated debt
     ◦ Tier 2 must be positive and ≤ Tier 1

[421] Q: Write Canadian LICAT ratio formulas.
A: TR = (Available Capital + Surplus Allowance + Eligible Deposits) / Base Solvency Buffer
   CR = (Tier 1 Capital + 70% of Surplus Allowance + 70% of Eligible Deposits) / Base Solvency Buffer
   • Total Ratio: Focuses on p/h/creditor protection
   • Core Ratio: Focuses on financial strength
   • Surplus Allowance = PfADs calculated under:
     ◦ CALM for pre-2023 business
     ◦ IFRS 17 for business issued 2023+
   • Eligible Deposits = Collateral/LOCs from unregistered reinsurers + claims
   fluctuation reserves
   • Base Solvency Buffer (BSB) = 1-year CTE 99 min capital requirement

[422] Q: How is the Base Solvency Buffer determined under LICAT?
A: BSB for each of 6 geographic regions = sum of 5 risk components:
   1. Asset default risk (credit + market risk)
   2. Mortality/morbidity/lapse risks
   3. Interest rate risk
   4. Segregated funds risk
   5. Foreign exchange risk
   Each item calibrated to 1-year CTE 99 level
   Additional adjustments also apply:
   • Par products, diversification, reinsurance, collateral, hedges

[423] Q: List Canadian regulatory action levels.
A: Target Minimum
   Total Ratio 100% 90%
   Core Ratio 70% 55%
   • If either ratio approaches target → OSFI assesses, takes necessary actions
   • If either ratio < minimum → OSFI can take control
   • Companies must hold min $5M available capital regardless

[424] Q: Describe capital requirement levels under Solvency II.
A: EIOPA sets standards for EU members, including Solvency II guidance
   Solvency II establishes 2 capital requirement levels:
   1. Solvency Capital Requirement (SCR): 1-year 99.5% probability
     ◦ Based on Basic Solvency Capital Calculation or internal model
     ◦ Internal models subject to regulator approval
   2. Minimum Capital Requirement (MCR): Level requiring regulator intervention
     ◦ Set at 1-year 85% solvency probability

[425] Q: Describe asset/liability valuation under Solvency II.
A: • Stresses starting economic balance sheet (similar to LICAT)
   • Assets/liabilities are marked to model
     ◦ Model calibrated rather than directly from market data
   • Liabilities based on exit value
     ◦ Based on transfer/settlement value between 2 willing parties with equal information
     ◦ Difficult to determine: No trading exchanges, sparse M&A activity
     ◦ Often determined using discounted cash flow models
     ◦ Discount rate = risk-free rate with adjustments

[426] Q: Describe key components of Solvency II balance sheet.
A: • Assets = Assets Backing Tech. Provisions + Basic & Auxiliary Own Funds
     ◦ Assets Backing Technical Provisions = All Tier 1 Assets + Some Tier 2 Assets
     ◦ Assets Backing Technical Provisions = Technical Provisions (liability side)
     ◦ Basic/Auxiliary Own Funds = Remaining Tier 2 Assets + Tier 3 Assets
   • Liabilities & Surplus = Technical Provisions + ECR + Free Own Funds
     ◦ Technical Provisions = BEL + Risk Margin
     ◦ Enhanced Capital Requirement (ECR) = SCR + MCR
     ◦ Free Own Funds = any additional surplus assets held
   = Assets − (Technical Provisions + ECR)
   Basic own funds currently exist; auxiliary could come from parent

[427] Q: Describe MCR’s relationship to Basic SCR under Solvency II and SCR action levels.
A: Basic SCR reflects correlations across/within risk categories:
   Basic SCR = √(Σ(i,j) Corr_{i,j} × SCR_{i} × SCR_{j})
   SCR Ratio = Available Capital / SCR
   MCR must be between 25% and 45% of Basic SCR and ≥ 1.2M

[428] Q: Describe Solvency II capital tiers.
A: Tier 1 = highest quality; Tier 3 = lowest
   • At least 50% of SCR must be backed by Tier 1
   • Max 15% of SCR can be backed by Tier 3
   • At least 80% of MCR must be backed by Tier 1 capital, no Tier 3

[429] Q: Describe regulatory action levels under Solvency II.
A: Company approaching MCR must submit plan to regulator
   • Regulator must approve plan
   • If capital < SCR or MCR → regulator requires more capital
   • EU regulators have latitude to decide further actions
   Regulatory actions determined by SCR Ratio:
   Level SCR Ratio Action
   Early Intervention Process [25%, 45%) Increasingly severe prescribed supervisory actions
   as capital → 25%
   Minimum Required Level <25% Company loses authorization to operate

[430] Q: List Solvency II risk categories.
A: 1. Underwriting risks
     ◦ Non-life (P&C)
     ◦ Life (7 categories)
     ◦ Health
   2. Market risks
   2.1 Interest rate risk
   2.2 Equity Risk
   2.3 Property risk (RE)
   2.4 Spread risk (credit risk)
   2.5 Market risk concentration
   2.6 Currency risk
   3. Counterparty default
     ◦ Type 1: All risk mitigation exposures (e.g., reinsurers)
     ◦ Type 2: Future receivables from p/h, mortgage loans

[431] Q: List Solvency II life UW risk subcategories and shocks.
A: 1. Mortality risk: Life ins mortality +15%
   2. Longevity risk: Payout annuity mortality −20%
   3. Disability-morbidity risk: Stresses initial claims, time on claim
   4. Lapse risk: RC = worst of 3 stress tests:
     ◦ +50% permanent lapse shock
     ◦ −50% permanent lapse shock
     ◦ −40% PIF reduction
   5. Expense risk: Expenses +10% all years + inflation +1%
   6. Revision risk: Payout annuity benefits +3%
   7. Catastrophe risk: Add 0.15% to 1-year mortality rate
   All calcs based on FMV assumptions with possible adjustments:
   • Spread adjustment: Accounts for “irrational” market movements
   • Matching/volatility adjustment: Can be made to discount rates

[432] Q: Describe Bermuda regulatory balance sheet.
A: Capital requirements prescribed by Insurance Act, regulated by BMA
   • Assets = Assets Backing EBS Technical Provision + Surplus Assets
   • Liabilities + Surplus = EBS Technical Provision + ECR + Free Surplus
     ◦ EBS Technical Provision = BEL + Risk Margin
     ◦ Enhanced Capital Requirement (ECR) = max[BSCR, MSM]
     ◦ Expressed as MSM + Required Capital Over MSM
     ◦ BSCR calibrated to 99.5% solvency (similar to Solvency II)
   Minimum Margin for Solvency (MSM) = max of:
   1. 25% × ECR
   2. $1M BMD for Class 3A/3B insurers or $100M BMD for Class 4 insurers
   MSM available capital based on “Bermuda Statutory” financials (US GAAP , IFRS)

[433] Q: Describe target capital level under Bermuda regulation.
A: Target Capital Level (TCL) = 120%× ECR
   • TCL = amount of capital insurers expected to hold
   • TCL available capital based on economic balance sheet (EBS)
   • EBS assets are market value
   • EBS liabilities are fair value: BEL + Risk Margin
   • BEL uses discount rates based on either:
     ◦ Standard approach: Based on market-representative portfolio
     ◦ Scenario-based approach: Based on yields from company assets with adjustments
   • Risk Margin: 6% CoC approach discounted at prescribed risk-free rates

[434] Q: Outline the BSCR risk categories.
A: 1. Market risk
     ◦ Fixed income/equity risk: Prescribed factors × asset MV
     ◦ Interest rate risk (1 of 2 approaches):
   1.1 Duration-based: ( |Asset Duration − Liability Duration|) × 2% × Asset MV
   1.2 Shock-based: Prescribed interest rate shocks
     ◦ Currency risk: Prescribed shocks varying by currency
     ◦ Concentration risk: Double RC for 10 largest holdings from single issuer
   2. Long-term risk – Risk from long-term liability fluctuations
     ◦ Mortality risk: (Face − BEL) × NAR factor
     ◦ Stop loss risk: Prescribed % × premium
     ◦ Morbidity risk: Prescribed % × premium
     ◦ Longevity risk: Prescribed % × BEL
     ◦ VA risk: Standard approach or CTE 95 internal model
     ◦ Other long-term ins risk: Prescribed factor × BEL
   3. Credit risk – Primarily counterparty risk
   4. P&C risk

[435] Q: Describe Bermuda capital ratios and regulatory action levels.
A: ECR Ratio = BSCR Ratio = Available Capital / ECR
   TCL Ratio = Available Capital / TCL
   Regulatory actions determined by ECR Ratio:
   Level: Early Intervention Process — ECR Ratio [100%, 120%) — Company submits remediation plan, provides additional reporting
   Level: Minimum Required Level — ECR Ratio <100% — Company must improve capital position, faces wind-up

E.1.2 — Life and Annuity Non-forfeiture Practices  (E.1 Regulation Topics)
----------------------------------------------------------------------

[436] Q: Why does nonforfeiture law exist?
A: • Regulators want equitable treatment for persisters and lapsers
   • Regulators want p/h to receive fair share of equity on lapse

[437] Q: List key pricing concepts/risks for nonforfeiture.
A: • Policies must provide min nonforfeiture value by US law
   • Lapse risks:
     ◦ High lapses on high CSV products
     ◦ Low lapses on low/no CSV products (e.g., T -100, lapse-supported)
     ◦ Disintermediation: High lapses when rates ↑, asset MVs ↓
   • Nonforfeiture option utilization rates
   • Anti-selection: Mortality ↑ for options maximizing benefits/cost
   • Expense assumptions: Differ from regular policies, vary by option
   • Substandard risks:
     ◦ May have same CSV as standard
     ◦ Impact of later qualifying for standard

[438] Q: Compare RPU vs. ETI nonforfeiture options.
A: 1. Reduced Paid-Up (RPU)
     ◦ Lower face than original: CSV_{[x]+t} = (RPU Face)A_{[x]+t}
     ◦ Maximizes coverage period
     ◦ Low anti-selection
   2. Extended Term Insurance (ETI)
     ◦ Maximizes p/h face
     ◦ ETI expenses < RPU
     ◦ Policy terminates when CSV = 0
     ◦ Higher anti-selection (unless ETI is default)

[439] Q: List nonforfeiture options besides RPU and ETI.
A: 1. Automatic Premium Loan (APL)
     ◦ Preserves original contract, benefits, riders
     ◦ P/h can repay loan anytime
     ◦ Policy terminates if Loan Balance > CSV
     ◦ May entice p/h to stop paying premium
     ◦ DB = Face − Loan Amount
   2. Annuitization
     ◦ Convert CSV → payment stream (mostly DAs)
   3. Cash Surrender
     ◦ Equivalent cash value of nonforfeiture options
   4. Flexible UL Premium Payments

[440] Q: Describe prospective approach for calculating nonforfeiture values.
A: • Forward-looking PV based on expected future experience
   • Maintains parity between persisters/lapsers
   NF Value = PVFB + PVFE + PV(Profit Contribution) − PV(Gross Prems)
   • Net premium approach (common for traditional US products):
   NF Value = PVFB − PV(Level Net Premium)
   Level Net Premium % = [PV_{0}(FB) + PV_{0}(AcqCosts)] / PV_{0}(Gross Premiums)
   • Assumptions for future experience:
     ◦ Persister approach: Mortality only decrement
     ◦ Whole contract approach: Reflects p/h option to terminate/convert

[441] Q: Describe retrospective nonforfeiture approach.
A: • Backward-looking accumulation value:
   NF Value = Fund_{t} − Surrender Charge_{t}
   Fund_{t} = Fund_{t-1} + Prem_{t} + Interest_{t} − Ben Charges_{t} − Exp_{t} − Profit_{t}
   • No-lapse guarantee: Policy stays in force even if NF Value< 0

[442] Q: Compare prospective vs. retrospective approaches.
A: • Product types (by premium flexibility):
     ◦ Prospective: Works well for fixed premium products (WL, etc.)
     ◦ Retrospective: Works well for flexible premium products (UL, annuities, etc.)
   • Retrospective easier to explain to p/h
     ◦ Based on actual experience, not future assumptions
   • Prospective maintains more parity between persisters/lapsers
   • Prospective NF value changes if assumptions change (hard to explain)
   • Prospective NF value depends on policy class definitions

[443] Q: Compare US vs. non-US nonforfeiture practices.
A: 1. United States
     ◦ Permanent policies must have nonforfeiture value
     ◦ Balance different views on equity:
     ◦ Lapsers: Want max value
     ◦ Persisters: Want to pay as little as possible for lapsers
     ◦ Company: Wants compensation for risk/costs
     ◦ Agent: Wants compensation for sale (AcqCosts)
     ◦ Regulator: Maximize p/h return without threatening solvency
   2. Outside United States
     ◦ Unlike US, most countries do NOT have specific nonforfeiture laws
     ◦ Some countries (e.g., Canada) may file general NF plan with regulator
     ◦ United Kingdom:
     ◦ Most life products are variable
     ◦ Strong disclosure regulation

E.1.3 — Overview of Non-Guaranteed Elements  (E.1 Regulation Topics)
----------------------------------------------------------------------

[444] Q: How do NGEs differ from policyholder dividends?
A: Principal NGE characteristics:
   1. Affect cost of contractual benefits/policy values
   2. Not guaranteed except for min/max values
   3. Insurer can change unilaterally (subject to contractual/legal restrictions)
   NGE Examples: COI charges, credited rates, index parameters
   NGEs ≠ p/h dividends:
   • P/h dividends = par p/h share of par block profits
   • NGEs based on future experience expectations
   • P/h dividends based on actual past experience

[445] Q: List 4+ NGE examples.
A: All NGEs subject to max/min values under contract/regulation
   • Credited rates: Adjustable based on investment performance
   • Premium loads and other expenses
   • COI charges: Vary by UW class, sex, age, policy year
     ◦ Guaranteed max rates comply with SNL/tax law
     ◦ Guaranteed rates may be higher if anticipated mortality > SNL mortality
   • Index parameters: Adjustable caps/participation rates
   • Nonguaranteed bonuses: AV credits or charge refunds under certain conditions
   • VA rider charges for enhanced GLB riders
   • Indeterminate premiums: Traditional products with guaranteed/nonguaranteed
   premium scales

[446] Q: List actuarial considerations for original NGE determination.
A: Actuary must balance profitability with consumer needs
   • Expected mortality:
     ◦ Varies with UW, target market, risk classes, policy sizes
   • COI charges relative to expected mortality
   • Policy load/expense charges:
     ◦ How to cover policy issue expenses
     ◦ Portion of premium covering costs
     ◦ Revenue source for flexible premium products
   • Basis for credited interest:
     ◦ Whether investment yields cover credited interest/other costs
     ◦ New money vs. portfolio method
   • Assess assumptions via sensitivity testing:
     ◦ Impact to profitability/competitiveness (individually and aggregate)

[447] Q: List actuarial considerations when revising NGEs.
A: NGE revisions allowed if experience doesn’t align with AEFs set in original pricing
   • Insurers have faced lawsuits:
     ◦ Changes not allowed by contract
     ◦ Changes calculated inappropriately
     ◦ Changes recouping past losses
   • Trend vs. variance:
     ◦ Future NGE changes result from variances from expected values
     ◦ NGE assumptions should already anticipate experience trends
     ◦ If actual trend aligns with expected → no changes needed
   • Insurers can provide illustrations of possible future values to p/h

[448] Q: Define the following from ASOP 2:
• NGE Framework
• Determination Policy
• Policy Classes
A: NGE Framework = combination of:
   • Company’s determination policy
   • Method to define policy class
   • Any other relevant criteria/principles for determining NGEs
   Determination policy = insurer’s governing principles/objectives for NGEs
   • Could be single document or collection of documents
   • Includes profitability/capital objectives/requirements
   • Specifies NGE review frequency with max time between reviews
   Policy classes = policies grouped together when determining NGEs
   • Different NGEs can have different class definitions
     ◦ Must appropriately reflect differences within AEFs
   • Classes not expected to change after issue
     ◦ Can be redefined if supported by new information

[449] Q: List ASOP 2 considerations for NGE scale determination/revision.
A: 1. Review prior determinations (including original):
     ◦ If not available, use reasonable approach to reconstruct
     ◦ Consider whether NGE scales consistent with policy language
   2. Analyze emerging experience vs. anticipated from prior determination:
     ◦ Expectation: Do not revise unless AEFs change
   3. Consider whether to recommend NGE scale revision:
     ◦ Not purely mathematical (consider multiple criteria)
     ◦ Other stakeholders: Senior mgmt, marketing, legal, admin
   4. If doing revision, determine revised NGE scales:
     ◦ Should be purely prospective: Focus on future profitability
     ◦ Do NOT recoup past losses or distribute past gains

[450] Q: List 3 ASOPs assisting with NGE data considerations.
A: • ASOP 23 data considerations:
     ◦ Actuaries must review data (or disclose why they didn’t)
     ◦ NOT required to audit data
     ◦ Disclose data limitations and implications
   • ASOP 2 data considerations:
     ◦ Use professional judgment if recreating past determinations
     ◦ Disclose any reliance on others
     ◦ Must still review data and methods/models using data
   • ASOP 25: Guidance on credibility considerations

[451] Q: Describe actuarial guidance for NGE modeling.
A: ASOP 56: Guidance on modeling (developing, modifying, reviewing, etc.)
   Ideally start with original or past determination model
   If prior model not available/usable:
   • Start with model designed for another purpose, modify it
   • Example: CFT models for asset adequacy testing
     ◦ May be done at aggregate level with simplifications
     ◦ May include PADs not appropriate for NGE determination

[452] Q: Outline Model 582 requirements for basic illustrations.
A: Basic illustration: Sent to all applicants for policies subject to Model 582
   • Must include numeric summary of DBs, policy values, premium outlay
   • Must be created under 3 bases:
   1. Policy guarantees (least favorable)
   2. Insurer’s illustrated scale (most favorable allowed)
   3. Insurer’s illustrated scale with reduced NGEs (in between)
     ◦ Based on avg of guaranteed values and illustrated scale values
   • Illustrated NGEs cannot be more favorable than illustrated scale
     ◦ Do not project NGE improvements
     ◦ Label all NGEs “nonguaranteed”
   • Illustrated scale ≤ lesser of:
     ◦ Disciplined current scale (DCS)
     ◦ Currently payable scale (current/actual scale)
   • Lapse-supported illustrations NOT allowed

[453] Q: List Model 582 requirements for disciplined current scale.
A: DCS = scale based on insurer’s recenthistorical experience
   • Illustration Actuary (IA) must certify Model 582 compliance
   • Reflect only insurer actions already taken
   • Can NOT include projected experience improvements
   • Assumed expenses ≥ minimums defined in Model 582
   Model 582 points to ASOP 24 for guidance on IA’s work
   • Defines parameters of DCS (NGE scale) certified annually by IA
   DCS must also pass self-support test (SST):
   • Passed if accumulated policy CFs ≥ illustrated CSV
   • Must pass at every illustrated point starting at 15th anniversary
     ◦ Second-to-die policies: Test 20th anniversary and later
   • Cash flows based on DCS assumptions

[454] Q: Describe Model 582 Lapse-Supported Test.
A: Lapse-supported illustrations not allowed
   • IA must certify illustrations are NOT lapse-supported
   • Certification based on lapse support test
   • Lapse support test = SST with modified lapse assumption:
     ◦ Years 1–5: Same lapse rates as SST
     ◦ Years 6+: Assume 100% persistency (0% lapses)

[455] Q: List provisions of NY Regulation 210.
A: • Effective March 2018 for policies issued in NY (inforce and new business)
   • More prescriptive (strict) than ASOP 2
   • Key requirements:
     ◦ Max time between NGE reviews ≤ 5 years
     ◦ Formally documented, board-approved NGE determination policy
     ◦ Various specific reinsurance considerations
     ◦ Waiting period for filings and disclosures to p/h

[456] Q: Describe regulation of NGEs in Canada.
A: ICA Requirements for NGEs:
   • Insurer/board must set policies for dividends/bonuses and criteria for adjusting policy values
   • Maintain separate par accounts
   • Actuary reports annually to board on fairness of par management and adjustable criteria
   CIA Edu. Note: Fairness Opinions
   • Define experience factor classes at issue
   • Apply policies consistently, based on objective methods
   • Align with policy/board provisions; avoid unreasonable pooling/cross-subsidies
   • Document support for fairness opinions

[457] Q: Describe regulation of NGEs in Asia.
A: Singapore
   • MAS Notice 320: Internal governance for charges/expenses, par disclosures,
   board/senior mgmt/AA roles
   • SoP L03: Guidance on fund policy management
   Hong Kong
   • Guideline 16: Fair treatment, AA advises board on p/h expectations (no
   prescribed NGE approach)
   • "Green light process" for unit-linked: Benchmark charges, ensure "treating
   customers fairly"
   Japan: One ASOP requires AA to confirm adequate funding of par policies

[458] Q: Describe regulation of NGEs in United Kingdom.
A: • Most current NGE regulation created in early 2000s
     ◦ Primary focus: “TCF principle” (treat customers fairly)
     ◦ Requires communication/disclosures to p/h around NGE determination
   • Principles and Practices of Financial Management (PPFM) document
     ◦ Published by UK insurers with with-profits (par) business
     ◦ Outlined in =Financial Conduct Authority’s Conduct of Business Sourcebook”
     ◦ Describes how NGEs are managed
     ◦ Detail varies by insurer but generally high-level
     ◦ Can be amended anytime with written notice to p/h

[459] Q: List data/information challenges for NGEs.
A: • Original product pricing often unavailable or insufficiently detailed
     ◦ ASOP 2: OK to reconstruct if needed
   • Experience data considerations/challenges:
     ◦ Sufficient granularity of experience studies
     ◦ Experience credibility
     ◦ Whether anticipated experience factors supported by emerging experience
     ◦ Materiality of experience changes
     ◦ Sensitivity of profit to assumption changes
     ◦ Availability of p/h behavior experience (premium persistency, lapse, etc.)
   • No industry standard for evaluating AEF changes
     ◦ ASOP 2: Changing NGE scales should NOT ↑ expected profit vs. original
   expectations

E.2.1 — Earnings Measures  (E.2 Earnings and Value Measurement)

[460] Q: Formulas for product cash flow and solvency earnings?
A: ProdCashFlow(t) = Prem(t) − Ben(t) − Exp(t)
   PreTaxSolvEarn(t) = ProdCashFlow(t) + InvIncome(t) − SolvResIncr(t)
   AfterTaxSolvEarn(t) = PreTaxSolvEarn(t) − Tax(t)

[461] Q: Formulas for distributable and stockholder earnings?
A: Distributable Earnings
   DistEarn(t) = AfterTaxSolvEarn(t) − ReqCapIncr(t) + ATInvIncRC(t)
   Stockholder Earnings (U.S. GAAP Earnings)
   PreTaxStockEarn(t) = ProdCashFlow(t) + InvIncome(t) − BenResIncr(t)
   − DACAmort(t) + InvIncRC(t)
   = PreTaxSolvEarn(t) + SolvResIncr(t) − BenResIncr(t)
   − DACAmort(t) + InvIncRC(t)
   AfterTaxStockEarn(t) = PreTaxStockEarn(t) − Tax(t) − TaxInvIncRC(t) − DefTaxProv(t)

[462] Q: Formula for annual ROE?
A: Annual ROE_{t} = AfterTaxStockEarn_{t} / EquityBase_{t}
   EquityBase = StockAssets − StockLiab
   Stock Assets = SolvRes + ReqCap + DAC
   StockLiab = BenRes + DefTaxLiab
   Alternatively, equity base can be mid-year:
   MidYearEquityBase_{t} = (EquityBase_{t} + EquityBase_{t-1}) / 2

[463] Q: List considerations for selecting profit goals.
A: 1. Select accounting basis:
     ◦ Usually solvency (Statutory) ñ distributable earnings
     ◦ May also use stockholder basis (GAAP)
   2. Determine how to reflect risk:
     ◦ Formula-based: Function of risk (mortality, expense, investment)
     ◦ Judgment-based
     ◦ Sensitivity tests (recommended)
   3. Interest/discount rate considerations:
     ◦ Cost of capital = weighted avg of company’s capital sources
     ◦ Opportunity cost = return on alternative ventures with similar risk
     ◦ Current/expected future capital position
     ◦ Discounting use:
     ◦ After-tax usually more appropriate than pre-tax
     ◦ Use CoC if determining whether product produces acceptable return

E.2.2 — Taxes  (E.2 Earnings and Value Measurement)
----------------------------------------------------------------------

[464] Q: Pre-tax earnings formulas for solvency and stockholder?
A: ProdCashFlow(t) = Prem(t) − Ben(t) − Exp(t)
   PreTaxSolvEarn(t) = ProdCashFlow(t) + InvIncome(t) − SolvResIncr(t)
   PreTaxStockEarn(t) = ProdCashFlow(t) + InvIncome(t) − BenResIncr(t)
   − DACAmort(t) + InvIncRC(t)
   = PreTaxSolvEarn(t) + SolvResIncr(t) − BenResIncr(t)
   − DACAmort(t) + InvIncRC(t)
   = ProdCashFlow(t) + InvIncome(t) − EarnResIncr(t) + InvIncRC(t)

[465] Q: Compare timing vs. permanent differences.
A: 1. Timing differences: Differences in earnings that will eventually reverse
     ◦ Most common (usually most significant) example:
   TimingDiff(t) = SolvResIncr(t) − TaxResIncr(t)
     ◦ Tax authorities prefer higher tax revenue (higher assets, lower liabilities)
   2. Permanent differences: Differences that will not reverse
     ◦ Example: Non-deductible investment income
   PermDiff(t) = − InvIncome(t) × NonTaxInvPct(t)

[466] Q: Formulas for taxable earnings and tax on earnings?
A: TaxableEarn(t) = PreTaxSolvEarn(t) + TimingDiff(t) + PermDiff(t)
   = PreTaxSolvEarn(t) + SolvResIncr(t) − TaxResIncr(t) + PermDiff(t)
   = ProdCashFlow(t) + InvIncome(t) − TaxResIncr(t) + PermDiff(t)
   Tax(t) = TaxRate × TaxableEarn(t)

[467] Q: How can negative taxable earnings affect profitability?
A: At company level, total tax cannot be negative
   • New products generate negative earnings
   • Smaller insurers may not be able to deduct first year losses
     ◦ Results in higher capital, lower ROI

[468] Q: Describe US tax treatment for capital gains/losses.
A: • Capital gains: Immediately taxable
   • Capital losses: Can only offset capital gains
   • Capital losses can be carried forward to offset future capital gains
     ◦ Creates timing difference
   • If carryforwards not used within 3 years → expire
     ◦ Creates permanent difference

[469] Q: Describe tax on “investment income less expenses.”
A: Attempts to tax annual ↑ in p/h value and insurer earnings
   InvIncome(t) − Exp(t) = Ben(t) + SolvResIncr(t) − Prem(t) + PreTaxSolvEarn(t)
   IETax(t) = IETaxRate × [InvIncome(t) − Exp(t)]
   Some countries may use this or use as minimum tax
   NOT used in US or Canada

[470] Q: Describe tax on capital.
A: Certain types of capital may be subject to tax (e.g., par surplus)
   TaxOnCap(t) = TaxCapital(t) × CapTaxRate(t)
   Could be assessed in addition to tax on earnings or used as minimum tax

[471] Q: Describe purpose and function of DAC tax.
A: • Temporarily ↑ taxable income as premiums collected
   • Reversed over next 10 years
   • DACTaxAmount(t) = additions to DAC tax − deductions from DAC tax
   TaxableEarn(t) = PreTaxSolvEarn(t) + TimingDiff(t) + PermDiff(t) + DACTaxAmount(t)
   For each premium collected (any policy year) for life insurance:
   • Year 1: Add 7.7% to Taxable Earnings; Deduct 5% of 7.7%
   • Years 2−10: Add 0; Deduct 10% of 7.7%
   • Year 11: Add 0; Deduct 5% of 7.7%

[472] Q: Describe tax treatment under US GAAP (stockholder) accounting.
A: GAAP reports taxes on accrued basis
   Tax(t) = TaxRate × TaxableEarnings
   AccruedTax(t) = TaxRate × PreTaxStockEarn(t)
   DefTaxProv(t) = AccruedTax(t) − Tax(t)
   AfterTaxStockEarn(t) = PreTaxStockEarn(t) − AccruedTax(t)
   = (1 − TaxRate) × PreTaxStockEarn(t)
   DefTaxLiab(t) = DefTaxLiab(t − 1) + DefTaxProv(t)
   = TaxRate × [TaxRes(t) − EarnRes(t)]
   Causes taxes to be level % of pre-tax stockholder earnings
   DefTaxLiab = liability (or asset if negative) for future tax payments

E.2.4 — Understanding Profitability in Life Insurance  (E.2 Earnings and Value Measurement)
----------------------------------------------------------------------

[473] Q: Describe 4 accounting-based profitability indicators:
1. Book Value Per Share
2. Price-to-Book Ratio
3. ROE
4. Operating Margin
A: 1. Book Value Per Share = \frac{Book Value}{Total Shares}
   2. Price-to-Book Ratio = \frac{Share Price}{Book Value Per Share}
   3. ROE = \frac{Shareholder Profits}{Shareholder Equity}
     ◦ Advantages: Well understood, common
     ◦ Disadvantages: Meaningful only at portfolio level
   4. Operating Margin = \frac{Operating Profits}{Net Premiums}
     ◦ Advantage: Easy comparisons
     ◦ Disadvantages: Doesn’t reflect timing, CoC, or riskiness

[474] Q: Describe 3 accounting-based profitability indicators:
1. Benefits Ratio
2. Lapse Ratio
3. General Expense Ratio
A: 1. Benefits Ratio = \frac{P/h Benefits + ∆Reserves}{Prems + Other Charges and Income}
     ◦ Advantages: Measures mortality/morbidity; easy to understand
     ◦ Disadvantages: Short-term focus is misleading
   2. Lapse Ratio = \frac{Lapsed Policies or Face}{In-Force Policies or Face}
     ◦ Advantage: Indicates net persistency
     ◦ Disadvantages: May not accurately reflect timing
   3. General Expense Ratio = \frac{General Operating Exp}{Net Prems}
     ◦ Advantages: Cross-company comparisons
     ◦ Disadvantages: Meaningful only for comparing similar business

[475] Q: Describe 2 accounting-based profitability indicators:
1. Return on Assets (ROA)
2. Net Investment Result
A: 1. Return on Assets (ROA) = Profit / Total Company Assets
     ◦ Advantages: Easy to calculate; common for DAs
     ◦ Disadvantages: Not good for life products; may be volatile
   2. Net Investment Result = Inv Gains − Impairments
     ◦ Good for products where this is important earnings source

[476] Q: List disadvantages of accounting-based indicators.
A: • Statutory focus is solvency, not income
   • Rules vary throughout world
   • Volatility of GAAP and IFRS earnings
   • Poor at comparing new and existing business
   • Not usually meaningful at product level
   • Assumptions are locked in
   • No accounting for CoC

[477] Q: Describe MCEV (market-consistent embedded value).
A: MCEV = VIF + Required Capital + Free Surplus
   VIF = PV Future Profits − Value of Options and Guarantees
   − Cost of Non-Hedgeable Risks − Frictional Cost of Req Cap
   Req Cap = MV of Cap Allocated to Support Business
   Free Surplus = MV of Any Extra Allocated Cap
   • Assets based on trading value
   • Liabilities valued using replicating portfolios
   • VIF highly sensitive to assumption changes
   • More downside interest rate risk (asymmetric)
   • Mortality and morbidity risk can be hedged (symmetric)
   • May be trumped by emerging standards (Solvency II, IFRS)

[478] Q: List components of MCEV earnings analysis.
A: 1. New business
   2. Unwinding of existing business
   3. Experience variances and assumption changes
   4. Economic variances

[479] Q: Describe 3 MCEV-based profitability indicators:
• MCEV
• MCEV Earnings Analysis
• Value of In-Force (VIF)
A: 1. MCEV
     ◦ Advantages: Measures long-term value; harmonized
     ◦ Disadvantages: May be volatile or not understood by investors
   2. MCEV Earnings Analysis
     ◦ Advantages: Understand MCEV drivers
     ◦ Disadvantages: Can be volatile
   3. Value of In-Force (VIF)
     ◦ Advantages: Shows BV net of relevant costs
     ◦ Disadvantages: Can be volatile

[480] Q: Describe 3 MCEV-based profitability indicators:
• Value of New Business (VNB)
• New Business Margin
• Return on MCEV
A: 1. Value of New Business (VNB)
     ◦ Advantages: Understand value of NB
     ◦ Disadvantages: May be overly optimistic
   2. New Business Margin = \frac{VNB}{NB Prems}
     ◦ Advantages: Compare to expected profit margins
     ◦ Disadvantages: May be overly optimistic
   3. Return on MCEV = \frac{MCEV Earnings}{MCEV}
     ◦ Advantages: Complements ROE
     ◦ Disadvantages: May be overly optimistic

[481] Q: List primary earnings sources and risk factors for insurers.
A: 1. UW Results – mortality, health, lapse products
     ◦ Worse-than-expected claims or surrenders
     ◦ Adverse selection and moral hazard
     ◦ Frequent product churning
   2. Inv Results – savings products
     ◦ Insurer’s asset allocation
     ◦ Market performance
     ◦ Level of guarantees
     ◦ Inv expertise
     ◦ P/h loss sharing
     ◦ Duration mismatch
   3. Inv Mgmt Fee Income – unit-linked products
     ◦ Market volatility
     ◦ P/h behavior

E.3.1 — LIME Ch. 1  (E.3 Policyholder Taxation)
----------------------------------------------------------------------

[482] Q: How are life insurance DBs treated for tax purposes?
A: Full DB is tax-free under §101(a) if there is an insurable interest
   • Insurable interest may not exist for
     ◦ Employer-owned life insurance (COLI)
     ◦ Transfers for value (e.g., life settlements)
   • If DB is annuitized, the DB proportion of payments (level %) is not taxed
   DB / Σ Expected Annuity Payments = Non-Taxable % of Each Payment

[483] Q: Define lifetime distribution.
A: Lifetime distributions result when CSV is brought outside the contract before death
   • Surrenders (partial or full), policy loans, dividends, maturities

[484] Q: Describe tax treatment of inside buildup.
A: 7702 and 7702A determine how the inside buildup is taxed before death
   • Never taxed on a 7702-compliant contract if CSV stays inside through death
   • If a distribution occurs, taxation depends on MEC status (7702A)
     ◦ Non-MECs: FIFO (best) – cost basis comes out first
     ◦ MECs: LIFO (worst) – gains come out first
     ◦ Additional 10% penalty tax on the gain
     ◦ Policy loans trigger gains just like surrenders (never for non-MECs)
     ◦ Taxed portion of policy loan ↑ inv in the contract
   • Gain inside the contract = 7702 CSV − Inv in the Contract
     ◦ Inv in the Contract = Prems Paid − Previous Distributions Excl From Income
     ◦ CSV ignores surrender charges and policy loans
     ◦ Actual surrender charges incurred are netted out of policy gain

[485] Q: Describe tax treatment of:
• Premiums paid and interest paid on policy loans
• 1035 contract exchanges
• Contract sales
• Gifted life insurance
• Deduction of loss on surrender or sale
A: • Prems paid and interest paid on policy loans – not tax deductible
   • 1035 contract exchanges – does not trigger taxable gain
     ◦ Cost basis carries over
     ◦ Works for any combination of life, annuity, or QLTCI contract exchanges
   • Contract sales (e.g., life settlements)
     ◦ Seller’s taxable gain = price received− cost basis
     ◦ Cost basis = prems paid − cumulative COI
     ◦ Buyer’s cost basis = price paid + future prems− future untaxed distributions
   • Gifted life insurance – always tax-free
   • Deduction of loss on surrender or sale – not tax deductible

[486] Q: How did DEFRA and TAMRA affect life insurance taxation?
A: DEFRA (1984): §7702 – applies to all contracts entered after 1984
   • Requires a contract to be life insurance under “applicable law”
   • Set definitional limitations (actuarial tests) to limit inv orientation
     ◦ Contracts must satisfy CVAT or GPT / CV Corridor Test
     ◦ Limits CSV relative to DB and/or prems paid
   TAMRA (1988): §7702A – applies to contracts entered after 6/20/1988
   • Defined the MEC, which mainly prevented abuse of UL as a tax shelter
     ◦ MEC = 7702 contract that fails 7702A’s 7-pay test
     ◦ Taxation of distributions is much less favorable than non-MEC
   • Amended 7702’s rules for mortality charges

[487] Q: List classes of life insurance under §7702 and §7702A.
A: 1. Non-MEC Life Insurance Contracts
     ◦ Passes 7702 and 7702A
     ◦ DB is tax-free and tax on inside buildup is deferred
     ◦ Inv comes out first when distributed (FIFO)
   2. MECs: Investment-Oriented Contracts
     ◦ Passes 7702 but fails 7702A
     ◦ DB is tax-free and tax on inside buildup is deferred
     ◦ Gains come out first when distributed (LIFO)
     ◦ Additional 10% penalty unless age ≥ 59½ or disabled
     ◦ Policy loans taxed
   3. Failed Contracts: "Short-Term Endowments" (Rare)
     ◦ Fails 7702
     ◦ NAR portion of DB is tax-free
     ◦ Inside buildup taxed annually
     ◦ Insurer’s tax reserve deduction is limited

[488] Q: What is §7702’s applicable law requirement?
A: Requires a contract to be life insurance by law in the jurisdiction where issued
   • Includes state law or foreign law
   • Canada is the only other country that defines life insurance for tax purposes
   • FATCA informs IRS about foreign-issued contracts owned by US taxpayers
   7702 requires insurable interest
   • P/h has an interest in the continued life of the insured
   • Must be present for tax-free DBs

[489] Q: Define tax law terms:
• Statutes
• Regulations
• IRS pronouncements
• Legislative history
• Case law
• Private letter rulings
A: Courts ruling 7702/7702A issues will act in the following order
   1. Look at the relevant statute (DEFRA, TAMRA, etc.)
   2. Look at regulations if any exist
     ◦ IRS pronouncements are official IRS interpretations of the Code
     ◦ Generally have same effect as law
     ◦ Includes notices, revenue rulings, and revenue procedures
   3. Look at the statute’s legislative history
     ◦ Formal legislative history – formal congressional reports and colloquies
     ◦ Blue books – bigger collection for each law but not as binding
   4. Follow any binding case law – legal precedents established by courts
   Private letter rulings – IRS decisions on situations related to a specific taxpayer
   • Indicates IRS’s thinking but have no precedential value

E.3.2–E.3.4 — LIME Ch. 2: Overview, Assumptions & Calculation Methods (7702/7702A)  (E.3 Policyholder Taxation)
----------------------------------------------------------------------

[490] Q: Describe CVAT (Cash Value Accumulation Test).
A: Policy passes if: CSV_{t} ≤ NSP_{t} at all durations
   • Prospective test: must pass by the terms of the contract
     ◦ Contract must be designed to pass now and in the future
   • NSP_{t} = PV_{t}(DB) + PV_{t}(Endow) + PV_{t}(QAB Charges)
     ◦ Interest Rate = max[4%, Guaranteed Rate in Contract]
     ◦ Mortality assumption: use 100% prevailing CSO (Notice 2006-95 safe harbor)
   • CSV = CV ignoring SCs, policy loans, & reasonable termination dividends
     ◦ CSV also equals any amount p/h can borrow against
     ◦ Pricing consideration: remove as much uncertainty around CSV as possible

[491] Q: Describe Guideline Premium / Cash Value Corridor Test.
A: Must satisfy BOTH GPT and CV Corridor Test
   Guideline Premium Limitation: Prems Paid ≤ max(GSP, ΣGLPs)
   • Retrospective test: requires measurement of actual events
   • Prems paid = prems actually paid into the contract less:
     ◦ Non-taxable distributions (inv portion)
     ◦ Force outs (prems returned within 60 days to ensure GPT compliance)
   Cash Value Corridor Test: DB_{t} ≥ CSV_{t} × Corridor Factor_{t}
   • Set by law: Ages 0–40 factor = 250%; for ages 41–95, factors grade to 100%

[492] Q: Describe guideline premium limitation and calculation.
A: Prems Paid ≤ max(GSP, ΣGLPs);
   GSP = [PV(DB) + PV(Endow) + PV(QAB Charges) + PV(Exp)] / (1 − PremLoad%)
   GLP = ([PV(DB) + PV(Endow) + PV(QAB Charges) + PV(Exp)] / ä_{x}) × (1 / (1 − PremLoad%))
   • Mortality assumption: use 100% prevailing CSO (Notice 2006-95 safe harbor)
   • GSP interest rate = max[6%, Rate Guaranteed in Contract]
   • GLP interest rate = max[4%, Rate Guaranteed in Contract]
   • Expense assumption: use reasonable exp expected to be charged

[493] Q: List considerations for choosing a 7702 test.
A: Strengths of CVAT = Weaknesses of GPT / Corridor Testing
   . . . and vice versa
   Normally choose 1 method and use for contract’s life
   Reasons to choose CVAT
   • Good for traditional products
   • Easier to ensure prospective compliance
   • Simpler (only 1 test)
   • Requires much less record-keeping
   Reasons to choose GPT and CV corridor test
   • Better for flexible prem products (e.g., UL)
   • Errors are less likely to affect all policies; easier to deal with

[494] Q: Describe 7-Pay Test.
A: Policy is not a MEC if. . .
   Amount Paid ≤ (7-Pay Prem) × t
   7-Pay Prem = \frac{PV(Future Benefits)}{ä_{x:7|}}
   = \frac{CVAT NSP_{0}}{ä_{x:7|}}
   in most cases
   • Interest, mortality, and future benefits: same as CVAT
     ◦ Policies designed to be funded at 7-pay limit usually pass CVAT
   • Exclude exp (same as CVAT)
   • If DB < 10,000, add $75 to 7-pay prem
   • Aggregation rule – combine all policies on same insured when testing

[495] Q: Describe 7702 treatment of:
• Multi-year interest rate guarantees
• Policyholder dividends
• Interaction with SNL
• Net rate plans
A: • Interest rate guarantees
     ◦ Include any initial interest guarantees
     ◦ Include any guaranteed bonus interest
     ◦ Disregard short-term guarantees after first year
   • Interest rates assumed do not include p/h dividends
     ◦ P/h dividend = ANY nonguaranteed element (IRC §808)
   • SNL and product design challenges
     ◦ Nonforfeiture interest rates have fallen steadily since 1980s
     ◦ The 4% and 6% 7702/7702A minimums have not changed
   • Net-rate plans: guaranteed interest rate should include implied mortality charges

[496] Q: Describe history leading to current mortality assumption regulation for future benefits.
A: Current safe harbor = 100% 2001 CSO
   Contracts Issued 1988 or Later (History of Changes):
   • TAMRA’s “reasonable” mortality standard created confusion
     ◦ Permanent mortality rule: max mortality = 100% prevailing CSO table
     ◦ Interim mortality rule said “reasonable” meant “expected to be imposed”
   • Notice 88-128 safe harbor = 100% of sex-distinct 1980 CSO
   • Notice 2006-95 safe harbor = 100% 2001 CSO (M/F SM/NS)
     ◦ Same mortality as current SNL
     ◦ In effect for all post-2008 contracts
     ◦ Grandfathers 1980 CSO contracts unless there are unusual changes
   Contracts Issued Before 1988: Use mortality guaranteed in contract

[497] Q: How are expense charges regulated by 7702?
A: “Expense charges” refers to QAB charges and guideline prem exp
   For contracts issued after 1988:
   • Include exp that company reasonably expects to actually charge
   • Use actual exp even if < guaranteed

[498] Q: List 4 computational rules under 7702.
A: 1. Assumed DBs cannot increase
     ◦ Option 1 NAR should ó through maturity
   2. Deemed maturity must be age 95–100
     ◦ Even if actual maturity age < 95
     ◦ Even if ending mortality table age > 100
     ◦ Limits inv orientation (no short-term endowments)
   3. Assume DBs until deemed maturity
     ◦ Allows partial endowments before age 95
   4. Assumed endowment ≤ smallest future DB
     ◦ “Endowment” includes CSV at maturity
     ◦ Prohibits endowments > DB

[499] Q: What are alternate DB rules under 7702?
A: Alternative rules "override" the non-increasing DB rule
   1. Increasing Death Benefits (e.g., option 2 UL contracts)
     ◦ Increasing DBs are allowed in the GLP calculation only
     ◦ The increases must only be used to keep the NAR from decreasing
     ◦ Guideline limitation = greater of:
       ▸ GSP assuming a non-increasing DB
       ▸ ΣGLPs assuming an increasing DB
     ◦ Least endowment rule – restricts option 2 endowment to be ≤ specified amount
   2. Net Level Reserve Test
     ◦ Allows a net level reserve test in place of CVAT
   CSV_{t} ≤ Net Level Reserve_{t}
     ◦ Based on a NL prem paid through age 95+ and assumes NAR does not increase
     ◦ Not well suited for contracts that allow face adjustments

[500] Q: What QABs are allowed under 7702, and how are they reflected in definitional
limitations?
A: QABs Specifically Listed Under 7702
   1. Guaranteed insurability riders
   2. Accidental death and disability (AD&D) riders
   3. Family term coverage – “family” means base insured, spouse, or child
   4. Disability waiver – waiver of prems or monthly deductions (UL)
   Include PV(QAB Charges) in CVAT NSP, GSP, GLP, and 7-Pay
   • “Charges” = actual charges expected
   • Effectively raises the limitations to allow for higher prems paid due to the QAB

[501] Q: Describe adjustment rules under 7702.
A: Changes are generally allowed if
   • Initiated by p/h (e.g., DB change)
   • Allowed by operation of the contract (e.g., PUA purchase)
   • NOT initiated by company
   Contractual changes have 2 potential effects
   1. May require recalculation of definitional limits
     ◦ CVAT is “self-adjusting” – recalculate based on new current DB
     ◦ Still must pass prospectively
     ◦ Guideline prems are more restricted
     ◦ Recalculate GSP and GLP as of current attained age
     ◦ Accumulate new GLP going forward
   2. May result in loss of grandfathered status
     ◦ Example: a 1980 CSO contract could become 2001 CSO
     ◦ Usually not an issue as long as change is allowed in contract

[502] Q: List 4+ adjustments allowed under guideline prem test.
A: 1. Changes in DB or endowment benefits requested by p/h
   2. A DB change by operation of the contract that was not reflected due to
   computational rules
     ◦ Example: can’t assume increasing DBs, but p/h might do it anyway
   3. Purchase of PUAs
   4. Addition/deletion of QAB
   5. Change in DB option (1 → 2)
   6. Change in UW class or rating

[503] Q: What rules govern adjustment events for 7-Pay Test?
A: 1. Reduction-in-benefits rule
     ◦ Must recalculate 7-pay prem from issue if DB reduced during first 7 years
     ◦ Retroactive – assume the contract always had the lower DB
     ◦ Same for scheduled and unscheduled changes
     ◦ If amount paid > new 7-pay limitation → MEC
     ◦ Best practice: if a DB reduction is scheduled, reflect from the outset
     ◦ Last-to-die contracts: rule applies even beyond year 7
   2. Material change rule (for events other than DB reductions)
     ◦ Triggered after any change to benefits or terms used in original 7-pay
     ◦ Applies any time during life of the contract (even beyond 7th year)
     ◦ Start a new 7-year test period using a new, "adjusted" 7-pay prem:
   7-pay(Adj) = (NSP − CSV) / ä_{x:7}

[504] Q: How is option 2 GLP calculated?
A: Option 2 DB = Face + Cash Value
   A_{x}^{DBO2} = (\sum_{t=1}^{100-x} v^{t} q_{x+t-1}) + v^{100-x}
   GLP_{x}^{DBO2} = \frac{Face · A^{DBO2} + PV(Exp)}{ä_{100-x|}} × \frac{1}{1 − PremExp%}
   Exp and QAB charges should also be discounted with interest only
   CVAT NSP, GSP, and 7-pay always use the option 1 approach

[505] Q: What approaches can be used to calculate monthly mortality and COI rates?
A: • Monthly Mortality Rates – Can be arithmetic or exponential
   q(m) =
     q/12   if arithmetic
     1 − p^{1/12}   if exponential
     ◦ Practically equal until age 55, then exponential grows much higher
     ◦ Arithmetic approach produces lower option 1 prems
     ◦ Exponential produces unreasonably large prems for option 2 contracts
   • COI Rates – Good for projection-based methods (UL)
   COI =
     (q/12) / (1 − q/12)   if arithmetic
     (1 − p^{1/12}) / p^{1/12}   if exponential
   It is important to use the correct rate (COI Rate > mortality rate)

[506] Q: Write basic commutation functions for option 1 and option 2 versions of A_{x} and ä_{x}.
A: These are equivalent to basic actuarial principles:
   A_{x} = M_{x}/D_{x}          ä_{x} = N_{x}/D_{x}
   A_{x}^{DBO2} = M_{x}^{DBO2}/D_{x}^{DBO2} + v^{100-x}     ä_{x}^{DBO2} = N_{x}^{DBO2}/D_{x}^{DBO2}

[507] Q: Describe projection-based approach used under 7702 and 7702A.
A: Equivalent to basic actuarial principles approach, but more practical for UL
   Basic idea: Solve for net level prem (NLP) by iteratively projecting cash flows
   • For GLP, NLP = prem that matures contract at age 100
   • For 7-Pay, NLP = prem that pays contract up at beginning of year 7
   CV_{t+1} = (CV_{t} + NLP − Exp_{t} − NAR_{t} × COI Rate_{t}) × (1 + i_{t}^{(m)})

[508] Q: What is immediate payment of claims adjustment?
A: To reflect that claims will be paid immediately:
   AIPC_{x} = (i/δ) · A_{x}
   where δ = ln(1 + i) and i/δ ≈ (1 + i)/2

E.3.6 — CIT Ch. 10: Taxation of Canadian Life Insurance Policies  (E.3 Policyholder Taxation)
----------------------------------------------------------------------

[509] Q: Describe primary classifications of life insurance for tax purposes in Canada.
A: Post-Dec 1, 1982 Policies
   1. Exempt policies ñ insurance-oriented (most favorable)
     ◦ Inside CSV build-up is only taxed on disposition
     ◦ DBs are 100% tax-free
     ◦ Must pass exempt test (different rules for pre-2017 vs. post-2016)
     ◦ Burden is on insurer to maintain exempt status
   2. Non-exempt policies ñ savings-oriented (least favorable)
     ◦ CSV build-up is taxed annually (“accrual taxation”)
     ◦ Untaxed CSV build-up is taxed at death
     ◦ Includes deferred annuities
   Grandfathered Pre-Dec 2, 1982 Policies (most favorable tax status of all)
   • No exempt/non-exempt distinction and higher cost basis
   • If lose grandfathered status ñ deemed disposition ñ perform exempt testing

[510] Q: Describe Canadian exempt policy rules.
A: "Existing" rules for pre-2017 issues, and "new" rules for post-2016 issue
   • If policy fails exempt test, it is non-exempt for life
   • A coverage remains exempt if this is true for all anniversaries (t's):
   AF_{t} ≤ AFETP_{t}
   • AF = MTAR = maximum tax actuarial reserve
     ◦ Post-2016: MTAR = max(CSV Gross of SC, NPR)
     ◦ Pre-2017: MTAR = max(CSV Net of SC, 1.5 PTR)
   • ETP = Exempt test policy
     ◦ Post-2016 ETP: 8-pay endowment at 90
     ◦ Mortality = generally same as actual policy
     ◦ Interest rate = 3.5%
     ◦ Pre-2017 ETP: 20-pay endowment at 85
     ◦ Mortality = generally same as actual policy
     ◦ Interest rate = max(contract rate, 4%)

[511] Q: How is exempt test policy accumulating fund calculated?
A: The AF grades linearly through the pay period, then equals PVFB
   Post-2016 AFETP_{t} =
     DB_{t} × A_{[x]+8:90-[x]-8} × (t/8)   if t ≤ 8 and [x]+t < 90
     DB_{t} × A_{[x]+t:90-[x]-t}   if t > 8 and [x]+t < 90
     DB_{t}   if [x]+t ≥ 90
   Pre-2017 AFETP_{t} =
     DB_{t} × A_{[x]+20:85-[x]-20} × (t/20)   if t ≤ 20 and [x]+t < 85
     DB_{t} × A_{[x]+t:85-[x]-t}   if t > 20 and [x]+t < 85
     DB_{t}   if [x]+t ≥ 85

[512] Q: List anti-avoidance rules under Canadian insurance taxation.
A: These rules put additional limits on cash accumulation to prevent abuse
   1. Always use total DB for ETP face amount
   2. 8% rule: If DB ↑ by > 8% between anniversaries:
     ◦ “Issue” new ETP for excessincrease
     ◦ Test excess DB’s CSV against new ETP AF
     ◦ Post-2016: applies at coverage level
     ◦ Pre-2017: applies to total policy DB
   3. 250% rule: Fund growth capped at 250% every 3 years starting in year 10
   \frac{Policy AF_{t}}{Policy AF_{t-3}} ≤ 250% for t ≥ 10
   Only applies if policy AF > 3/20 of total ETP AFs
     ◦ If triggered, set all ETP issue dates = max(actual issue date, t − 3)
     ◦ Will not apply again for 6 years once triggered

[513] Q: How is adjusted cost basis determined for a typical exempt life insurance policy in
Canada?
A: Generally speaking, for an exempt policy. . .
   ACB = ΣPrems + ΣTaxable POD − ΣNCPI − ΣTotal POD
   • Prems = all prems paid except portion funded by a disposition
     ◦ Also include amounts paid to acquire (e.g., life settlement)
   • Taxable POD = portion of all dispositions included in income
     ◦ ACB falls by Total POD − Taxable POD
   • NCPI = net cost of pure insurance
   NCPI_{t} = q_{x+t} × (DB_{t} − AF*_{t})
   q_{x+t} = prescribed mortality rate
   AF*_{t} = Policy AF ignoring policy loans = gross CSV (usually)

[514] Q: List 4+ types of dispositions for Canadian tax purposes.
A: All of the following are considered dispositions:
   1. Full and partial surrenders
   2. Policy loans
   3. Policy dividends
   4. Maturity benefits
   5. Absolute assignment of ownership (sale, transfer, or gift to another party)
   6. If a previously exempt policy fails the exempt test
   7. If a non-exempt policy dies

[515] Q: How is taxable income and ACB impact determined by pre-death dispositions in
Canada?
A: Taxable Income =
     POD − Prorated ACB   for post-Dec 1982 partial surrenders
     max(POD − Full ACB, 0)   for all other dispositions
   Post-Dec 1982 partial surrenders ONLY:
   Prorated ACB =
     [Partial Surrender / (CSV − Policy Loan)] × Current ACB   if post-2016
     [Partial Surrender / Policy AF] × Current ACB   if pre-2017
   Remember: pre-2017 policy AF = max(CSV Net of SC, 1.5 PTR), so it’s not always the CSV!
   In ALL Situations:
   ACB After = ACB Before − POD + Taxable Income

[516] Q: How are policy loans taxed in Canada?
A: Policy loan POD = Loan Amount − Portion Used to Pay Prem
   • Cannot exceed CSV − Prior Policy Loan Balance
   Taxable income and ACB impact = same as any other non-partial surrender
   Future ACB impacts:
   • ADD non-deductible paid policy loan interest to ACB
     ◦ Don’t add capitalized interest
   • ADD loan repayments to ACB after gain is repaid
     ◦ Repaid taxable income from policy loans is deductible (so don’t add it)
     ◦ Net effect of repayments is to restore the original ACB reduction

[517] Q: How does accrual taxation work for non-exempt life insurance policies in Canada?
A: Post-Dec 1982 non-exempt policies are subject to accrual taxation
   Post-1989 policies: every anniversary (and on death):
   Taxable Income_{t} = Policy AF_{t} − ACB Before_{t}
   ACB After_{t} = ACB Before_{t} − POD + Taxable Income = Policy AF_{t}

[518] Q: List ways a policy can lose grandfathered status.
A: If any of these occur, grandfathered status is lost forever
   • Prescribed increases in BOTH prems and DBs
     ◦ “Prescribed” means “unscheduled” in contract
     ◦ Exceptions for changes in u/w class, payment frequency, certain riders
   • Prescribed prem paid on a non-exempt policy
   • Non-permitted changes in ownership
   The following will never result in loss of grandfathered status:
   • Adding a term or other rider
   • Non-arm’s length transfer (e.g., assign to a family member)
   • Roll overs into an annuity contract

E.4.1 — Ch. 3: The Primary Life Insurance Distribution Channels  (E.4 Distribution Channels and Marketing)

[519] Q: Describe financial advisor distribution channel.
A: Typical market: wealthy clients, more sophisticated products
   • Individuals who arrived from outside of the life insurance industry
   • May be found in inv advisory companies, banks, or credit unions
   • Stockbrokers (largest group)
   • Does NOT include fee-based planners
   • Reached through wholesalers
   • Most advanced uses of life insurance
   • More face-to-face interaction than other channels

[520] Q: Describe independent agent distribution channel.
A: Typical market: middle to high income clients
   • Includes brokers and PPGAs
   • Producer groups common
     ◦ Benefits for agents: higher comp, networking, education
   • Wholesalers – how companies reach independent agents
     ◦ Spread-sheeting model vs. assisted sales model
     ◦ Can be organized by product line or one for all product lines
     ◦ External vs. internal wholesalers
   • Companies compete over agents with commissions and services provided

[521] Q: Describe the career agent distribution channel.
A: Typical market: middle to high income clients
   • Recruited and hired by a single company, but often sell for other companies too
   • Field sales offices vs. agencies
     ◦ Agency Managers: salaried employees; company pays expenses
     ◦ General Agencies: independent contractors; paid commissions/overrides; GA pays
   expenses; harder to replace
   • Other staff: specialists and sales managers
   • Recent trends: Numbers have been declining
     ◦ Very expensive form of distribution
     ◦ Agent retention has been low
     ◦ Independent agents becoming more popular
     ◦ Moving from smaller to “mega-agencies”
     ◦ Traditional distinctions between agency managers and GAs blurring

[522] Q: Describe MLEA distribution channel.
A: Typical market: mostly middle income life clients
   • Captive – Only sell only for one company
   • Known for extremely high retention rates (80%, which is 4x career agents)
   • Sell both P&C and life insurance
   • P&C sales are very different from life insurance
     ◦ P&C ownership is mandatory
     ◦ P&C sales process is “one and done”
     ◦ Wealthy MLEA clients more likely to be P&C
     ◦ Life insurance requires more agent involvement during u/w process
   • Life insurance clients: few wealthy clients; mostly middle income

[523] Q: Describe IMO distribution channel.
A: Typical market: low to middle income
   • Independent agency-like organizations
   • IMOs recruit, train, and support agents
   • Differences with career agent system:
     ◦ IMO system is built for high productivity
     ◦ No business brokered outside of IMO
   • Highly structured sales presentations
   • Usually focus on specific set of products sold to a specific market

[524] Q: Describe call center distribution channel.
A: Typical market: low to middle income
   • Prospects directed to toll-free number
   • Effective with affinity groups and association marketing (e.g., college alumni)
   • Gaining popularity

[525] Q: Describe home service agent distribution channel.
A: Typical markets: lower income, inner cities, rural south
   • Personal, face-to-face interaction between prospect and sales intermediary
   • Captive – only sell for one company
   • Traditional form: industrial or debit life insurance
     ◦ Agents collected prem door to door weekly
     ◦ Commissions paid on growth, not individual sales
     ◦ Became obsolete (labor force changes, selling practices, agent safety)
   • Monthly debit ordinary has replaced weekly industrial prem collection

[526] Q: Describe worksite distribution channel.
A: Typical market: low to middle income, smaller face policies
   • Employers allow policy prems to be paid via payroll deduction
   • Intended to supplement employees’ existing coverage
   • Consumers like because
     ◦ Ease of purchase and convenience
     ◦ Perception that employer vetted quality first
   • Insurer must be an expert in administrative and payroll systems

[527] Q: Describe forms of direct distribution channels.
A: Typical market: low to middle income
   1. Internet direct
     ◦ Company-specific or market-specific websites
     ◦ Aggregators (most popular) – multiple companies on one site
     ◦ Mostly term life with larger face amounts than direct mail
     ◦ Companies compete on price
     ◦ Simplified issue u/w
   2. Pure direct
     ◦ Mailed solicitations
     ◦ More effective when combined with human intervention (e.g., sales rep in call center)
     ◦ Overly saturated (not very effective)
     ◦ Only 2–5% realized prem

     ◦ New product chassis mitigate interest rate risk (VUL)
   • Continued inv in inforce mgmt, including prem rate ↑

[550] Q: Describe general trends in past 20 years when interest rates have risen and how
current period may affect insurers
A: Past periods of rising interest rates have 2 things in common:
   1. The 10-year rate only ↑1% to 1.5% over a 2-year period
   2. Rates fell sharply after each period
   Assuming the trends are repeated with the current rise in interest rates:
   1. Portfolio rates won’t change significantly, so it won’t improve insurers’ profits
   2. The ↑in rates may be short-term

[551] Q: Provide examples of product designs that perform best when interest rates rise
A: Focus on flexible product designs
   • WL is the most stable when interest rates change dramatically
     ◦ Simple, compatible product design
     ◦ Use of portfolio pricing instead of new money
   • Interest-sensitive products are hurt when interest rates are low
     ◦ ULSG becomes expensive for p/h
     ◦ FAs/MYGAs become unattractive compared to FIA, IUL, VAs
     ◦ FIA/IUL perform well when rates rise if equity markets are also rising

[552] Q: Provide 4+ examples of products that perform well and mitigate interest rate risk along
with advantages to insurers and p/h
A: • 1-year rate guarantee instead of multi-year
     ◦ Pro to insurer: can make timely adjustments based on market
     ◦ Pro to p/h: potentially higher guarantees that adapt to markets
   • Multi-year rate guarantees with small stair-step rate ↑
     ◦ Pro to insurer: less risk since steps are small (e.g., 15 bps)
     ◦ Pro to p/h: mitigates risk of low/flat guarantee
   • Participation in equity markets/indexes
     ◦ Pro to insurer: higher illustrated returns with lower guarantees
     ◦ Pro to p/h: earn returns comparable to other financial products
   • Monthly cap instead of annual cap
     ◦ Pro to insurer: ↑product popularity with low risk of large loss
     ◦ Pro to p/h: possibility of much higher annual return
   • Cumulative guarantees over a defined period (e.g., 2%/year for 5 years)
     ◦ Pro to insurer: cheaper than annual guarantee
     ◦ Pro to p/h: same guaranteed ↑over a longer term
   • Participation by paying dividends during favorable market conditions
     ◦ Pro to insurer: weaker guarantee, so less risk in unfavorable markets
     ◦ Pro to p/h: can participate in insurer’s performance
   • Stacking rollup for FIA GLWBs
     ◦ Pro to insurer: ↓strain and adds flexibility
     ◦ Pro to p/h: potential upside if market performs well

[553] Q: Describe how a temporary rise in interest rates might affect insurer’s portfolio returns
A: If the rise in rates is short-term, portfolio rates will continue to fall
   • Be prudent in providing higher credited rates and/or longer guarantees
   • Reduce nonguaranteed benefits and costs (e.g., sales rep comp)
   • Closely monitor features (e.g., ROP riders) where low interest rates with level
   prems will erode profits

[554] Q: Provide an example of a tail risk scenario involving rising interest rates
A: • Early 1980s: UL writers shortened asset portfolios when ST yields > LT yields
   • When ST yields fell, UL writers moved to risky assets to maintain returns
     ◦ Worked well until junk bond market collapsed in the early 1990s
     ◦ 3 large insurers went insolvent with mass UL surrenders

F.1.1 — Ch. 10: Corporate Bonds  (F.1 Invested Assets)
----------------------------------------------------------------------

[555] Q: Corporate bond issuer-bondholder legal structure?
A: Indenture – contract between issuer and bondholders
   Corporate trustee – bank/trust co. acting as fiduciary for investors
   • Paid by issuer
   • Enforces B/H interests in indenture
   • Can declare default if issuer breaches indenture

[556] Q: Corporate bond fundamentals?
A: • Issuers: utilities, transportation, industrial, banks, etc.
   • Maturity: typically < 30 years
   • Most are registered or book entry → automatic coupons
   • Straight coupon bonds: fixed coupons paid semiannually
     ◦ Participating bonds share profits above certain level
     ◦ Income bonds (rare) pay interest only if issuer income sufficient
   • Coupons may also be zero, deferred, or paid-in-kind (PIK)

[557] Q: Compare Zero vs. Deferred vs. PIK coupons.
A: • Zero-coupon bonds
     ◦ A type of OID bond
     ◦ Less popular today (tax benefits declined)
     ◦ No reinvestment risk if held to maturity
   • Deferred-interest bond (DIB) – blend of straight coupon + zero coupon
     ◦ Most are junk bonds
     ◦ Most pay no cash interest for first 5 years
   • Pay-in-kind (PIK) debentures
     ◦ Pays additional securities instead of coupons
     ◦ Additional securities can be sold separately
   Note: All have higher credit risk than straight coupon bonds

[558] Q: Describe 5 bond security types.
A: • Debentures – no specific security (most common)
     ◦ B/H = general creditors; security = issuer financial strength
     ◦ Common restrictions: min net worth, selling major assets, stock dividends
     ◦ Other B/H protections: negative pledge clause
     ◦ Subordinated issues may offer conversion rights
   • Mortgage Bonds – B/H gets 1st-mortgage lien on property
     ◦ Blanket mortgage → allows additional series on same mortgage
     ◦ Possible B/H protection: after-acquired clause
   • Collateral Trust Bonds – backed by stock of issuer’s subsidiaries
     ◦ B/H protection: issuer default → forfeits voting rights on subsidiary stock
   • Equipment Trust Certificates – issued by railroads
     ◦ Trustee owns RR equipment, leases to RR, RR buys at lease end
     ◦ Very secure: RR depends on equipment, always buys back
   • Guaranteed Bonds – guaranteed by one or more other companies

[559] Q: List 5 early debt retirement methods.
A: 1. Call and refunding provisions
   2. Sinking fund provisions
   3. Maintenance and replacement funds (doesn’t retire bonds)
   4. Redemption through sale of assets (usually restricted)
   5. Tender offers – issuer buys back bonds at PV using CMT + fixed spread
   First 4 must be in indenture if applicable

[560] Q: Describe corporate bond call provisions.
A: • Riskiest for B/H: fixed price call
     ◦ Call price usually starts at premium, then declines
     ◦ May have initial lockout period or non-refundable period
   • Less risky, more common: make-whole call
     ◦ Call price = max(par, PV remaining CFs at CMT + spread)
     ◦ Call price moves inversely with interest rates
     ◦ Issuer advantage: can issue at lower yield

[561] Q: Describe corporate bond sinking fund provisions.
A: Sinking fund provisions popularity has declined
   • Indenture requires issuer to retire portion of bonds each year (usually at par)
   • ↓ default risk
   • B/H benefit if rates ↑ (get par even if MV < par)
   • Issuer may accelerate sinking fund if rates ↓

[562] Q: Issuer default rate formula?
A: Issuer default rate – based on number of defaulting issuers
   No. Issuers that Default in Year
   Total Issuers at BOY

[563] Q: Dollar default rate formula?
A: Dollar default rate – based on amount of defaulted par
   Over multiple years:
   Cumulative $ Value of Defaulted Bonds
   Cumulative $ Value of All Issues × Wtd Avg Years Outstanding
   Annual rate:
   Cumulative $ Value of Defaulted Bonds
   Cumulative $ Value of All Issues

[564] Q: Default loss rate and recovery rate formulas?
A: Default Loss Rate = Default Rate − Recovery Rate
   Recovery rate = % of defaulted bond B/H recover after default
   • Difficult to measure: consists of cash and securities

[565] Q: Event risk vs. headline risk?
A: Event risk – stockholder-driven event → lower bond prices
   • Rating downgrades, ↑ leverage from M&A, etc.
   Headline risk – bad media coverage → lower bond prices

[566] Q: Describe high-yield bond unique features.
A: A.k.a. “junk bonds” – many only just below IG (< BBB or Baa)
   • Deferred Coupon Structures (3 kinds)
   1. Deferred-interest bonds (most common) – no coupons 3–7 years
   2. Step-up bonds – low initial coupon, then ↑
   3. PIK bonds
   • Extendible Reset Bonds
     ◦ Coupon resets periodically to maintain specified price
     ◦ New rate = new interest rate level + credit spread
     ◦ ≠ floating rate (which uses fixed spread)
     ◦ In practice, issuer may not afford the coupon
   • Clawback – redeem bonds during non-callable period w/ IPO proceeds
     ◦ Can hurt B/H since investment would be rising

F.1.2 — Ch. 21: An Overview of Mortgages and the Mortgage Market  (F.1 Invested Assets)
----------------------------------------------------------------------

[567] Q: Mortgage payment characteristics?
A: • Can be amortizing (principal and interest), IO, and/or balloon
   • Rate can be fixed, adjustable (ARMs), or hybrid ARMs
     ◦ ARMs require payment to be recast when the index-based rate changes

[568] Q: Key mortgage industry participants?
A: • Direct lenders – UW and provide loans directly to borrowers
   • Brokers – work with clients and wholesale lenders
   • TPOs – when a loan is completely/partially originated/processed by a party other
   than seller
   • Depository institutions – fund loans with bank deposits
   • Non-depository lenders – fund loans by selling them to investors
   • Originators – UW and fund loans
   • Servicers – collect monthly payments, handle property tax payments, deal with
   delinquencies
   • MBS providers – public or private companies that buy and securitize mortgages
   for investors

[569] Q: Describe aspects of the mortgage underwriting process.
A: UW evaluates borrower’s credit and value of property
   Criteria used to evaluate credit:
   1. Credit (“FICO”) scores: 730+ is strong
   2. If LTV ratio > 80%, mortgage insurance required
   3. Debt-to-income ratio criteria (lower is better) (e.g. front ratio and back ratio)
   Front Ratio = \frac{Total Monthly Payments on the Home}{Borrower’s Pretax Monthly Income}
   Back Ratio = \frac{Front Ratio + Other Debt Payments Like Auto Loans}{Borrower’s Pretax Monthly Income}
   Documentation/standards ↓until 2008 crisis, then ↑

[570] Q: How are mortgage lending rates generated?
A: • MBS providers heavily influence mortgage rates
   • WAC = Weighted avg coupon in MBS pool
   • Originators set rates > MBS coupon to profit
   • MBS investor coupon ≈ 25–75 bps < WAC due to:
   1. Servicing fee
   2. Guaranty fees
   3. Excess servicing fee
   • Discount points – % of loan upfront fee to borrowers
   • Subordination – non-agency credit enhancement; junior bonds absorb losses
   first

[571] Q: How do rates affect prepayment/duration?
A: • Falling interest rates: prepayments↑, durations↓
   • Rising interest rates: prepayments ↓, durations↑(“extension”)

[572] Q: List 5 factors impacting mortgage prepayment risk.
A: 1. Sale of property
   2. Destruction of property (e.g. fire)
   3. Borrower default
   4. Partial principal prepayments
   5. Refinancing
   Prepayments can be rate-sensitive or rate-insensitive

[573] Q: Define SMM, CPR, and the PSA model.
A: 1. SMM (Single Monthly Mortality)
   SMM = (Scheduled Bal − Actual Bal) / Scheduled Bal
   2. CPR (Conditional Prepayment Rate) = annualized SMM
   CPR = 1 − (1 − SMM)^{12}
   3. PSA Model – CPRs not constant over loan life
     ◦ CPR ↑ by 0.2% per month until 6.0% in month 30
     ◦ Can express in multiples

[574] Q: List 5 mortgage credit risk factors.
A: 1. Credit scores
   2. LTVs
   3. Number of days delinquent
   4. Default (90+ days delinq.)
     ◦ Some agencies insure (Ginnie Mae)
     ◦ Non-agency recoveries depend on foreclosure process
   5. Default loss severity ↑when:
     ◦ High LTV
     ◦ Appraisal values > market values
     ◦ Property values ↓after origination
     ◦ Foreclosure process is expensive

F.1.3 — Ch. 7: Interest Rate Forwards and Futures  (F.1 Invested Assets)
----------------------------------------------------------------------

[575] Q: What is an FRA?
A: FRAs allow a borrower (lender) to lock in a borrowing (lending) rate for a future period
   Timeline: t = 0 (Enter FRA) → t (Time of Borrowing, r_{t} = ?) → t + s (FRA Maturity)
   The Borrowing Period spans from t to t + s

[576] Q: FRA payoff formula?
A: A borrower enters an FRA to hedge against rising borrowing costs
   The borrower’s payoff is positive if the actual borrowing rate > r_{FRA}
   Borrower’s Payoff =
     (r_{t} − r_{FRA}) × Notional   if paid in arrears
     [(r_{t} − r_{FRA}) / (1 + r_{t})] × Notional   if paid at time of borrowing
   The lender’s payoff is always the negative of the borrower’s
   If paid at time of borrowing, the payment is "tailed" by the current rate r_{t}

[577] Q: How to create synthetic FRA?
A: Let r_{t,t+s} be the implied forward rate between time t and t + s
   1 + r_{t,t+s} = P_{t}/P_{t+s}
   where P_{t} and P_{t+s} are the current prices of zeros maturing at time t and t + s
   To lock in r_{t,t+s} as a borrowing rate:
   1. Short 1 + r_{t,t+s} zero-coupon bonds maturing at time t + s
   2. Buy 1 zero-coupon bond maturing at time t
   Reverse the long/short position on each bond to lock in a lending rate

[578] Q: Eurodollar futures for hedging?
A: To lock in a borrowing (lending) rate, short (buy) Eurodollar futures
   The payoff on a single short $1M Eurodollar contract with futures price F_{t} is:
   Payoff = [F_{t} − (100 − LIBOR_{t})] × 100 × 25
   = [(100 − r_{forward}) − (100 − LIBOR_{t})] × 100 × 25
   = [LIBOR_{t} − r_{forward}] × 100 × 25
   The payoff is positive if LIBOR is higher than the locked-in forward rate
   The lender’s payoff is always the negative of the borrower’s

[579] Q: Eurodollar futures vs. FRAs?
A: Both can be used to lock in borrowing or lending rates
   Key difference: Eurodollar futures systematically favor borrowers
   • The Eurodollar futures payoff is always at time of borrowing
   • FRAs can payoff at time of borrowing or arrears
   • If paid at time of borrowing, FRAs are automatically tailed at current rate
   • Eurodollar futures can only be tailed at the forward rate ("convexity bias")
   Payoff = [F_{t} − (100 − LIBOR_{t})] × 100 × 25 / (1 + r_{forward})

[580] Q: LIBOR vs. 3-Month T -Bills?
A: LIBOR > Treasury rates because of:
   1. Default premium in LIBOR rates
   2. Higher liquidity premium in LIBOR rates
   3. Market fluctuations that cause spreads to widen
   4. State tax exemption of Treasuries
   LIBOR is far more common for futures contracts
   • Tracks private lending rates better than T -bills

[581] Q: Duration formulas?
A: ΔB per 1% change in y = B × [−ModDur(Δy) + (1/2)(Convexity)(Δy)²]
   B = \sum_{t=0}^{n} v^{t} CF_{t}
   MacDur = \frac{\sum_{t=0}^{n} tv^{t} CF_{t}}{B}
   ModDur = MacDur × v
   Alternative to modified duration: PVBP or "DV01" = ModDur/100

[582] Q: Convexity formula?
A: ΔB per 1% change in y = B × [−ModDur(Δy) + (1/2)(Convexity)(Δy)²]
   B = \sum_{t=0}^{n} v^{t} CF_{t}
   Convexity = \frac{\sum_{t=0}^{n} t(t+1)v^{t+2} CF_{t}}{B}

[583] Q: Describe how a Treasury note futures contract works.
A: A Treasury note futures contract will specify:
   • Underlying bond’s YTM and maturity (e.g. 6% 10-year T-note)
   • Acceptable maturity range of delivered note: e.g. 6.5–10 years
   • Conversion factor: price of delivered note at underlying’s YTM (e.g. 6%)

[584] Q: Invoice price vs. no-arbitrage price?
A: Invoice Price = Futures Price at Maturity × Conversion Factor + Accrued Interest
   The no-arbitrage futures price is
   Futures Price at Maturity = \frac{Price of CTD Bond}{Conversion Factor of CTD Bond}

[585] Q: How to find CTD bond?
A: 1. Identify all available bonds falling within the maturity range (6.5–10 years)
   2. Calculate the price of each one at the underlying’s YTM (6%)
     ◦ Conversion factor = PV bond CFs at 6% (expressed per dollar)
     ◦ E.g. if the price at 6% is 105, the conversion factor is 1.05
   3. The CTD bond is the bond that results in:
   Invoice Price − Futures Price at Maturity × Conversion Factor = 0
   There should only be one bond that satisfies the above; all others will be negative

[586] Q: Describe repos.
A: • Repo – a sale of a security with a commitment to repurchase it at a specified
   price at a designated future date
     ◦ The borrower is the one selling the security (receives cash for it)
     ◦ The lender holds the security until the borrower repurchases it
   • Haircut – the amount by which the repo collateral exceeds the loan

[587] Q: Clean vs. dirty price?
A: Dirty price = true market price of bond (PV bond CFs at YTM)
   Clean price = Dirty Price − Accrued Interest
   Accrued interest = prorated portion of the coupon since the last coupon date
   Accrued Interest = \frac{Days Since Last Coupon}{Total Days Between Coupon Dates}

[588] Q: Why are insurer duties complex?
A: • Answering to policyholders, shareholders, rating agencies and tax agencies
   • Managing its group’s overall balance sheet and capital adequacy
   • Reporting financials based on accounting requirements (if publicly traded)

[589] Q: Insurer challenges in financial crisis?
A: • Significant realized losses
   • Unrealized losses via Accumulated Other Comprehensive Income (AOCI)
     ◦ Many assets are classified as AFS→ must reflect MV on balance sheet
     ◦ AOCI = MV − Amortized Cost
     ◦ Flows through equity on the balance sheet
     ◦ AOCI losses↑sharply between June 2008 and March 2009, then back to normal by
   September 2009
   • Severe drops in market capitalization vs. the broader market
   • Falling RBC ratios→ need to raise capital

[590] Q: Insurer actions post-crisis?
A: Increased liquidity, lowered risk
   • Almost doubled allocations to cash and short-term securities
   • Significantly reduced exposure to securitized assets
   Raised capital
   • Issued common and preferred equity
   • Issued unsecured debt, surplus notes, and subordinated debt
   • De-risked through changing asset allocations
   • Attempted to divest businesses

[591] Q: List 3 ways to create integrated risk management strategy.
A: 1. Clearly define a market risk budget
   2. Evaluate economic objectives vs. insurance constraints
   3. Determine how and where ALM fits into the overall risk management framework

[592] Q: List 6 core ERM processes.
A: 1. Risk governance
   2. Risk and capital measurement
   3. Risk budgeting
   4. Liquidity risk management
   5. ALM and SAA
   6. Risk reporting

[593] Q: List GSAM’s 6 ALM/SAA steps.
A: • Step 1 – Investment Objectives and Constraints
   • Step 2 – Asset Universe and Assumptions
   • Step 3 – Liability Cash Flow and Replicating Portfolio
   • Step 4 – Risk Measures
   • Step 5 – Risk Return Trade-Offs
   • Step 6 – ALM and SAA

[594] Q: GSAM Step 1: Investment Objectives/Constraints?
A: Gives insight into a portfolio’s risk and return attributes under different scenarios
   • Most important step
   Examples of constraints:
   • Achieve 5.5% target yield
   • Asset-liability duration mismatch tolerance: ±0.2
   • Define exposure limits to specific asset/credit classes

[595] Q: GSAM Step 2: Asset Universe/Assumptions?
A: • Establish a broad set of asset classes to achieve diversification and portfolio
   efficiency
   • Correlations are key
     ◦ Correlations between assets
     ◦ Correlations between assets and liabilities

[596] Q: GSAM Step 3: Liability CF/Replicating Portfolio?
A: 3 main steps:
   • Project liability cash flows to understand the liability profile
   • Develop the duration profile of the liabilities
   • Design a risk-minimizing asset portfolio that matches the key economic
   characteristics of the liabilities

[597] Q: GSAM Step 4: Risk Measures?
A: • Helps assess effectiveness of different investment strategies
   • Risk metrics:
     ◦ Asset-only volatility
     ◦ Surplus volatility
     ◦ Economic or required capital, Value at Risk (VaR)
     ◦ Conditional Value at Risk (CVaR) (a.k.a. CTE)
     ◦ Surplus drawdown risk

[598] Q: GSAM Step 5: Risk/Return Trade-Offs?
A: • Apply optimization techniques
   • Evaluate and compare various efficient frontiers

[599] Q: GSAM Step 6: ALM/SAA?
A: • Overall SAA decision reflects an insurer’s risk and return objectives, ALM and
   other constraints
   • SAA is used to develop customized benchmarks
   • To recommend a portfolio, must also consider:
     ◦ Appropriate benchmark(s) to be constructed in line with SAA
     ◦ Relevant tolerance for active management to achieve target alpha
     ◦ How will current market views be incorporated into the portfolio?

F.2.2 — ALM Management of Financial Institutions, Ch. 16  (F.2 ALM)
----------------------------------------------------------------------

[600] Q: Capital market vs. actuarial ALM view?
A: • Capital Market View: Insurance product = bundle of options granted by the
   insurer to policyholders in exchange for a fee
     ◦ Preferred by the authors
     ◦ Insurance products increasingly offer options that policyholders can exercise
   • Actuarial View: Series of future benefit payments/cash flows tied to contingent
   events (death, etc.)
     ◦ Relies on historical experience and current market for assumptions
     ◦ If actual experience ≠ historical, insurers face substantial risk
     ◦ Assumes economic variables stay within a reasonable range of historical experience
   Optimal Solution = Robust ALM that combines:
   • Actuarial approach (risk measurement, capital allocation)
   • Capital markets view to manage embedded options

[601] Q: ALM experience loss examples?
A: • Guaranteed Investment Contracts (GICs):
     ◦ Falling interest rates make it difficult to fund guaranteed payments
   • Credit risk on high-yield and real estate
     ◦ Insurers were short credit options that were exercised (defaults)
   • VA GMxBs
     ◦ Unhedged insurers took losses when equity markets and rates ↓
   Common Feature: Poor ALM practices susceptible to paradigm market changes
   • Insurers wrote options based on an incorrect view of the world
   • Products were mispriced, and portfolios overly risky

[602] Q: LTCI ALM lessons learned?
A: • LTCI products may collect premiums decades before claims begin
   • Pricing relies heavily on assumed reinvestment returns
     ◦ Premiums must accumulate sufficient reserves
   • Insurers in the 1990s assumed high reinvestment rates (7%)
   • Unhedged insurers took large losses when rates Ó in the early 2000s
   • Erroneously assumed rates would mean revert higher
   Lesson: Active management of options/rights protects against drops in future
   investment yield, increasing certainty of meeting obligations

[603] Q: Describe short options written by insurers.
A: • Risk Products: Term life, disability, LTCI
     ◦ Feature: Option to renew a policy at guaranteed premiums
     ◦ Embedded Short Option: Call on the value of future benefit payments
   • Investment Products: Fixed and variable annuities
     ◦ Features: Additional deposits, withdrawals, guaranteed benefits/interest
     ◦ Embedded Short Options:
     ◦ Call on the value of future annuity payments
     ◦ Put on the value of the policy
     ◦ Interest rate floor
   • Hybrid Products: WL and VUL
     ◦ Features: cash surrender, borrowing at pre-determined rates
     ◦ Embedded Short Options:
     ◦ Put on the value of the policy
     ◦ Series of puts on fixed-rate bonds

[604] Q: How can fixed annuity rate risk be hedged?
A: Insurer objectives:
   • Crediting rate ≥ one-year LIBOR
   • Earn a 150 bps spread (Yield − Crediting Rate)
   Assume: yield on 10-year investment = 6%, and LIBOR = 4.0%
   • Objectives are met as long as LIBOR ≤ 4.5%
   • If LIBOR rises above 4.5%, the insurer cannot earn a 150 bps spread
   Solution: enter a swap: pay fixed (4.5%), receive floating (LIBOR)
   • Locks in a spread of 6.0% − 4.0% − 4.5% + 4.0% = 150 bps
   • The floating LIBOR payment will always offset the crediting rate

[605] Q: Describe options granted to insurers.
A: • Risk Products: Term life, disability, LTCI
     ◦ Features: Right to receive either future premiums or reserve
     ◦ Right/option: Callable bond
   • Investment Products: Fixed and variable annuities
     ◦ Product features:
     ◦ Right to convert the policy from a fixed to floating rate
     ◦ Right to receive future M&E fees and/or SCs (MV ↑ with underlying assets)
     ◦ Right/option: swaption, bond with equity call option
   • Hybrid Products: Permanent, whole, and variable life
     ◦ Product features:
     ◦ Right to change the dividend or NGE scale
     ◦ Right to receive future premiums or excess of reserve over CSV
     ◦ Right/option: swaption, callable bond
